## CHAPTER 6: A Critical Analysis of Previous Empirical Studies (Page 152)

**Opening context:**

The classical and Marxian traditions share the distinction between production and nonproduction labor. But Marx was particularly concerned with that portion of production labor which is **productive of capital**, since only this labor creates surplus value. The rest of labor is unproductive of capital, even though it may be wage labor (in distribution and state activities) or production labor (productive of value or of use value).

Marx himself does not imply that productive labor is in any way superior to, or more necessary than, unproductive labor. But as we pointed out in Chapter 2, not all Marxists proceed in the same way.

**Baran's redefinition:**

Most notably, **Baran (1957, p. 32)** redefines productive labor as labor that would be necessary under a "rationally ordered" (socialist) society. Marx's definition of productive labor is thereby replaced with a definition based on **necessity**,¹ and the concept of surplus value is replaced with the concept of "**surplus**" - defined as the excess of the total product over essential personal and public consumption.

**Footnote 1** (page 152):
> "It is no coincidence that Baran turns to a criterion of necessity. Neoclassical economics is also based on a criterion of necessity. In their case, the benchmark is a 'perfectly competitive market,' which perfectly reflects social preferences. Baran retains the necessity criterion, but inverts the benchmark by making a 'rationally ordered [socialist] society' the point of reference."

## Classification Scheme (Page 153)

**Three basic categories of studies**:

1. **No distinction** between productive and unproductive labor:
   - Implicitly or explicitly reject the distinction
   - End up treating Marxian categories as equivalent to NIPA categories

2. **Make the distinction** between productive and unproductive labor:
   - Base themselves on Marx's distinction
   - Differ in how they implement this distinction

3. **Substitute necessity for production**:
   - Replace production/nonproduction with necessary/unnecessary
   - Substitute "surplus" (Baran & Sweezy) for "capitalist surplus product" (Marx)

**Within each category**:
- **Sectoral vs. aggregate studies**: Systematic transfers of value make sectoral studies less reliable²
- **Money vs. labor value estimates**: Whether labor values are calculated

**Footnote 2** (page 153):
> "If we abstract from transfers of value between nations, or between capitalist and noncapitalist sectors, then the total surplus value realized in the production sector as a whole will always be less than that produced in it, because of the portion transferred to the trade sector. Price-value deviations may cause further intersectoral and international transfers, which can in principle reverse the outflow from production. See Sections 3.1.2 and 3.4.2."

## Page 153 (continued): Money vs. Labor Value Measures

**Two theoretically distinct issues**:

1. **Productive vs. unproductive labor**: Money form of surplus value differs from aggregate profit even if prices proportional to values

2. **Price-value deviations**: Money measure differs from labor value measure even if all labor productive³

**Footnote 3** (page 153):
> "Strictly speaking, the comparison is between the money form of surplus value at prices that are not proportional to values (such as market prices and prices of production), and the money form of surplus value at prices proportional to values (which we call 'direct prices')."

**The second issue is far better known**, but empirical studies typically encompass both.

**Example - Wolff's studies**:

**Early studies** (Wolff 1975, 1977a):
- Estimate both labor value magnitudes and money forms
- Assume all wage labor is productive
- Tantamount to assuming money forms of Marxian categories = IO-NIPA categories
- Because mapping is one-to-one when all labor treated as productive (Section 3.1.1)

**Later studies** (Wolff 1977b, 1979, 1987; Khanjian 1989):
- Distinguish between Marxian and IO-NIPA categories
- Incorporate distinction between productive and unproductive labor
- Estimate value categories and money forms separately

## Subsection 6.1.1: Aggregate money value estimates (Page 155-157)

### Glyn and Sutcliffe (1972)

**One of the earliest and most influential** of this type

**Their argument** (p. 54):
- Secular postwar decline in British rate of profit lies at heart of stagnation of capital accumulation
- Since capital/output ratio roughly stable, decline in profit rate attributed to secular fall in **profit share**⁴

**Footnote 4** (pages 155-156):
> "The rate of profit r = P/K, where P = aggregate profit and K = aggregate capital advanced. The rate of profit can also be expressed as r = (P/Y)/(K/Y), where Y = aggregate value added, P/Y = the profit share, and K/Y = the capital output ratio."

**Critical error**: "A fall in the profit share is 'an increase in labour's share [which is] very roughly the equivalent of a decrease in the rate of exploitation'" (p. 45)

**Implication**: Real wages must have risen faster than labor productivity over extended periods

**Conclusion**: Secular decline in British rate of profit ultimately attributed to fact that "the position of labour as a whole has been improving relative to that of capital" (p. 45)

**Notice**: At heart of whole argument is important association of rate of exploitation with NIPA-type measure of profit/wage ratio

## Page 156-157: Followers of Glyn & Sutcliffe

**We confine discussion to those who explicitly connect analysis to Marxian categories**:

### Boddy and Crotty (1975)

- Transform Glyn and Sutcliffe's secular argument into **cyclical** one
- Apply to United States
- Base themselves on chapters of Marx's *Capital* (vol. I) where all labor still treated as production labor
- Treat profit share as index of rate of exploitation
- Cyclical fluctuations in rate of profit explained by fluctuations in profit share, hence in rate of exploitation (p. 2)⁵

**Footnote 5** (page 157):
> "It should be noted that although the Marxian measure of productivity differs markedly from the conventional one in terms of its trend, the (short-run) cyclical patterns of the two are quite similar (Section 5.7, Figures 5.19 and 5.20). Moreover, cyclical fluctuations in productivity are quite modest compared with those in real wages, so that cyclical patterns in the wage share of (all) workers may well mirror corresponding fluctuations in the rate of exploitation. Thus Boddy and Crotty's cyclical results may not be substantially altered when their measure of productivity is replaced by a conventional one. But any attempt to link the short-run cycles to longer-term trends would certainly be affected."

### Sherman (1986)

- Makes same association: "in terms of national income accounting, the rate of exploitation is roughly profit/wages" (p. 198)
- Takes more agnostic approach to determinants of cyclical fluctuations in rate of profit
- Allows for both "Marxist supply-side" (labor-squeeze) and demand-side (consumption-demand) effects of cyclical variations in rate of exploitation (p. 192)

### Weisskopf (1979)

**Concerns himself with both cyclical and secular behavior** of rate of profit in United States since World War II

**Explicit identifications** (pp. 342-346):
- "Variable capital with the total wage bill [and] surplus value with the volume of profits"⁶
- Net output with NIPA measure of net output (value added)
- Implicitly assumes all labor is productive: defines productivity as real net output per unit of (all) labor⁷

**Footnote 6** (page 157):
> "Weisskopf (1979, p. 343) does acknowledge in a footnote that surplus value 'is usually defined to correspond to a substantially larger share of national income' than does the NIPA measure of profits, but he ignores this in his data and analysis."

**Footnote 7** (page 157):
> "Weisskopf (1979, p. 354) distinguishes between supervisory and nonsupervisory labor, but only because he believes the two types of employment exhibit different cyclical patterns."

**Not surprisingly**, Weisskopf finds that the "long term decline in the [U.S.] rate of profit from 1949 to 1975 was almost entirely attributable to a rise in the true share of wages, which indicates a rise in the strength of labour" (p. 370)

### Bowles, Gordon, and Weisskopf (1984)

**Further development** of Weisskopf's argument:
- By late 1960s, rising strength of U.S. workers also took form of reduced work effort
- Served to slow down rate of growth of productivity (pp. 29-32, 122-49)
- Further squeeze on profits

## Page 158: Wolff's Puerto Rico Study

**Puerto Rico study** (Wolff 1975):

Explicitly identifies profit/wage ratio as equivalent of money form of rate of surplus value

**Striking result**: Problem of price-value deviations has so great an impact that money form of rate of surplus value (profit/wage ratio in his calculations) is a "poor proxy" for either level or trend of true (labor value) rate of surplus value (Wolff 1975, p. 940)

## Page 158-159: Explanation of Discrepancy

**Two separate issues involved**:

### 1. Large discrepancy between money and value measures

**Wolff points out**: Discrepancy depends primarily on ratio of direct price (price proportional to labor value) of consumption goods to their market price⁸

**Footnote 8** (pages 158-159):
> "Wolff normalizes the sum of direct prices (prices proportional to labor values) TV = C + V + S to the sum of market prices TV* = C* + V* + S*. He finds that the market price of constant capital was fairly close to its direct price. Thus V* + S* ≈ V + S, which implies that (1 + S*/V*) = (1 + S/V) · (V/V*), where S/V = S/V since direct price magnitudes are proportional to labor values. Therefore Wolff's money rate of surplus value differs from his value rate by the ratio of the direct price of workers' consumption V to its market price V*. But this latter ratio is simply an index of the price-value deviation for consumption goods (Wolff 1977a, pp. 144-5)."

Wolff notes his empirical estimates of this ratio are mostly less than 1⁹

**Footnote 9** (page 159):
> "Wolff normalizes the sum of prices to equal the sum of values, 'values' being defined here as money prices proportional to labor values. Thus the average value/price ratio is 1."

**And here it seems very likely** that Wolff's procedure leads to biased estimates of this value/price ratio. Estimates of labor values require data on input-output flows and also on labor flows. Ideally, these labor flows should be adjusted for skill differences; only if differences among sectoral wage rates due largely to skill differences may one then plausibly substitute relative wage coefficients for labor coefficients.

**Wolff uses wage coefficients** because he is unable to obtain data on labor coefficients (Wolff 1977a, p. 148, n. 9)

## Page 159 (continued): Puerto Rico Context

**For Puerto Rico**, substitution of wage coefficients for skill-adjusted labor coefficients is highly suspect:

**Probable situation**:
- Poor people spend most income on agricultural products and other staples
- Workers in sectors supplying these goods relatively underpaid (relative to skill level)
- Agricultural workers particularly underpaid: averaging between **40% and 60% of wage of other laborers** (Reynolds and Gregory 1965, p. 65, table 2-8)

**Implication**: In Puerto Rico, substitution of wage coefficients for skill coefficients likely to seriously underestimate labor value of workers' consumption goods, thus giving rise to large discrepancy between profit/wage ratio and estimated rate of surplus value

**A 20%-30% differential** between skill-adjusted wage rates in two types of sectors would easily account for Wolff's results

**Furthermore**: To extent that wages in consumption-goods sector catch up to average wage, gap between estimated rate of surplus value and profit/wage ratio would narrow. In itself, such rise in relative wages would raise estimate of labor value of variable capital and hence reduce growth rate of value rate of surplus value, all other things being equal. Thus bias introduced by wage coefficients would also explain apparent fall in Wolff's estimates of value rate of surplus value in Table 6.1.

## Page 159-160: Contrast with US Results

**Preceding factors appear to explain** why:
- Wolff's estimated rates of surplus value so much higher than his profit/wage ratios
- Why this gap appears to narrow over time
- Why estimated value rate of surplus value falls over time

**His estimate of value rate**:
- Biased upward relative to level of profit/wage ratio
- But biased downward relative to its trend (to extent gap narrows)

## Page 160: Later US Studies - Different Results

**Wolff's subsequent studies on United States**: Results quite different

**By using labor coefficients directly** (Wolff 1979, p. 332, n. 9) without any adjustment for skill levels, he implicitly assumes workers in each sector in his input-output tables all have roughly same mix of skills. This is opposite extreme from his Puerto Rico study.

**Results**:
- Value and money rates of surplus value differ by only **8%-15%**
- Both display same general trend (p. 334, table 1)

**Subsequent article** with different definition of wages yields essentially similar results, although both measures decline (Wolff 1986, p. 94, table 2, cited in Khanjian 1989, p. 119)

**Khanjian (1989, table 19)**: Finds price-value deviations result in variations between value and money rates of surplus value of only **6%-9%**

**Conclusion**: Price-value deviations do not seem to pose a significant problem

**Of course**: All problems associated with assumption that all labor is productive still remain

## Page 160-161: The Second Problem - Productive/Unproductive Labor

Even when prices proportional to values, so correct measure of money rate of surplus value equals corresponding value rate, the profit/wage ratio can be downward-biased index of both level and trend of this true money rate of surplus value

**Same reasoning applies** to Wolff's value rate (which is his labor equivalent of profit/wage ratio) vis-à-vis true value rate (computed using only productive labor)

**Thus**:
- Level of Wolff's value rate of surplus value lower than level of true value rate (owing to failure to distinguish between productive and unproductive labor)
- His profit/wage ratio doubly lower (since its level downward biased relative to his own measure of value rate)

**Impact on trend biases not so clear**: Two factors seem to operate in opposite directions in this particular study

**In any case**: Wolff's results must be interpreted in light of all these considerations

## Page 160: Extension by Sharpe

**Wolff's methodology extended by Sharpe (1982a)**, and applied to Canada (Sharpe 1982b, pp. 12, 42)

**Following Mandel's definitions** of basic variables of Marxian economic analysis, Sharpe develops detailed estimates of these variables in both price and value terms

**In various estimates of value rate of surplus value**:
- Generally assumes all capitalist wage labor (even in trade and royalties sectors) is productive
- In one set of measures, even assumes government administrative labor is productive

**Consequently**: Sharpe's main estimates of rate of surplus value much lower than, and probably grow more slowly than, our equivalent measures¹⁰

**Footnote 10** (page 161):
> "Sharpe also calculates two alternative measures of the rate of surplus value. As part of his input-output estimate (Sharpe 1982b), he calculates a 'narrowly defined' measure of the rate of surplus value which is based on the assumption that surplus value is the same thing as property-type income. This gives an estimate of 29% in 1976, as compared to 109.1% for his main measure and to 188% for Cuneo's estimate of the rate of surplus value in Canadian manufacturing. In an unpublished paper, Sharpe (1982a, pp. 41-5) calculates a NIPA-based constant-dollar estimate which is similar in form to the main estimates of his input-output study. This has a similar trend to his labor value estimates, but an even lower level."

## SECTION 6.2: Studies that do distinguish between productive and unproductive labor (Page 161)

**We turn now to those studies** that attempt to incorporate distinction between productive and unproductive labor in their estimates of Marxian categories

### Subsection 6.2.1: Sectoral money value estimates (Page 161)

**Varga (1928, 1935)** provides earliest set of sectoral estimates of rate of surplus value. The methodology he developed remains influential to this day.

**Basing himself on manufacturing census data**:
- Measures variable capital as **wages of production workers**
- (Money form of) surplus value as **excess of net value added over variable capital**¹¹
- Seven benchmark years from 1899 to 1931

**Footnote 11** (page 161):
> "Varga actually calculates surplus value as the difference between the money value of the total product and the sum of materials, depreciation, and wage costs of production. But the difference between the first item and the sum of the next two is simply value added, so his measure of surplus value is net value added minus production wages."

## Significance for NSW Project

**Chapter 6 establishes critical framework** for evaluating empirical estimates of Marxian categories:

1. **Productive/unproductive labor distinction is fundamental**
   - Cannot equate wage share with variable capital share
   - Cannot equate profit/wage ratio with rate of surplus value
   - Failure to make distinction produces systematically biased estimates

2. **Price-value deviations are secondary**
   - When proper methodology used, deviations small (6-9%)
   - Much less important than productive/unproductive distinction
   - But skill adjustments critical in developing economies

3. **"Labor squeeze" thesis fundamentally flawed**
   - Rising wage share does NOT imply falling rate of exploitation
   - Can have rising S*/V* while wage share rises if Lu/Lp rising
   - US postwar experience confirms this pattern

4. **Sectoral vs. aggregate estimates**
   - Value transfers make sectoral estimates less reliable
   - Aggregate estimates preferable
   - Must account for trade sector absorption of production-sector surplus

5. **Implications for our Phase 1 and Phase 2 work**
   - Our methodology correctly distinguishes productive/unproductive labor
   - Uses aggregate approach
   - Can rely on money measures as good value proxies (price-value deviations small)
   - Avoids errors of Glyn & Sutcliffe, Weisskopf, and others
