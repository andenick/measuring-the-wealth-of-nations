## Table 1: Comparison of Summary Statistics of U.S. NSW/GDP Data, 1959-1997

**Location**: Page 5
**Table Type**: Methodological validation comparison
**Time Period**: 1959-1997 (overlap period between Shaikh-Tonak and Moos datasets)

### Purpose

This table serves a critical methodological function: **validating Moos's replication of the Shaikh-Tonak (2000) NSW methodology** by comparing summary statistics for the 38-year overlap period.

### Table Structure

**Columns**:
- Authors (Shaikh and Tonak vs. Moos)
- Min (Minimum NSW/GDP ratio)
- Median (Median NSW/GDP ratio)
- Mean (Mean NSW/GDP ratio)
- Max (Maximum NSW/GDP ratio)

**Rows**:
1. Shaikh and Tonak (2000) original data
2. Moos (2017) replicated data

### Data Content

**Shaikh and Tonak (1959-1997)**:
- Minimum: -0.021 (-2.1% of GDP)
- Median: -0.002 (-0.2% of GDP)
- Mean: -0.000 (0.0% of GDP, essentially zero)
- Maximum: 0.029 (2.9% of GDP)

**Moos (1959-1997)**:
- Minimum: -0.012 (-1.2% of GDP)
- Median: 0.010 (1.0% of GDP)
- Mean: 0.011 (1.1% of GDP)
- Maximum: 0.037 (3.7% of GDP)

### Key Findings

**Differences Between Series**:
- Mean difference: ~1.1 percentage points of GDP
- Maximum difference: 0.8 percentage points
- Pattern consistency: Both series show same general pattern (see Figure 1)

**Source of Differences**:
As Moos notes (page 4):
> "This may be due, at least in part, because the NIPA tables were revised several times since Shaikh and Tonak's data was constructed, including a comprehensive revision in 1999."

**Validation Outcome**:
Moos concludes the differences are "slight (approximately 1 percent of GDP)" and attributes variation to NIPA revisions. The series is considered "a faithful reconstruction of Shaikh and Tonak's series."

**Critical Implication**:
The most significant difference is in the **1980s-1990s period**:
- Shaikh-Tonak: Shows slightly negative NSW in some years
- Moos: Shows slightly positive NSW in same years

This matters because it affects interpretation of the neoliberal era's early phase.

### Data Sources

**Shaikh and Tonak (2000)**:
- Based on NIPA tables as of late 1990s
- Original methodology from Shaikh & Tonak (1987)
- Published in "The Rise and the Fall of the U.S. Welfare State"

**Moos (2017)**:
- Current NIPA tables (as of 2017)
- Same methodology, updated data
- Incorporates comprehensive 1999 NIPA revision

### Methodological Notes

**Why Comparison Matters**:
1. **Replication check**: Confirms Moos correctly applied Shaikh-Tonak methodology
2. **Data consistency**: Establishes baseline for 21st century extension
3. **NIPA revision impact**: Documents effect of statistical revisions on historical estimates

**Limitations**:
- Cannot fully separate NIPA revisions from potential methodological differences
- Minor classification changes in NIPA over time may affect comparability
- No access to Shaikh-Tonak's original worksheets for exact comparison

### Cross-References

- **Figure 1** (page 5): Visual comparison of Moos vs Shaikh-Tonak NSW/GDP series
- **Equation 1** (page 3): NSW = TotalLaborBenefits - TotalLaborTaxes
- **Table 2**: Extended statistics including post-1997 period
- **Section 2** (pages 3-4): Full methodology description

### Significance for Paper's Argument

This table is **foundational** for the paper's credibility. By demonstrating close replication of Shaikh-Tonak's results for 1959-1997, Moos establishes:

1. **Methodological validity**: Her calculations are correct
2. **Baseline credibility**: The ~0% mean NSW for 1959-1997 is robust
3. **Extension legitimacy**: Post-2001 deviation is real, not methodological artifact

Without this validation, the paper's main finding (positive NSW in 21st century) would be suspect.

## Table 2: Summary Statistics of U.S. NSW Data, 1959-2012

**Location**: Page 5
**Table Type**: Comprehensive descriptive statistics
**Time Period**: 1959-2012 (full 54-year Moos dataset)

### Purpose

This table presents **complete summary statistics for all NSW measures** across the entire study period, documenting the full range of variation in the extended dataset that includes the 21st century.

### Table Structure

**Columns**:
- Variable (NSW measure type)
- Min (Minimum value)
- Median (Median value)
- Mean (Mean value)
- Max (Maximum value)

**Rows**:
1. NSW (nominal dollars, billions)
2. Real NSW (2010 constant dollars, billions)
3. NSW/GDP (ratio, decimal form)
4. NSW/EC (NSW/Employee Compensation ratio, decimal form)

### Data Content

**NSW (Nominal, billions of dollars)**:
- Minimum: -13.18 billion
- Median: 54.87 billion
- Mean: 212.60 billion
- Maximum: 1288.00 billion (in 2010)

**Real NSW (2010 constant dollars, billions)**:
- Minimum: -76.48 billion
- Median: 119.40 billion
- Mean: 255.10 billion
- Maximum: 1288.00 billion (in 2010)

**NSW/GDP (ratio)**:
- Minimum: -0.012 (-1.2% of GDP)
- Median: 0.013 (1.3% of GDP)
- Mean: 0.020 (2.0% of GDP)
- Maximum: 0.086 (8.6% of GDP, in 2010)

**NSW/EC (ratio)**:
- Minimum: -0.022 (-2.2% of Employee Compensation)
- Median: 0.024 (2.4% of EC)
- Mean: 0.037 (3.7% of EC)
- Maximum: 0.161 (16.1% of EC, in 2010)

### Key Findings

**Historic Maximum (2010)**:
All measures peak in 2010:
- Nominal NSW: $1.288 trillion
- Real NSW: $1.288 trillion (2010 is base year)
- NSW/GDP: 8.6%
- NSW/EC: 16.1%

This is **unprecedented** in the 54-year time series.

**Central Tendency**:
- Median NSW/GDP of 1.3% suggests positive NSW is typical
- But mean of 2.0% pulled up by extreme 21st century values
- Contrast with Shaikh-Tonak mean of ~0% for 1952-1997

**Range**:
- NSW/GDP varies by nearly 10 percentage points (from -1.2% to +8.6%)
- NSW/EC shows even greater volatility (from -2.2% to +16.1%)
- This range is much wider than observed in 20th century

### Period Breakdown (from text)

Although not in the table itself, Moos provides critical context:

**1959-2000**:
- Mean NSW ≈ 0% (consistent with Shaikh-Tonak)

**2001-2012**:
- Mean NSW ≈ 5% of GDP
- **This is the paper's central finding**

### Interpretation of Different Measures

**NSW (Nominal billions)**:
- Shows absolute dollar transfer to labor
- Not adjusted for inflation or economy size
- Useful for understanding fiscal budget impact

**Real NSW (2010 dollars)**:
- Inflation-adjusted using GDP deflator
- Shows "true" purchasing power of transfer
- Confirms positive NSW is real, not nominal illusion

**NSW/GDP**:
- Normalizes by economy size
- Main measure used in paper
- Allows comparison across time and countries
- 2.0% mean = workers receive net transfer of 2% of GDP on average

**NSW/EC**:
- Normalizes by employee compensation
- Alternative presentation (used by Shaikh-Tonak 2000)
- More volatile because EC fluctuates with business cycle
- 3.7% mean = workers receive 3.7% of their wages back from state (net)
- At 2010 peak: workers received 16.1% of EC as net transfer

### Data Sources

**Primary Source**: NIPA (National Income and Product Accounts) tables from BEA (Bureau of Economic Analysis)

**Specific NIPA Tables Used** (from methodology):
- Employee compensation: NIPA Table 2.1
- Personal income: NIPA Table 2.1
- Government expenditures: NIPA Tables 3.1, 3.2, 3.3
- Government revenues: NIPA Tables 3.1, 3.2, 3.3
- GDP deflator: NIPA Table 1.1.4

**Conversion to Real Terms**:
- Base year: 2010
- Deflator: GDP deflator (chained 2010 dollars)
- All nominal values converted using this deflator

### Methodological Notes

**Why Four Measures?**:
1. **Nominal NSW**: Shows fiscal impact (budget effect)
2. **Real NSW**: Shows purchasing power (inflation-adjusted)
3. **NSW/GDP**: Shows relative size (economy-normalized)
4. **NSW/EC**: Shows wage supplement effect (worker-normalized)

**Units Matter**:
- Decimal ratios (not percentages) in table
- NSW/GDP of 0.086 = 8.6%
- NSW/EC of 0.161 = 16.1%

**Outliers**:
- 2010 is clear outlier (maximum for all measures)
- 2009 also very high (not shown in table but in figures)
- Great Recession period (2007-2012) dominates positive extremes

### Limitations

**Summary Statistics Can Mislead**:
- Mean of 2.0% masks dramatic shift post-2001
- Period-specific means (pre/post 2001) tell better story
- Median vs mean difference suggests skewed distribution

**EC vs GDP Denominator**:
- NSW/EC more volatile than NSW/GDP
- EC is subset of GDP (labor share ~62-73% over period)
- EC fluctuates more with business cycle than GDP

**Real vs Nominal**:
- Real NSW conversion assumes GDP deflator appropriate
- May not capture differential inflation in benefits (healthcare) vs general prices

### Cross-References

- **Table 1**: Comparison with Shaikh-Tonak for overlap period
- **Figure 1** (page 5): Time series visualization of NSW/GDP
- **Figure 2** (page 7): Time series visualization of NSW/EC
- **Figure 10** (page 17): Real NSW with recession shading
- **Equation 10** (equations file): Real NSW conversion formula
- **Section 3** (page 4): Discussion of extended data series

### Significance for Paper's Argument

This table provides **quantitative foundation** for the paper's main claims:

1. **Positive NSW in aggregate**: Mean of 2.0% confirms net transfer to workers over full period

2. **21st century deviation**: Maximum of 8.6% in 2010 signals unprecedented shift

3. **Not trivial**: Mean of 2.0% of GDP is ~$300 billion/year transfer (in 2012 dollars)

4. **Worker perspective**: 16.1% of wages (peak) is substantial supplement to market income

5. **Validation of "real" effect**: Real NSW statistics confirm not just inflation

The **historic high in 2010** during Great Recession is the paper's empirical centerpiece - all subsequent analysis aims to explain this phenomenon.

## Table 3: Unemployment Rates and Intensity: Comparison of 1983 and 2010

**Location**: Page 20
**Table Type**: Analytical comparison (snapshot years)
**Time Period**: Two years compared: 1983 and 2010

### Purpose

This table demonstrates a **critical puzzle**: Why did the same unemployment rate (9.6%) produce vastly different NSW outcomes in different recessions?

The answer: **Unemployment intensity** (Shaikh 2013) captures duration effects missed by standard unemployment rate.

### Table Structure

**Columns**:
- Variable (unemployment measure)
- 1983 (early 1980s recession)
- 2010 (Great Recession)

**Rows**:
1. Official Unemployment Rate (U-3, percent)
2. Unemployment Intensity Measure (Shaikh 2013, percent)
3. NSW/GDP (ratio, percent)

### Data Content

**1983 (Early 1980s Recession)**:
- Official Unemployment Rate: 9.6%
- Unemployment Intensity Measure: 18.95%
- NSW/GDP: 2.1%

**2010 (Great Recession)**:
- Official Unemployment Rate: 9.6%
- Unemployment Intensity Measure: 31.46%
- NSW/GDP: 8.6%

### Key Findings

**Unemployment Rate Identical**:
- Both years: 9.6% (U-3)
- Peak of respective recessions
- Standard measure suggests equivalent labor market distress

**Unemployment Intensity Much Higher in 2010**:
- 1983: 18.95%
- 2010: 31.46%
- **66% higher** despite same unemployment rate

**NSW Dramatically Higher in 2010**:
- 1983: 2.1% of GDP
- 2010: 8.6% of GDP
- **4x higher** (310% increase)

**Correlation Pattern**:
The ~66% increase in unemployment intensity corresponds to ~310% increase in NSW, suggesting:
- Longer unemployment spells → longer benefit collection periods
- More severe economic hardship → higher benefit utilization
- Intensity matters more than rate alone

### Unemployment Intensity Methodology (Shaikh 2013)

**Definition** (from Equation 8 in equations file):
```
Unemployment Intensity = Unemployment Rate × Duration Index
```

**Components**:
- **Unemployment Rate**: Official U-3 (% of labor force unemployed)
- **Duration Index**: Index of average weeks unemployed

**Interpretation**:
- Captures **how many** unemployed (rate) × **how long** unemployed (duration)
- Long-term unemployment has greater economic/social impact
- More accurately reflects severity of labor market distress

**Why It Matters**:
Standard unemployment rate treats:
- Person unemployed 1 week = Person unemployed 52 weeks
- Intensity measure distinguishes these cases

### Contextual Information (from text)

**1983 Recession Context**:
- Early 1980s "Volcker recession"
- Part of neoliberal restructuring
- Unemployment peaked but duration relatively short
- NSW rose modestly (2.1% vs ~0% long-term average)

**2010 Great Recession Context**:
- Worst crisis since Great Depression
- Started December 2007, worsened September 2008 (financial crisis)
- "Jobless recovery" - output recovered but employment didn't
- Long-term unemployment (>27 weeks) unprecedented
- NSW peaked at historic high (8.6%)

**Why 2010 Duration So Long**:
- Financial crisis destroyed jobs permanently
- Structural unemployment (mismatch of skills/geography)
- Slow recovery meant fewer job openings
- Exhaustion of unemployment insurance led to discouragement
- Many workers dropped out of labor force entirely

### Data Sources

**Official Unemployment Rate**:
- Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS)
- Current Population Survey (CPS)
- Monthly household survey
- U-3 measure (standard rate)

**Unemployment Duration**:
- Also from BLS/CPS
- Average (mean) duration of unemployment in weeks
- Published monthly

**Unemployment Intensity**:
- Calculated by Moos using Shaikh (2013) methodology
- Combines BLS unemployment rate with duration data
- Indexed to show intensity as percentage

**NSW/GDP**:
- Moos's own calculations from NIPA data
- As described in methodology section

### Methodological Notes

**Why Compare 1983 and 2010?**:
- Only two years in dataset with 9.6% unemployment
- Controls for unemployment rate, isolates duration effect
- Demonstrates intensity > rate for explaining NSW

**Alternative Unemployment Measures**:
Paper mentions (page 17-18) that alternative measures (U-4, U-5, U-6) also show labor market worse in 2010:
- U-6 (broad unemployment + underemployment) much higher in 2010
- Discouraged workers (~1 million by 2010)
- Involuntary part-time workers (5% of workforce)

**Limitations**:
- Only two data points (not statistical relationship)
- Other factors also changed 1983→2010 (benefits generosity, demographics, etc.)
- Intensity is explanatory but not sole cause of NSW difference

### Calculation Example (Illustrative)

**1983**:
- Unemployment rate: 9.6%
- Suppose average duration: ~19.7 weeks (approximate)
- Intensity ≈ 9.6% × (19.7/10) = 18.95%

**2010**:
- Unemployment rate: 9.6%
- Suppose average duration: ~32.8 weeks (approximate)
- Intensity ≈ 9.6% × (32.8/10) = 31.46%

(Note: Exact duration index calculation details from Shaikh 2013 not fully specified)

### Cross-References

- **Figure 11** (page 18): NSW/GDP vs Unemployment Rate (shows 1983 and 2010 as same U-rate, different NSW)
- **Figure 12** (page 19): Unemployment Rate vs Unemployment Intensity over full period
- **Equation 8** (equations file): Unemployment Intensity formula
- **Section 4.2.1** (pages 17-20): "Unemployment Effects" full discussion
- **Section 4.2.2** (page 19): "Jobless Recovery and the Net Social Wage"

### Significance for Paper's Argument

This table is **crucial for explaining the 2010 NSW peak**:

**Countercyclical Argument**:
- NSW rises in recessions (automatic stabilizers + unemployment insurance)
- But 1983 recession had same unemployment rate, much lower NSW
- Standard business cycle story incomplete

**Duration Argument**:
- Great Recession featured **long-term unemployment**
- Longer unemployment → longer benefit collection → higher NSW
- Intensity measure captures this effect

**Structural Change Argument**:
- 2010 wasn't just deeper recession, it was different kind
- "Jobless recovery" meant benefits extended longer
- Structural unemployment persisted years after "recovery"

**Policy Implication**:
- Standard unemployment rate misleading for fiscal planning
- Intensity measure better predicts benefit expenditures
- Long-term unemployment more costly to state than short-term

**Broader Theme**:
Supports paper's argument that 21st century NSW reflects:
1. More severe economic instability (financialization)
2. Structural labor market problems (neoliberal economy)
3. State forced to accommodate longer-duration distress

Without this table, one might conclude "same unemployment → same NSW expected." The table shows this is **false**: duration matters enormously.
