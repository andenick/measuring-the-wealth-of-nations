## Figure 3: Components of Social Expenditures/GDP

**Location**: Page 8 (Section 4.1)
**Type**: Six-panel time series graphs
**Panels**: Income Support, Education, Health, Housing, Energy, Transport
**Y-axis**: Each component as % of GDP
**X-axis**: Years 1959-2012

**Panel 1: Income Support/GDP**
- **Largest component** of social expenditures
- Rose from ~2.5% of GDP (1959) to ~12.5% of GDP (2012)
- Steady upward trend throughout period
- Includes: Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, SNAP, EITC, unemployment insurance

**Panel 2: Education/GDP**
- Stable around 5-6% of GDP throughout period
- Slight decline in 1980s-1990s
- Modest recovery post-2000

**Panel 3: Health/GDP**
- Refers to hospitals/clinics, NOT insurance programs
- Relatively stable around 1-1.5% of GDP
- Slight upward trend

**Panel 4: Housing/GDP**
- Declining trend from ~0.6% (1970s) to ~0.3% (2000s)
- Reflects neoliberal withdrawal from public housing

**Panel 5: Energy/GDP**
- Very small component (~0.2-0.4% of GDP)
- Declining trend

**Panel 6: Transport/GDP**
- Stable around 1.2-1.6% of GDP
- Slight decline in neoliberal era

**Significance**:
- Income support is the **dominant driver** of NSW growth
- Other components relatively stable or declining
- Confirms focus on income support for investigating NSW variation

## Figure 4: Major Old-Age Program Spending/GDP

**Location**: Page 9 (Section 4.1.1 - Support for Seniors)
**Type**: Time series line graph
**Y-axis**: Old-age programs as % of GDP
**X-axis**: Years 1959-2012

**Content Description**:
Combined spending on three major old-age programs:
1. Social Security (retirement benefits)
2. Medicare (health insurance for 65+)
3. Pensions and disability insurance

**Key Features**:
- Growth from 2.5% of GDP (1959) to 8.5% of GDP (2012)
- Rapid growth 1959-1980 (from 2.5% to 5.8%)
- Plateau 1980-2005 (around 6-7%)
- Renewed rapid growth 2006-2012 (from 6.5% to 8.5%)

**Demographic Context**:
- Baby Boomers (born 1946-1964) began reaching retirement age (65) in 2011
- But major NSW deviation occurred **before** Boomers retired (2002-2010)
- Suggests demographics alone don't explain positive NSW

**Interpretation**:
- Old-age spending grew steadily but can't fully explain post-2001 NSW spike
- Timing doesn't match: NSW spiked 2002-2010, Boomers retired 2011+
- Must look to other factors

**Type**: Time series line graph with three series
**Y-axis**: Spending in billions of dollars
**X-axis**: Years 1960-2010
**Three series**:

**Content Description**:
Nominal spending levels for three programs.

**Key Observations**:
- **Social Security**: From ~$10B (1960) to ~$800B (2010)
- **Medicare**: From $0 (pre-1966) to ~$500B (2010)
- **Pensions/Disability**: Relatively flat, ~$50-100B

**Medicare Growth**:
- Created 1965, spending began 1966
- Exponential growth due to:
  - Expanding eligible population (aging)
  - Healthcare inflation (fastest growing component)

**Social Security Growth**:
- More linear growth than Medicare
- Tied to wage indexing and demographics
- Less affected by sector-specific inflation

**Significance**:
- Medicare growth outpaces Social Security in percentage terms
- Healthcare inflation is major driver of expenditure growth
- Nominal dollars help visualize absolute fiscal burden

## Figure 6: Major Low-Income Programs/GDP

**Location**: Page 11 (Section 4.1.2 - Support for Low-Income Households)
**Type**: Time series line graph
**Y-axis**: Low-income programs as % of GDP
**X-axis**: Years 1960-2012

**Content Description**:
Combined spending on three major anti-poverty programs:
1. SNAP/Food Stamps
2. Refundable tax credits (EITC, Child Tax Credit)
3. Medicaid (health insurance for low-income)

**Key Features**:
- Negligible before 1965 (~0% of GDP)
- Programs created/expanded 1964-1977 (Great Society era)
- Steady growth from 0% (1960) to ~3.5% (2012)
- **Dramatic growth 1990-1995**: From 1.5% to 2.5% of GDP (Clinton EITC expansion)
- Accelerated growth 2007-2012 (Great Recession)

**Program Creation Timeline**:
- 1964: Food Stamp Act (made permanent)
- 1965: Medicaid created
- 1975: EITC created
- 1986: EITC first major expansion
- 1993: EITC second major expansion (Clinton)
- 1997: Child Tax Credit created

**Significance**:
- Anti-poverty spending grew even during "welfare reform" era
- Tax expenditures (EITC) substituted for cash welfare
- Counter-cyclical: Spikes during recessions

**Y-axis**: Spending in billions of dollars
**X-axis**: Years 1960-2010
**Three series**:
1. SNAP/Food Stamps (solid line)
2. Refundable Tax Credits (dashed line)
3. Medicaid (dotted line)

**Content Description**:
Nominal spending levels for three anti-poverty programs.

**Key Observations**:
- **Medicaid**: Largest, grew from $0 (1965) to ~$400B (2010)
- **Refundable Tax Credits**: From $0 (1975) to ~$100B (2010)
- **SNAP/Food Stamps**: Relatively modest, ~$10-80B

**Medicaid Dominance**:
- Dwarfs other anti-poverty spending
- Growth driven by healthcare inflation + coverage expansions
- CHIP (1997) added children's coverage

**EITC Growth**:
- Minimal 1975-1985 (~$1-2B)
- Moderate 1986-1993 (~$5-15B)
- Rapid growth post-1993: $15B → $100B (2010)
- Became largest federal anti-poverty cash program

**Interpretation**:
- Medicaid growth parallels Medicare (both hit by healthcare inflation)
- EITC growth reflects neoliberal preference for "work incentives" over cash welfare
- SNAP relatively stable except during recessions

## Figure 8: U.S. Labor Benefits and Taxes, 1959-2012

**Location**: Page 12 (Section 4.1.3 - Tax Expenditures and Refundable Tax Credits)
**Type**: Time series line graph with two series
**Y-axis**: Spending and revenue in billions of dollars
**X-axis**: Years 1960-2010
**Two series**:
1. Benefits (solid line)
2. Taxes (dashed line)

**Content Description**:
Direct comparison of total labor benefits and total labor taxes (both in nominal billions).

**Key Features**:
- **1959-1990**: Benefits and taxes track closely, nearly parallel
- **1990-1999**: Small gap emerges (benefits slightly exceed taxes)
- **2000**: Gap closes, nearly equal again
- **2001-2012**: Large persistent gap (benefits far exceed taxes)
- **Peak divergence 2010**: Benefits ~$3,100B, Taxes ~$2,000B (gap = $1,100B)

**Critical Turning Points**:
1. **2001**: Dot-com bubble burst, Bush tax cuts begin
2. **2009**: Great Recession, ARRA stimulus, automatic stabilizers

**Interpretation**:
- This figure visually demonstrates the **positive NSW** in 21st century
- Gap = NSW in nominal terms
- 1990s: Modest positive NSW (Clinton EITC expansion)
- 2000s-2010s: Unprecedented positive NSW (tax cuts + recession + healthcare inflation)

**Significance**:
- Central visual for paper's main finding
- Shows both components of NSW (benefits rising, taxes falling/flat)
- Emphasizes that BOTH factors contribute (not just spending)

## Figure 9: Tax Revenue in Billions

**Location**: Page 14 (Section 4.1.3)
**Type**: Time series line graph with three series
**Y-axis**: Revenue in billions of dollars
**X-axis**: Years 1960-2010
**Three series**:
1. Federal Income Taxes (solid line)
2. State Income Taxes (dashed line)
3. Property Taxes (dotted line)

**Content Description**:
Breakdown of major tax categories showing **federal taxes are most variable**.

**Key Observations**:
- **Federal Income Taxes**: Most volatile
  - Growth 1960-2000: $50B → $1,200B
  - **Drop 2001-2003**: $1,200B → $800B (Bush tax cuts + recession)
  - Recovery 2003-2007: $800B → $1,000B
  - **Drop 2008-2010**: $1,000B → $900B (Great Recession + Obama extensions)
- **State Income Taxes**: Steady moderate growth, $10B → $300B
- **Property Taxes**: Steady slow growth, $20B → $50B

**Federal Tax Volatility**:
- Highly pro-cyclical (falls in recessions)
- Policy-sensitive (cuts in 2001, 2003, 2010, 2012)
- Accounts for most of the tax decline driving positive NSW

**Significance**:
- Federal income tax is the "swing variable" in NSW
- Neoliberal tax policy (Bush/Obama cuts) directly reduced labor taxes
- Recessions amplify effect (automatic decline in tax revenue)

## Figure 10: U.S. Real Net Social Wage (2010 dollars), 1959-2012

**Location**: Page 17 (Section 4.2 - Macroeconomic and Business Cycle Effects)
**Type**: Time series line graph with NBER recession shading
**Y-axis**: Real NSW in billions (2010 constant dollars)
**X-axis**: Years 1960-2010
**Shaded areas**: NBER-dated recessions

**Content Description**:
Inflation-adjusted NSW showing **cyclical pattern** and **secular trend**.

**Key Patterns**:
1. **1959-1970**: Flat around $0 (zero NSW)
2. **1970-1975**: Sharp spike during 1973-1975 recession (oil shock)
3. **1975-1980**: Returns to near-zero
4. **1980-1990**: Negative NSW during Reagan era
5. **1990-1993**: Spike during 1990-1991 recession
6. **1993-2000**: Elevated NSW (Clinton EITC era)
7. **2000-2001**: Brief return toward zero
8. **2001-2012**: **Unprecedented sustained positive NSW**
   - 2001-2003: Initial spike (dot-com bust, tax cuts)
   - 2003-2007: Remains elevated (jobless recovery)
   - 2007-2010: Dramatic spike (Great Recession, reaches $1,300B in 2010)
   - 2010-2012: Slight decline but still historic high

**Recession Correlation**:
- NSW **rises during recessions** (shaded areas)
- NSW **falls during expansions**
- **But**: 21st century NSW stays high even during expansion (2003-2007)

**Key Difference**:
- Past recessions: NSW spiked temporarily, returned to near-zero
- 2000s recessions: NSW spiked and **stayed elevated**
- Suggests structural change, not just automatic stabilizers

**Significance**:
- Demonstrates counter-cyclical character of NSW
- Shows 21st century is qualitatively different from earlier periods
- Real dollars control for inflation, confirming genuine increase

## Figure 11: Unemployment Rate and NSW/GDP, 1959-2012

**Location**: Page 18 (Section 4.2.1 - Unemployment Effects)
**Type**: Dual-axis time series line graph
**Y-axis (left)**: Unemployment rate (%)
**Y-axis (right)**: NSW/GDP ratio
**X-axis**: Years 1960-2010
**Two series**:
1. Unemployment Rate (solid line, left axis)
2. NSW/GDP (dashed line, right axis)

**Content Description**:
Tests Shaikh-Tonak (2000) claim that NSW is "substantially driven by variations in unemployment."

**Key Observations**:
- **Positive correlation**: Generally, when unemployment ↑, NSW ↑
- **But inconsistent magnitude**:
  - 1983: Unemployment = 9.6%, NSW/GDP = 2.1%
  - 2010: Unemployment = 9.6%, NSW/GDP = 8.6%
  - **Same unemployment, 4x higher NSW!**

**Critical Finding**:
- Unemployment **does not fully explain** NSW variation
- 21st century NSW much higher for same unemployment levels
- Other factors must be at play

**Implication**:
- Automatic stabilizers (unemployment insurance) are only part of story
- Structural factors (healthcare inflation, tax policy, demographics) matter more
- Challenges Shaikh-Tonak's emphasis on unemployment as main driver

## Figure 12: Unemployment Rate and Unemployment Intensity Rate, 1959-2012

**Location**: Page 19 (Section 4.2.1)
**Type**: Time series line graph with two series
**Y-axis**: Rate (%)
**X-axis**: Years 1960-2010
**Two series**:
1. Unemployment Rate (official, solid line)
2. Unemployment Intensity (Shaikh 2013 measure, dashed line)

**Content Description**:
Compares official unemployment rate with **Shaikh's (2013) unemployment intensity measure**:

```
Unemployment Intensity = Unemployment Rate × Duration Index
```

**Key Findings**:
- Unemployment intensity is **always higher** than official rate
- **Gap widens during recessions** (longer unemployment spells)
- **1983**: Unemployment = 9.6%, Intensity = 18.95%
- **2010**: Unemployment = 9.6%, Intensity = **31.46%**
  - Intensity 66% higher than official rate!

**Great Recession Distinction**:
- 2010 had same unemployment rate as 1983
- But unemployment was **much more intense** (longer duration)
- Average unemployment spell:
  - 1983: ~15 weeks
  - 2010: ~33 weeks (all-time record)

**Connection to NSW**:
- Higher unemployment intensity → longer benefit collection periods
- Unemployment insurance, SNAP, Medicaid benefits extend longer
- Helps explain why 2010 NSW was 4x higher than 1983 despite same unemployment rate

**Significance**:
- Unemployment **intensity** matters more than unemployment **rate** for NSW
- Great Recession was historically unique in duration of joblessness
- Provides partial explanation for unprecedented NSW in 2010

## Figure 13: Traditional Labor Share and Mohun Income Share, 1959-2012

**Location**: Page 22 (Section 4.3 - Rising Inequality and Methodological Issues)
**Type**: Time series line graph with two series
**Y-axis**: Labor/income share estimates
**X-axis**: Years 1960-2010
**Two series**:
1. Traditional Labor Share (solid line)
2. Mohun Income Share (dashed line, from Mohun 2016)

**Content Description**:
Compares two measures of labor's share of income to test robustness of NSW methodology.

**Traditional Labor Share**:
```
LS_trad = Employee Compensation / (Employee Compensation + Operating Surplus)
```
- Includes ALL employee compensation (including high earners)
- Range: 0.73 (1970) to 0.62 (2012)
- Declining trend, but moderate

**Mohun Income Share** (Mohun 2016):
```
LS_Mohun = [Employee Comp - Supervisory Wages] / [EC - Supervisory + Operating Surplus]
```
- Excludes supervisory/managerial wages (treated as quasi-capital income)
- Range: 0.68 (1970) to 0.45 (2012)
- **Much steeper decline**

**Key Differences**:
- Both decline, but Mohun's declines **faster**
- 1970: Small gap (~5 percentage points)
- 1980s: Gap widens significantly
- 2000s: Large gap (~20 percentage points)
- By 2010: Traditional LS = 0.65, Mohun LS = 0.45

**Interpretation**:
- Growing inequality within "labor" (high vs low earners)
- Top earners' income may be more like capital income
- Traditional LS may **overstate** workers' true income share in neoliberal era

**Critical Question**:
Does using alternative labor share change NSW results?

## Figure 14: NSW/GDP with Traditional Labor Share and NSW/GDP with Mohun Income Share, 1959-2012

**Location**: Page 23 (Section 4.3)
**Type**: Time series line graph with two series
**Y-axis**: NSW/GDP ratio
**X-axis**: Years 1960-2010
**Two series**:
1. NSW/GDP using Traditional LS (solid line)
2. NSW/GDP using Mohun Income Share (dashed line)

**Content Description**:
Robustness check: Does alternative labor share measure change NSW findings?

**Key Finding**: **NO - results are robust**

**Observations**:
- Two series track **very closely** throughout entire period
- Differences are **small** (typically <0.5 percentage points of GDP)
- Pattern is **identical**: Both show dramatic post-2001 positive NSW
- Peak in 2010: Both reach ~8-9% of GDP

**Why So Similar Despite Different Labor Share Measures?**
1. E1 and T1 (direct benefits/taxes to labor) are **not multiplied by LS**
   - These are the largest components
2. Only E2 and T2 (mixed benefits/taxes) are multiplied by LS
   - These are smaller components
3. Therefore, LS variation has **limited impact** on overall NSW

**Implication**:
- NSW findings are **robust to labor share specification**
- Growing inequality within labor does **not** undermine NSW methodology
- Main drivers of positive NSW (healthcare, tax cuts, recession) are captured regardless of LS measure

**Significance for Paper**:
- Addresses potential methodological criticism
- Confirms that 21st century positive NSW is **real**, not artifact of measurement
- Validates continued use of traditional LS for NSW calculation
