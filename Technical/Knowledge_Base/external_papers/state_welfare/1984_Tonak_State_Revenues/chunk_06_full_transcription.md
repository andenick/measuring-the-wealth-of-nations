The basic set of activities so far covered -- production, distribution and social reproduction and maintenance -- not only have common features within themselves over various historical stages of human societies but also have something in common among themselves which is the fact that all are labor activities.

The only set of activities involving no labor in the proper sense is personal consumption which is reproductive of members of society, but is neither production nor distribution nor social reproduction of the social order. Consequently, as a first step in defining PL as such, it would be useful to classify these basic human activities as labor and non-labor activities. Figure I represents such a classification.

As a second step in defining PL, we must focus on activities under the heading of labor. The division of human activities into labor and non-labor clearly does not give sufficient ground to define PL which must result in the creation of new wealth.<sup>4</sup> This latter condition is obviously not satisfied by the activities

of distribution and of social reproduction and maintenance; they are non-production activities which require an involvement of human labor. However, this elimination process leads us only to define a set, "productive labor in general" is only a subset. In this sense, the historical specificity of the concept of PL should be emphasized.<sup>5</sup> Consequently, the proper definition of PL must cover a set of activities within capitalist society which leads to the production of surplus value from the "standpoint of capital". This definition can also be interpreted as a criterion for the identification of PL (hence UPL) within capitalist society. Labor which satisfies the following two conditions should be termed as productive labor: a) it must be exchanged with capital and thus satisfy the condition of being within the capitalist relations, b) it must produce surplus-value for the capitalist. Both these conditions clearly indicate the historical specificity of the concept.

In the next section, I shall exemplify the above criteria by referring to Marx's own writings on this issue and on the writings of other 19th century economists, particularly Adam Smith.

Secondly, I will discuss some misconceptions and failures in dealing with that distinction in the context of Marx's own evaluations of other contributors into this theoretical domain. Through that exposition it will be seen that various formulations, whose theoretical origins and failures are specifically underlined by Marx, are actually the original versions of the so-called 'original contributions' which have been recently presented in the debate on PUPL.

Prior to a review of how Marx defined PUPL, it would be useful to touch upon two factors which are usually unrecognized in theoretical works based on Marx, and, in turn, generally become a source of conceptual confusions. The first one is the failure of seeing Marx's work as theoretical product of a continuous process with a higher level of theoretical maturity at each stage of this process. Although manifestations of that failure can be observed in different forms in different contexts, for my present purpose I will only point out the one most widely shared; that is, picking up various definitions of PUPL developed by Marx in the course of a period of more than 25 years and, instead of capturing the evolution of these various definitions,

The other factor is the mistake of perceiving Marx's published and unpublished works, most of which were not written with a view to publication but rather for self-clarification purposes, without any distinction in terms of their relative significance for Marx's theoretical totality. That particular failure at times goes so far as to consider certain views, concepts, etc., written by Marx exclusively for self-clarification as almost absolute truths.<sup>7</sup> One manifestation of this error is insufficient attention paid to the content of some concepts which are particularly relevant to the present discussion, and to the question of whether they were developed in finished works or presented in unfinished and unpublished works so that emphasis can be placed on them accordingly.<sup>8</sup> This failure is the basis of many ongoing conceptual unclarities. There should not be any doubt that Marx himself defined PUPL within the context of circuits of capital and of revenue for historically specific society, namely capitalist society. Regardless of whether it is capitalists, any society must, in order to reproduce itself, carry out numerous production activities which themselves correspond to a certain level of

An interpretation of PUPL distinction within a general and a historical framework, which classifies all laborers engaged in production activities in a pre-capitalist societies as productive laborers, has nothing to do with the definition of PL that Marx developed specifically in relation to the capitalist mode of production in order to explain the functioning of this particular form of society.

Let us look at this issue more closely. When the writings of Marx on PUPL are carefully studied, it is easily recognizable that the circuits of capital and of revenue (and related to these, relations of exchange and capital) play a central role. In the M-C phase of M-C ... P ... C'-M', which is itself the most general representation of the capitalist reproduction of material conditions, labor-power as a part of C is exchanged with a part of M (exchange with capital), then in the course of the production process it reproduces not only necessary values to reproduce itself but also a surplus-value.<sup>9</sup> That characteristic of labor, the quality of producing surplus-value, leads Marx to define those activities as productive activities of labor, and labor as productive labor. That PL, to use Marx's phrase, "objectifies itself directly during the labor process as a fluid quantum of value." (Marx, 1973, p. 1040). A

few pages later in the Resultat, Marx recapitulates his position in the following way:

> "Productive labor is exchanged directly for money as capital, i.e. for money which is intrinsically capital, which is destined to function as capital. In this general framework of PUPL, it is conceivable to interpret other specific definitions of PUPL in Marx. He presents the definition of PL as an element of the general circuit of capital just after the beginning of a section in the Resultat subtitled 'Productive and Unproductive Labor'.

> "Since the immediate purpose and the authentic product of capitalist production is surplus-value, labor is only productive, and an exponent of labor-power is only a productive worker, if it or he creates surplus-value directly, i.e. the only productive labor is that which is directly consumed in the course of production for the valorization of capital." (Marx, 1976, p. 1038)

And in the same context, he also defines UPL in the following manner:

The previous quotations make very clear that if a labor expenditure is paid out of revenue then such labor activity must be considered as UPL.<sup>11</sup> On the other hand, a similar reasoning does not guarantee that when a labor expenditure is paid out of capital it always becomes PL. Being exchanged with capital is only one of two necessary conditions, of which the other is the fact that labor engages in the production of surplus-value.

As demonstrated above, Marx defined productive labor as "value-creating" (Marx, 1976, p. 1038) and "directly surplus-value" - creating (Marx, 1976, p. 1038) labor, and unproductive labor as labor remunerated directly or indirectly out of revenue. And again in this sense, i.e., in relation to the general circuit of capital, he emphasizes that "these definitions are therefore ... derived ... from the definite social form and the social relations of production within which the labor is realized." (Marx, 1963, p. 157). Like Marx's other ideas, all his views concerning PUPL must be understood as a product of a long intellectual process
