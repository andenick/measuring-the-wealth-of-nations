## Table I: Historical Stages of Human Societies and The Nature of Production Activities

**Location:** Page 37
**Type:** Conceptual/Theoretical Framework Table
**Purpose:** Establish how the purpose of production transforms across historical modes of production

---

### Table Structure

**Table I**

| Historical Stages of Human Societies | The Nature of Production Activities |
|--------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| I. Primitive Communism | Production of Use-Values for Personal and Social Use. |
| II. Other Pre-Capitalist Societies | -- Production of Use-Values for Personal Use.<br>-- Production of Values for Exchange. |
| III. Capitalism | -- Production of Values and Surplus-Values for Exchange<br>-- Negligible Production of Use-Values (some farmers, etc.) |

---

### Context and Significance

**Theoretical Foundation** (Page 36):

This table operationalizes Marx's statement (1973, p. 87):
> "all production is appropriation of nature on the part of an individual with and through a definite form of society"

**Key Distinction:**

The table shows that production activities themselves don't change fundamentally across modes - humans always **create** or **transform** use-values. What changes is the **purpose** and **social form** of production.

### Analysis by Historical Stage

#### Stage I: Primitive Communism

**Nature of Production:**
- **Use-values only** - Production for direct use
- **Personal and social use** - No exchange, no market

**Labor Categories:**
- No productive/unproductive distinction (no surplus-value production)
- All labor is "productive" in general sense (creates use-values)

**Significance:**
- Baseline showing production in its simplest form
- Demonstrates production ≠ necessarily capitalist production

#### Stage II: Other Pre-Capitalist Societies

**Nature of Production (Dual character):**
1. **Use-values for personal use** - Subsistence production continues
2. **Values for exchange** - Exchange emerges alongside use-value production

**Examples:** Feudalism, slave societies, petty commodity production

**Labor Categories:**
- Some production for exchange (values created)
- But production **not yet predominantly** for surplus-value
- Mix of use-value and exchange-value production

**Significance:**
- Transitional forms
- Shows exchange can exist without capitalism
- Production for use and production for exchange coexist

#### Stage III: Capitalism

**Nature of Production (Dominant + Residual):**
1. **Values and Surplus-Values for Exchange** - Dominant, defining characteristic
2. **Negligible production of use-values** - Residual (some farmers, etc.)

**Critical transformation:**
- Production **predominantly** for exchange
- But more specifically: for production of **surplus-value**
- Use-value production becomes "negligible" (marginal, exceptional)

**Labor Categories emerge:**
- **Productive labor** = produces surplus-value
- **Unproductive labor** = does not produce surplus-value (even if engaged in production sphere)

**Significance:**
- This is why PUPL distinction is **historically specific** to capitalism
- In earlier modes, no surplus-value → no PUPL distinction applicable
- The table shows PUPL categories are not transhistorical

### Implications for PUPL Classification

**From Table Context (Pages 36-37):**

> "Obviously, throughout there various stages of human history all human activities which involve production are labor activities as such. To be specific, such activities can be termed production labor. However, labor activities itself does not consist of only activities in the sphere of production. There are two more areas in which human beings engage in labor activity: the spheres of distribution and of social reproduction and maintenance."

**Four Spheres of Labor Activity:**

1. **Production** (creation/transformation of use-values)
2. **Distribution** (allocation of produced values)
3. **Personal consumption** (individual use)
4. **Social reproduction and maintenance** (collective reproduction)

**Classification Rule:**

- Being in "production sphere" ≠ automatically productive labor
- Must **produce surplus-value** (not just use-values, not just values)

**State Labor Classification** (Implication):

Most state activities fall in spheres **2-4**:
- **Distribution** (tax collection, transfer payments)
- **Social reproduction** (education, welfare)
- **Maintenance** (military, police, administration)

Therefore, by definition, most state labor is **unproductive** (even if socially necessary).

---

### Methodological Significance

#### 1. Historical Specificity

The table demonstrates that:
- **PUPL distinction applies only to capitalism** (Stage III)
- Cannot apply PUPL retroactively to pre-capitalist modes
- Categories are mode-of-production specific

**Implication for empirical work:**
- US economy 1952-1980 is **fully capitalist**
- PUPL distinction is appropriate analytical tool
- But recognize it wouldn't apply to earlier periods

#### 2. Dominance vs. Coexistence

Even in capitalism:
- Some use-value production persists ("negligible")
- Some petty commodity production remains (small farmers)
- But **dominant** form is surplus-value production

**Implication for classification:**
- Focus on **dominant relations**
- Small-scale use-value production can be treated as exception
- NIPA data reflects predominantly capitalist relations by 1952

#### 3. Purpose of Production Determines Labor Type

**Not the activity itself** but **its purpose** determines classification:

Same activity (e.g., baking bread):
- **Primitive communism:** Productive of use-values (personal/social use)
- **Pre-capitalist:** Might be productive of values (for exchange)
- **Capitalism:** Productive of surplus-value (if hired labor in bakery firm)

**Implication:**
- Can't classify labor by **what they do** (occupation)
- Must classify by **relation to surplus-value production**

### Relationship to State Analysis

**The table sets up the state question** (addressed in remainder of Chapter III):

**State activities are primarily:**
- **Distribution** (tax and transfer systems)
- **Social reproduction** (education, health, welfare)
- **Maintenance** (military, police, courts, administration)

**Very limited production** (some public enterprises, but minority)

**Therefore:**

1. **Most state labor is unproductive** (doesn't produce surplus-value)
2. **State workers' wages come from surplus-value** (deduction, like rent/interest)
3. **State activities affect accumulation** but don't directly produce surplus

**This will be formalized** in circuit of capital framework (later in Chapter III).

### Connection to Empirical Chapters

**Chapter IV will apply this framework:**

1. **Classify all US economic activities 1952-1980** as:
   - Productive sectors (surplus-value producing)
   - Unproductive sectors (distribution, maintenance, state)

2. **Calculate:**
   - $V$ = wages of productive workers only (based on this framework)
   - $S$ = surplus-value (value created minus $V$)
   - $e'$ = S/V (rate of surplus-value)

3. **State impact:**
   - State workers = unproductive
   - State wages = deduction from surplus
   - Affects $e'$ through taxation and transfers

**Without Table I's framework**, this classification would lack theoretical justification.
