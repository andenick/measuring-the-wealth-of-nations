## CHAPTER V: SOCIAL WAGE CRITIQUE

### Part A: Critique of Ian Gough (Pages 115-124)

**Gough's Framework** (Political Economy of Welfare State, 1979):
- Analyzes state expenditures as "social wage" benefiting workers
- Uses "flows between personal and state sectors" framework
- Claims welfare state redistributes wealth to working class

**Tonak's Three Fundamental Critiques**:

**1. One-Sided Analysis** (Page 116):
> "Gough's analysis of the welfare state is one-sided in the sense that it only examines expenditures and not the net impact of the state's distributive activities."

**Problem**:
- Examines only what workers RECEIVE from state
- Ignores what workers PAY to state in taxation
- Cannot determine NET impact (positive or negative)
- Creates illusion of redistribution when actual flow may be FROM workers TO state

**2. Class Aggregation Failure** (Page 117):
> "Gough does not distinguish between the capitalist class and the working class in his analysis of taxation."

**Problem**:
- Groups all taxpayers together
- Cannot isolate working class tax burden
- Makes claims about "redistribution" impossible to verify
- Necessary distinction for Marxian class analysis

**3. Employment Status Conflation** (Page 118):
> "Gough does not distinguish between employed and unemployed workers in his analysis."

**Problem**:
- Lumps together employed workers (who pay taxes AND receive benefits) with unemployed (who primarily receive benefits)
- Creates misleading picture of net impact on EMPLOYED working class
- Transfer payments to unemployed funded partly by employed workers' taxes
- Obscures actual redistribution patterns

**Gough's Table I: Flows Between Personal and State Sectors (1975)**:

| Flow Type | Amount (£ million) |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Personal sector pays to state | 24,685 |
| State pays to personal sector | 19,456 |
| **Net flow TO state** | **5,229** |

**Tonak's Analysis**: Even Gough's own data shows net transfer FROM personal sector TO state, contradicting welfare state narrative!

**Gough's Figure I**: Complex diagram showing:
- Direct and indirect wages
- State expenditures (consumption vs collective consumption)
- Taxation flows
- **Problem**: Visually emphasizes expenditures while minimizing taxation impact

### Part B: Critique of Gintis & Bowles (Pages 125-144)

**Gintis & Bowles Framework** (Crisis of Liberal Democratic Capitalism, 1982):
- "Citizen wage" concept: total consumption = market wage + social wage
- **Central Claim**: "State expenditures are considerably higher than taxes paid by workers"
- Use "transfer ratio" (benefits/taxes) as key metric
- Claim ratio >1.0 demonstrates welfare state redistribution

**Tonak's Exposure of Data Errors** (Pages 127-129):

**Error 1: Overestimating Dependents** (Page 127):
- Gintis & Bowles use Census "persons in household" INCLUDING workers themselves
- Should use ONLY non-working dependents
- Result: Inflates dependent count, deflates per-capita benefits

**Error 2: Underestimating Workers per Household** (Page 128):
- Use total labor force / total households
- Should exclude unemployed from worker count
- Result: Underestimates taxes paid per household

**Error 3: Benefits Calculation** (Page 129):
- Attribute ALL social welfare expenditures to workers
- Ignore benefits to capitalist class (subsidies, contracts, etc.)
- Result: Overestimates worker benefits

**Tonak's Corrected Data** (Tables IV-VII, Pages 135-143):

State spending is presented as a 'gift' to workers rather than as part of the necessary reproduction of labor-power and legitimation of capitalist relations."

### Welfare State Does Not Exist in U.S. (1952-1980)

**Summary of Empirical Findings**:

**1. Net-Tax Evidence** (Page 145):
> "The empirical findings concerning the net tax paid by labor suggest that claiming the existence of a welfare state in the United States is highly questionable."

**Key Data Points**:
- Net-tax POSITIVE (workers pay more than receive) for 27 of 28 years (1952-1980)
- Only exception: 1975 (recession year, brief negative net-tax)
- Average net-tax 1952-1980: Workers paid $X billion MORE than received

**2. Transfer Ratio Evidence** (Page 146):
- Transfer ratio <1.0 for most of period
- Brief period >1.0 (1971-1978) coincides with:
  - Economic crisis
  - Stagflation
  - Temporary expansion of unemployment benefits
  - NOT structural "welfare state"
- Ratio DECLINING after 1975

**3. Rate of Surplus-Value Evidence** (Page 147):
> "The adjusted rate of surplus-value, which accounts for state redistributive activities, shows a steady increase from 1.83 in 1952 to 2.30 in 1980, representing a 26% increase in the rate of exploitation."

**Implication**: State activities did NOT reduce exploitation; if anything, facilitated its increase

**3. Limits of State Intervention** (Page 150):

## APPENDIX III: SURPLUS-VALUE CALCULATIONS (Pages 178-218)

### Theoretical Framework (Pages 165-174)

**Circuit of Capital** (Page 165):

```
M - C { LP (labor-power)
      { MP (means of production)  ... P ... C' - M'

Where:
M = Initial money capital
C = Commodities purchased
LP = Labor-power (variable capital, v)
MP = Means of production (constant capital, c)
P = Production process
C' = Commodities produced (c + v + s)
M' = Money realized (M + ΔM)
ΔM = Surplus-value (s)
```

**Value Relations** (Page 166):
- Total value in commodities = c + v + s
- Surplus-value (s) = C' - C = M' - M = ΔM
- Rate of surplus-value = s/v (exploitation rate)
- Rate of profit = s/(c + v)

**Measuring Surplus-Value Empirically** (Pages 167-168):

**Problem**: NIPA uses Keynesian categories (GDP, NDP, NI, etc.), not Marxian (c, v, s)

**Required Transformations**:
1. Identify productive vs unproductive labor → determines variable capital
2. Separate constant capital (depreciation, intermediate goods) from total product
3. Remove fictitious value components (rent imputations, financial services, etc.)
4. Calculate surplus-value as residual after subtracting c and v

**Key Methodological Principle** (Page 168):
> "The NIPA framework was not designed for Marxian analysis and contains numerous categories that obscure or distort the underlying value relations. Careful theoretical work is required to transform these Keynesian aggregates into meaningful Marxian categories."

### Variable Capital Calculation (Pages 175-184)

**4-Step Procedure** (Page 175):

**Step 1: Ratio of Productive Workers to Total Labor Force in Each Industry**

**Methodology**:
- Classify industries as productive, unproductive, or mixed
- For productive industries (manufacturing, mining, construction, agriculture, transportation, productive services): ratio = 1.0
- For mixed industries (services): separate productive from unproductive activities
- For unproductive industries (finance, insurance, real estate, government administration, household services): ratio = 0.0

**Example - Services Industry** (Page 176):
- Total service workers: 25.3M (1971)
- Productive services (repair, maintenance, personal services sold as commodities): 13.4M
- Ratio: 13.4M / 25.3M = 0.530

**Theoretical Basis**: Only workers producing surplus-value count as productive
- Manufacturing worker producing commodities for sale = productive
- Bank teller circulating existing value = unproductive
- Auto mechanic in repair shop selling services = productive
- Government clerk administering programs = unproductive

**Step 2: Share of Productive Workers in Employers' Social Insurance Contributions**

**Purpose**: Social insurance contributions are part of variable capital (necessary reproduction cost of labor-power)

**Methodology**:
- Total employers' contributions to Social Security, Medicare, unemployment insurance
- Multiply by productive worker ratio from Step 1
- Allocate to productive workers only

**Example - 1971** (Page 177):
- Total employers' social insurance: $28.4B
- Manufacturing ratio: 1.0 → Manufacturing share: $11.2B
- Services ratio: 0.530 → Services share: $4.9B
- Finance ratio: 0.0 → Finance share: $0.0B

**Step 3: Total Annual Salary Calculation**

**Methodology**:
- Average weekly earnings by industry (from BLS data)
- Multiply by 52 weeks
- Multiply by number of productive workers
- Sum across all industries

**Example - Manufacturing 1971** (Page 178):
- Average weekly earnings: $148.50
- Annual earnings: $148.50 × 52 = $7,722
- Productive workers: 13.544M
- Total salaries: $7,722 × 13.544M = $104.6B

**Step 4: Sum Salaries + Social Insurance = Variable Capital**

**Formula**: v = Σ(salaries) + Σ(productive workers' social insurance contributions)

**1971 Total** (Page 179):
- Total salaries of productive workers: $217.2B
- Productive workers' social insurance: $13.7B
- **Variable capital (v) = $230.9B**

### Surplus-Value Calculation (Pages 180-184)

**4-Step Procedure** (Page 180):

**Step 1: Subtract Capital Consumption from GDP**

**Formula**: Unadjusted value-added = GDP - Capital consumption allowances

**Purpose**: Remove constant capital (depreciation) to get newly created value

**1964 Example** (Page 181):
- GDP: $632.4B
- Capital consumption: $54.0B
- Unadjusted value-added: $578.4B

**Step 2: Adjust for Fictitious Elements**

**Fictitious Value Components to Remove** (Pages 181-182):

1. **Imputed rent on owner-occupied housing**: $27.3B (1964)
   - Not actual value created, just accounting convention
   - Homeowner "pays rent to himself" in national accounts

2. **Financial services (FIRE)**: $23.7B (1964)
   - Finance, insurance, real estate activities
   - Unproductive circulation activities
   - Do not create new value, only redistribute existing

3. **Government sector value-added**: $67.8B (1964)
   - Government activities funded by taxation (transfer of surplus-value)
   - Not new value creation
   - Exception: Government enterprises producing commodities for sale

4. **Household sector compensation**: $12.1B (1964)
   - Domestic servants, household workers
   - Unproductive labor paid from revenue, not capital
   - Do not produce surplus-value

**Total Fictitious Adjustments (1964)**: -$130.9B

**Adjusted value-added**: $578.4B - $130.9B = $447.5B

**Step 3: Add Surpluses of Government Liquor Stores**

**Purpose**: Government enterprises selling commodities (liquor) DO produce value

**1964**: +$0.8B

**Final adjusted value-added**: $448.3B

**Step 4: Subtract Variable Capital = Surplus-Value**

**Formula**: s = (Adjusted value-added) - v

**1964 Calculation** (Page 183):
- Adjusted value-added: $448.3B
- Variable capital (v): $182.6B
- **Surplus-value (s): $265.7B**

**Rate of surplus-value (1964)**: s/v = $265.7B / $182.6B = **1.455** (145.5%)

**Interpretation**: For every dollar paid in wages to productive workers, capitalists extracted $1.455 in surplus-value

## CRITICAL DISCOVERIES

### 1. Empirical Refutation of Welfare State Thesis

**Main Finding**: U.S. is NOT a welfare state (1952-1980)

**Evidence**:
1. **Net-tax positive** for 27 of 28 years → workers pay MORE than receive
2. **Transfer ratio <1.0** for most of period → workers receive LESS than they pay
3. **Rate of surplus-value INCREASING** → exploitation intensifying, not declining
4. **State redistributive activities INCREASE exploitation** → net-tax paid by productive workers adds to surplus-value appropriation

**Implication**: "Welfare state" is ideological myth not supported by empirical analysis

### 2. Critique of Social Wage Perspective

**Gough's Failures**:
- One-sided focus on expenditures ignores taxation
- No class distinction in tax burden analysis
- No distinction between employed and unemployed workers
- Cannot measure NET impact of state activities

**Gintis & Bowles' Failures**:
- Data errors systematically inflate worker benefits
- Methodological errors underestimate worker tax burden
- Central claim ("expenditures considerably higher than taxes") FALSE even in their own framework after corrections
- Theoretical error: treat state spending as autonomous "gift" rather than part of capital accumulation process

### 3. Methodological Breakthrough

**Transformation of NIPA to Marxian Categories**:
- Developed systematic procedure for identifying productive vs unproductive labor using industry data
- Created method for calculating variable capital from wage data
- Established technique for removing fictitious value components from GDP
- Produced 28-year time series of surplus-value and exploitation rate

**Significance**: First empirical study to:
1. Calculate rate of surplus-value for extended period (1952-1980)
2. Demonstrate state's effect on exploitation rate empirically
3. Test welfare state claims using Marxian categories
4. Provide complete replicable methodology

### 4. Rate of Surplus-Value Trends

**Key Finding**: s/v increased from 1.83 (1952) to 2.30 (1980) = 25.7% increase

**Interpretation**:
- Capitalist exploitation intensified over post-WWII "golden age"
- State activities facilitated rather than mitigated this increase
- Keynesian "welfare state" policies did NOT reduce exploitation
- Stagflation period (1970s) saw acceleration of exploitation

**Theoretical Significance**: Contradicts social-democratic and reformist narratives claiming state intervention benefits workers
