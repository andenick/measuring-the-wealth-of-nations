## 5. Net Tax

Since the methods of allocating both taxes and government expenditures to labor and non-labor segment were discussed, the concept of net tax can be empirically constructed on that basis. Net tax was defined as "taxes paid minus benefits and income received" andis applicable both to labor and non-labor.

Table X is a presentation of the net tax category for the period of 1952-1980 in the U.S.

Net labor taxes, all through the years 1952-1980, were positive, except for 1975. In other words, employed workers paid more in taxes than they received in benefits or income from the State. A positive net labor tax points to a net income transfer from labor to non-labor. Remember that "non-labor", in this context, consist of not only the capitalist class but also the State itself. Therefore, the net income transfer from labor to non-labor does not imply taht the net-tax paid by the capitalist class has always been negative. What is crucial in this respect is the absorbed portion of the net-tax paid by labor by the activities of the State.

Another way of looking at this empirical evidence concerning the performance or the very existence of a welfare state is to construct a ratio of benefits and income received to taxes paid (by employed labor) which I call the transfer-ratio. The progression of that ratio is presented in the table and the graph on the pages 114 and 115. The same phenomenon as manifested by the movement of net labor taxes now becomes more apparent in the value of the transfer-ratio throughout the period of 1952-1980, - always less than one, except 1975.

## IV.C. The State and the Rate of Surplus-Value

Net labor-tax, as a concept, presents a quantified measure of the role of the State vis-=a-vis labor as a class, at the level of redistribution of value. That is, mainly determined by the surplus-value producing capacity of the society and by the effectiveness of economic and political struggle of the working class as a whole.

On the other hand, to see the indirect impact of the State at the level of production (through its redistributive activities) in the necessary and surplus portions of the working day, there is a need to approximate the portion of net labor-tax paid by productive workers. ### Section IV.C: Impact on Surplus Value (Page 117)

**Key Concept**: Net-tax paid by productive workers affects rate of surplus value.

**Mechanism**:
1. Net-tax comes out of wage bill of productive workers
2. This **reduces** effective variable capital: `v' = v - NetTax`
3. This **increases** surplus value: `s' = s + NetTax`
4. Result: **Higher adjusted rate of surplus value**: `s'/v' > s/v`

**U.S. Finding**:
> "This has been the case in the U.S. since 1952: redistributive activities of the [state have increased the rate of exploitation]"

**Interpretation**: State's fiscal activities systematically **increase** capitalist exploitation of workers by reducing their effective consumption fund while increasing surplus extracted.

## Historical Context

### 1975 Anomaly

**Why was 1975 the only negative net-tax year?**

Economic context:
- Severe recession (1974-1975)
- Unemployment peaked at ~9%
- Massive expansion of unemployment benefits
- Tax revenues declined sharply
- One-time deviation, not structural shift

### Post-1975 Trajectory

**1976-1980 pattern**:
- Brief approach to parity (1976: exactly 1.00 transfer ratio)
- Sharp increase in net-tax by 1979 ($50.06B - highest in entire period)
- Suggests fiscalausterity and welfare retrenchment beginning
- Precursor to Reagan-era cuts

### Great Society Legacy

**1965-1975 period**:
- Medicare/Medicaid introduction (1965)
- War on Poverty programs
- Social Security benefit expansions
- Transfer ratios rose from ~0.65 to ~1.00
- BUT: Even at peak, barely reached parity

**Interpretation**: Largest expansion of U.S. welfare state in history still insufficient to offset tax burden on workers.

## Theoretical Implications

### Contradicts Mainstream View

**Standard welfare state theory** claims:
- Progressive taxation redistributes from rich to poor
- Social programs create net benefits for workers
- State mediates class conflict through redistribution

**Tonak's evidence** shows:
- Workers pay more in taxes than receive in benefits
- Net redistribution goes FROM labor TO non-labor
- State activities systematically favor capital

### Class Character of the State

Table X quantifies **class bias** in fiscal policy:
- $449 billion cumulative transfer from labor (1952-1980)
- Faux frais (military, police, administration) exclude workers
- Corporate subsidies and net interest benefit capital
- Social programs insufficient to offset tax burden

### Marxist Framework Validated

**Circuit of Capital Analysis** (from earlier chapters):
- State interventions occur at distribution level
- Redistributive activities affect rate of surplus value
- Net-tax increases exploitation rate by reducing v and increasing s
- Empirical data confirms Marx's theory of state under capitalism

## Significance

### Historical Importance

**1952-1980 = "Golden Age" of U.S. Capitalism**:
- Highest union density
- Strongest welfare state
- Most progressive taxation
- Greatest economic growth

**Yet even in this period**: Workers paid net-tax in 28 out of 29 years.

**Implication**: If workers didn't receive net benefits during the BEST conditions, when would they?

### Political Economy Critique

**"Welfare state" is misnomer**:
- Primarily a **warfare state** (military spending)
- Plus **administration state** (bureaucracy)
- Plus **debt state** (net interest to wealthy)
- Social programs are **residual**, not primary function

### Policy Implications

**For working class politics**:
- Cannot rely on state redistribution to improve conditions
- Tax system is **regressive in practice** despite progressive rates
- Direct workplace struggle more effective than lobbying for benefits
- Welfare programs are **concessions**, not structural changes
