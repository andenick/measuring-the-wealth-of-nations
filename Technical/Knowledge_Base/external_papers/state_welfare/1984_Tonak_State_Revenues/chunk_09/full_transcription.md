## Chapter IV

## State in Distribution Process

In this Chapter, the fundamental question raised is how the State directly participates in the distribution process vis-a-vis working class and what effect such participation has on the rate of surplus-value.^1 The first part of the question requires an operational concept which measures the net impact of state activities in taxation and expenditures on the working class as whole. The concept proposed is that of the 'net-tax', namely, taxes paid by workers to the state minus benefits and income received from it. The second part of the question, as manifested in the fluctuations of the rate of surplus-value, can only be posed by narrowing down the concept of the net-tax paid by labor. In order to analyze the effect of the State's distributional activities on the rate of surplus-value, it is necessary to determine the portion of net-tax paid exclusively by productive workers, because it is the labor time of these workers which yields variable capital. As long as this latter portion is positive, the magnitude of variable capital will be smaller, while that of surplus-value due to the state's distributive activities, will be larger by the same amount; hence the increased rate of surplus-value. Both of these questions, which are linked but address different

[Page 72]

issues, will be discussed, formulated and empirically checked in the context of the U.S. for the period 1952 - 1980 by using NIPA data.^2

In the second section, the empirical conceptual framework of the study in the context of NIPA will be briefly presented. In the third section, the tax and expenditure structures of the U.S. will be discussed and some remarks made concerning their content. The methods of allocating taxes and benefits and income will subsequently be outlined; and details of the calculations and data sources will be presented in Appendix I and II. The method of calculation of the rate of surplus-value will be presented in Appendix III.

## A. NIPA and Net-Tax

The concept of net-tax can be situated within the discussion of finding an appropriate measure of wages so that the concept itself can pe properly understood in relation to some other concepts within conventional public finance theory. As an appropriate measure of wages fundamentally two approaches can be adopted. The first approach is based on observed true wages, i.e., focusing on empirical measurements of certain appropriate concepts. Various measures of disposable income used by both orthodox and radical economists fall under this category. The other approach is based on a comparison of observed true wages with some

[Page 73]

hpyothetical, alternative scenarios: thus, here we would compare, say, some measure of the observed true wage with an estimate of its magnitude under some hypothesized alternative circumstances. It should be noted that the first approach is methodologically prior to the second. Accordingly, we will distinguish the two stages by referring to them as Incidence of Type I (observed true wages) and Incidence of Type II (comparison of observed true wage with true wages would occur under the hypothesized conditions, respectively.

The question we ask in this study focuses on the first aspect of analyzing wages: Incidence of Type I. The question here is, what is the observed true wage when the direct participation of the state in the distribution process is taken into consideration? Hence, a concept, net-tax, which measures the actual net impact of state activities in both taxation and expenditures on wages, is proposed. Observed true wage then equals employee compensation minus net-tax which itself equals taxes minus benefits. We consider that the question of Type I Incidence methodologically comes first in the sense that it provides a ground on which we can trace the effect of an alternative distribution scheme. Concretely speaking, for example, the question of what would have been the effect of an increase in the social security tax on wages can be asked only after the construction of an appropriate measure of observed true

[Page 74]

wages. In addition, though the question of Type II Incidence is clearly important, at the same time it requires the construction of rather elaborate models of price formation, growth, demand, etc., besides an empirical specification of observed true wages. Finally, it should be noted that the estimation of Type II Incidence requires not only the construction of adequate and elaborate models, but also the careful specification of valid alternative conditions under which the operation of these models is examined. One conventional empirical approach to the latter question involves the adoption of a 'no-government scenario' as the alternative condition to the existing one, and then attempting to figure out what the true wage would have been in the absence of government activities, i.e., no taxes and no benefits. We reject this sort of alternative condition on the ground that is fails to recognize the state as a necessary aspect of capital accumulation. Instead, we propose a two-stage analysis in which, first, the observed true wage is measured by using the concept of net-tax, and then at the second stage alternative schemes can be developed and compared to the observed true wage. To illustrate this two-stage procedure, so to speak, a simple numerical example is constructed below.

Suppose that wages are $100.00 a week which include gross wage and fringe benefits. Then, the disposable -

[Page 75]

income can be obtained by subtracting taxes, say, $40.00, from wages. However, disposable income is not adequate measure of the observed true wage since it does not take into account the benefits and incomes received from the state. Therefore, we should not only subtract taxed from wages, but also add any such benefits and incomes, i.e., net-tax, to wages. Consequently, the observed true wage equals wages minus taxes ($40.00) plus benefits and incomes ($30.00), i.e., $90.00. Now, at the second stage, suppose that we examine the consequences of a hypothetical rise in the social security tax. Let us say that our model, taking into account the feedback effects of such changes on other taxes, expenditures, profits, demand for labor, etc., indicates that under these conditions the true wage would become $83.00. We could now compare this hypothetical $83.00 true wage with our previous $90.00 observed true wage to estimate the potential effect of a change in the social security tax on true wage.

In this particular study, we are concerned only with the estimation of observed true wage given the state's actual involvement in the distribution process. The second kind of hypothetical question, though very important, methodologically appears later and requires rather complicated macro-models which are outside the scope of our study.

other hand, is made up of 'proprietors' income', 'business subsidies', and 'surpluses of government enterprises'. The NIPA framework overestimates Gross Non-Labor Income while underestimating Gross Labor Income, due to its treatment of indirect business taxes. This category of taxes not only contains 'sales and excise taxes' but also property taxes' of which approximately 50% is paid by homeowners, i.e., mostly babor. The inclusion of property taxes in the category of indirect business taxes derives from the fact that houses are treated as businesses in NIPA. In other words, NIPA treats individual homeowners as individual capitalist firms which rent out their buildings to themselves. This particularly strange treatment of property taxes within indirect business taxes is corrected in the next phase. ## Methodological Decisions

1. **Focus on Type I Incidence**: Tonak chooses to measure observed true wages first, before attempting hypothetical comparisons
2. **Rejection of No-Government Scenario**: Explicitly rejects conventional approach comparing actual wages to hypothetical no-state wages
3. **Two-Stage Analysis**: First measure observed true wage via net-tax, then develop alternative schemes for comparison
4. **State as Necessary Aspect**: Recognizes state as necessary for capital accumulation, not optional add-on
