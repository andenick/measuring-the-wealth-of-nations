## Table II: Calculation of Labor Share, 1952-80

**Location**: Page 92
**Time Period**: Annual data for 1952-1980 (29 years)
**Significance**: **CRITICAL TABLE** - Labor share is the key coefficient used to allocate mixed taxes and expenditures

### Table Structure

**5 Columns**:
1. **Year**: 1952-1980
2. **(1) Total Labor Income**: In billions of dollars
3. **(2) Personal Income**: In billions of dollars
4. **(3) Imputed Income in Personal Income**: In billions of dollars
5. **(4) Adjusted Personal Income**: = (2) - (3), in billions of dollars
6. **(5) Labor Share**: = (1)/(4), ratio

### Formula

```
Labor Share = Total Labor Income / Adjusted Personal Income
```

Where:
- **Total Labor Income** = Wage and salary disbursement + Other labor income (employer contributions to private pension/welfare funds, directors' fees)
- **Adjusted Personal Income** = Personal Income - Imputed Income
- **Imputed Income** = Income items not actually received (imputed rent, imputed interest, etc.)

**Selected Years**:

### Trend Analysis

**Period Averages**:
- 1952-1960: Mean ≈ 0.73 (73%)
- 1961-1970: Mean ≈ 0.73-0.74 (stable)
- 1971-1980: Mean ≈ 0.72 (slight decline)

**Overall Trend**: Labor share **declined gradually** from 73-74% (1950s-1960s) to 71-72% (1970s-1980), reflecting the beginning of the neoliberal shift in income distribution.

**Significance**: Despite the decline, labor share remained relatively stable around 72-73% for most of the period, validating its use as an allocation coefficient.

**Comparison Pattern**:

**1952-1956**: Tonak's LS consistently higher than Weisskopf's
- 1952: Weisskopf .70, Tonak .73 (difference: +0.03)
- 1956: Weisskopf .72, Tonak .74 (difference: +0.02)

**1957-1979**: Weisskopf's LS consistently higher than Tonak's
- 1960: Weisskopf .75, Tonak .73 (difference: -0.02)
- 1970: Weisskopf .78, Tonak .74 (difference: -0.04)
- 1979: Weisskopf .79, Tonak .71 (difference: -0.08)

### Methodological Differences

**Weisskopf's Method** (from text, page 90-91):
- Uses IRS (Internal Revenue Service) data on federal income taxes
- Calculates labor share based on proportion of federal income taxes paid on wage and salary income
- Method: (Federal income taxes on wages) / (Total federal income taxes)

**Tonak's Method**:
- Uses NIPA data on total labor income and adjusted personal income
- Method: (Total labor income) / (Adjusted personal income)
- Broader definition including all labor income, not just wages subject to federal income tax

### Key Insights

1. **Directional Agreement**: Both methods show similar trends over time

2. **Magnitude Differences**:
   - Early period (1952-1956): Tonak's higher by 2-3 percentage points
   - Later period (1957-1979): Weisskopf's higher by 2-8 percentage points

3. **Validation**: The differences are relatively small (< 10 percentage points) and consistent, suggesting both methods are capturing the same underlying phenomenon

4. **Tonak's Conclusion** (page 91):
> "Therefore, there can be no overestimation of the taxes paid by labor due to the method of estimation of labor shares."

The comparison demonstrates that Tonak's labor share estimates are **conservative** for the later period, meaning his calculations of taxes paid by labor are unlikely to be overestimated.

### Key Findings

1. **Labor's Tax Burden**: Labor pays approximately 60% of total taxes in both 1952 and 1980, despite labor share being 73% (1952) and 71% (1980)

2. **Progressive Effect**: The difference between labor share (~72%) and labor's tax share (~60%) suggests a mildly progressive tax structure

3. **Payroll Tax Dominance**: Social insurance contributions (100% allocated to labor) grew from $9B (1952) to $204B (1980)

4. **Corporate Tax**: Entirely allocated to non-labor, declined from $19B (1952) to $34B (1980) in real terms relative to economy size

5. **Property Tax Correction**: NIPA's misclassification corrected, with roughly 50% allocated to labor (homeowners) and 50% to non-labor (businesses)

### Methodological Note (from page 94)

Text discusses validation of the 71% labor share figure used for 1980:
- IRS data: Income up to $49,999 contributed 68.9% of federal income taxes
- But this is **household** income, not individual income
- Therefore "some have incomes which in 1980 fall in the range of $50,000 and above"
- Conclusion: 71% figure is "perfectly adequate" and does not suggest overestimation
