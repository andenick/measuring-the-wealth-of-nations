## ABSTRACT/SUMMARY

This chapter extends the analysis of the U.S. welfare state from the earlier 1987 study through 1997, covering the full postwar period and including the Clinton era. The authors examine the net social wage - the difference between social benefit expenditures and taxes levied directly on the working population - as a proportion of total employee compensation.

**Key Findings:**

1. **Over the entire postwar period (1952-1997)**: The net social wage as a percentage of employee compensation is very modest - it seldom fluctuates beyond ±4%, with an average of mere 0.6% (Figure 29.3, p. 253)

2. **Social wage largely recirculates income**: In effect, social wage flows largely recirculate income among wage and salary earners as a whole. The redistributive effect within the working population appears quite limited in most countries (OECD 1985, ch. 7, section B, 203)

3. **Year-to-year movements strongly affected by unemployment**: Since this affects government expenditures on welfare, unemployment insurance, and so on, and the taxes paid by workers. Unemployment in turn depends on long waves of accelerated growth and slowdown characteristic of capitalist economies.

4. **Three historical phases** identified:
   - **1952-1969 (Boom)**: Net social wage actually negative (net labor tax)
   - **1969-1989 (Crisis and Attack)**: Rise in structural unemployment triggered rises in government spending and simultaneous declines in tax revenues, with Reagan-Bush attack reversing this
   - **1989-1997 (Clinton Era)**: Noncyclical base of benefit ratio raised, tax ratio rises, net social wage modest but stable

5. **Right exploited fiscal disequilibrium**: Combined increased defense spending with attack on welfare state, creating mushrooming government debt and budget deficits that justified further cuts

6. **By 1997**: Net social wage had essentially come back down to zero, reinforcing that the welfare state has very limited impact on worker incomes

**Theoretical Contribution**: Distinguishes between **observed incidence** (actual taxes paid) and hypothetical **tax-shifting incidence** (what workers might have earned in absence of taxes), focusing on the former as the appropriate measure for analyzing state's redistributive impact.

## INTRODUCTION (pp. 247-248)

### Historical Context of Welfare States

**European Origins:**
- The growth of welfare states is one of the hallmarks of modern capitalist democracies
- European welfare states began with pension and social insurance programs in late 19th and early 20th centuries
- Then grew into comprehensive systems of social support between 1930s and 1950s

**U.S. Development:**
- By contrast, the U.S. state only began its excursions into social insurance and public assistance during the Great Depression of the 1930s
- Was typically much less comprehensive in the postwar period (Skocpol 1987)

### Postwar Growth of Welfare State

**Expansion Trends:**
- Nonetheless, in the postwar period the welfare role of the state grew rapidly throughout the advanced capitalist world
- Evidenced by significant rates of increase in state expenditure and taxation, particularly for social expenditures

### Misleading Focus on Expenditures Alone

**Problem with Conventional Analysis:**

In thinking about the financing of the welfare state, it is misleading to focus on the rise in social expenditures alone. Such analyses rose equally sharply (OECD 1985, 16-17).

**More Appropriate Measure:**

Thus when considering the impact on worker incomes, it is more appropriate to look at **the net social wage**: social benefit expenditures received by workers minus taxes paid by them.

**Two Possible Outcomes:**

- **When positive**: Represents a net addition to workers' wages, a net transfer from the state to workers
- **When negative**: Represents a net tax on workers, which is a net transfer in the other direction

### Principal Findings

One of our principal findings is that over the postwar period from 1952 to 1997, the net social wage as a percentage of employee compensation is very modest indeed:

- It seldom fluctuates beyond ±4 percent
- Its average is a mere **0.6 percent** (Figure 29.3, p. 253)

**Implication**: In effect, social wage flows largely recirculate income among wage and salary earners as a whole. And even here, the redistributive effect within the working population appears quite limited in most countries (OECD 1985, ch. 7, section B, 203).

### Cyclical Variations

**Unemployment as Driver:**

Year-to-year movements of the net social wage are strongly affected by the level of unemployment, since this affects:
- Government expenditures on welfare, unemployment insurance, and so on
- The taxes paid by workers

**Long Waves Connection:**

Unemployment in turn depends on the long waves of accelerated growth and slowdown that are characteristic of capitalist economies.

**U.S. Example:**

Thus when in the United States the long boom of 1947-1968 gave way to a subsequent long phase of slowdown and stagnation from 1969 to 1989, the resulting rise in structural unemployment in the latter phase triggered:
- Automatic rises in government spending
- Simultaneous declines in tax revenues

**Fiscal Crisis:**

Combined with the increased defense spending in this period, the average government budget deficit rose almost sixfold as a percentage of GDP.

### Right's Political Strategy

**The Right was able to take advantage** of the structural fiscal disequilibrium and mushrooming government debt of this period by focusing an attack on the welfare state.

**Policy Instruments:**

Public assistance and unemployment benefits were sharply restricted, and unions were systematically undermined.

**Economic Outcomes:**

- Union membership declined rapidly during this period
- Real wages fell
- Worker concessions and givebacks became commonplace
- The number of people in low-wage jobs rose sharply (Rosenberg 1987)

**Military Spending Context:**

On the other hand, military spending was maintained even as social spending was slashed, and corporate taxes were lowered.

### Reagan-Bush Era Results

**Dramatic Rise in Profits:**

These policies had their desired effect. The Reagan-Bush era ushered in a dramatic rise in profits beginning in 1982.

**Subsequent Neoliberal Clinton Era:**

The subsequent neoliberal Clinton era from 1992 to the present has proved equally profit-friendly, though as we shall see, the attack on labor was moderated once favorable conditions for a new round of the accumulation of capital had been restored (Albelda 1999, 15; Mishel et al. 1999).

## MEASURING THE SOCIAL WAGE (pp. 248-249)

### Abstract Level: Division of Net Product

At the most abstract level, the net national product may be thought of as being divided into:
- A portion that goes to labor
- A remainder, the surplus product, which is appropriated by capital

### Concrete Level: State's Role in Redistribution

But at a more concrete level of analysis, it becomes essential to examine the role of the state in modifying this division of the net product.

**Capitalist Accumulation Dependencies:**

Capitalist accumulation depends on the level of profits, while the standard of living of workers depends on their access to:
- Consumption
- Healthcare
- Education
- And so forth

**State's Extracting and Redirecting Function:**

The state performs welfare intervention by:
- Extracting taxes from both sides
- While simultaneously redirecting expenditures back toward them

### Focus of This Study

**Primary Question:**

Our primary focus is on the extent to which the state's involvement in taxation and expenditures serves to redistribute a portion of the nation's surplus product to, or from, the working class.

**Class-Based Methodology:**

In keeping with our focus on class, we define the category of **"working population"** as consisting of those members of the population not having ownership of capital as a principal income source.

**Task Statement:**

Our task is to assess the impact of government activities on the income and consumption of this population by properly accounting for both the expenditures directed toward them and the taxes deducted out of their income stream.

### Two Traditional Methods of After-Tax Income Analysis

In accounting for after-tax income, it is important to note that there are two traditional methods:

#### Method 1: Observed Incidence

**Focus**: The **observed incidence** of taxes

**Definition**: Calculate the income workers actually obtain after the deduction of all taxes flowing out of gross wages

**This is the measure with which we are concerned.**

#### Method 2: Tax-Shifting Incidence

**Focus**: The hypothetical **tax-shifting incidence**

**Question**: In economic analysis, it is also common to estimate the income that workers *might hypothetically* obtain in the absence of some particular taxes

**Definition**: This latter measure of **tax-shifting incidence** is adopted by Miller (1988, 1989), for instance, and many others.

### Comparison of Methods

**Both are relevant**, but they ask rather different questions (Shaikh and Tonak 1987, 193, note 8).

**Extension to Second Methodology:**

Were we to extend our study to the second methodology, our conclusions on the paucity of the net social wage would be strengthened, because the resulting (counterfactual) measure of the net social wage would be considerably more negative, and quite similar to those reported in Miller.

### Government Labor Benefits Categorization

On the side of government labor benefits, we count all social welfare expenditures:
- Health
- Education
- Welfare
- Housing
- Transportation
- Parks and recreation
- Transfer payments to workers
- Etc.

**But exclude** other government spending:
- Transfer payments to businesses
- Expenditures for general administration
- Defense
- Etc.

**Rationale**: This is in sharp contrast to conventional methodology, which tends to treat all government expenditure as a direct social benefit, so that an increase in military spending is viewed as essentially equivalent to an increase in social welfare expenditures.

### Taxes on Working Population

On the side of taxes we count all those that are levied directly on the working population:
- Income taxes
- Social Security taxes
- Property and other taxes
- But exclude those levied on businesses (sales taxes, profit taxes, etc.)

**Notation:**

```
T₁ = total (employee and employer) Social Security taxes

T₂ = personal income taxes, motor vehicle licenses, personal property taxes (primarily on homes), and other taxes and non-taxes (a very small category, which includes passport fees, fines, etc.)
```

### Labor Share (LS)

```
LS = the labor share = the share of wages and salaries in total personal income
```

**Significance:**

The preceding derivation allows us to see that changes in the measure of the labor share affect only a part of measure of the net social wage.

**Reference**: Table 29.1 provides a detailed derivation of the net social wage, and depicts the typical magnitudes involved for 1964.

**Detailed Data**: All further detail is provided in the data appendix, for 1952-1997.

**Important Note**: It is worth remarking that, as in Table 29.1, NSW₁ is positive, and NSW₂ is negative (and is therefore a net labor tax) throughout the postwar period.

**Key Finding**: In effect, direct income support for labor always exceeds direct (Social Security) taxes deducted for this, while social expenditures on health, education, and so forth, always fall short of the general taxes on income and property (see the Appendix).

**Context Over Whole Period:**

Over the whole period, the portion of the net labor tax that arises from the latter virtually cancels out the labor benefit represented by the former.

**Figure 29.1 demonstrates that**, as in all advanced countries, U.S. total labor benefits and total labor taxes rise hand-in-hand over the postwar period.

**Key Finding**: This underscores the importance of looking at both sides of the balance in addressing the social wage issue.

## FORMULAS AND DEFINITIONS (p. 249)

### Basic Tax Categories

```
T₁ = total (employee and employer) Social Security taxes

T₂ = personal income taxes, motor vehicle licenses, personal property taxes
     (primarily on homes), and other taxes and non-taxes (a very small
     category, which includes passport fees, fines, etc.)
```

### Labor Share

```
LS = the labor share = the share of wages and salaries in total personal income
```

### Expenditure Groups

#### Expenditure Group I (E₁)

```
E₁ = government expenditures on labor training and services, housing and
     community services, income support, Social Security, and welfare
     (except the small items called military disability and military
     retirement, which we treat as a cost of war)
```

#### Expenditure Group II (E₂)

```
E₂ = government expenditures on education, health, and hospitals,
     recreational and cultural activities, energy, natural resources,
     passenger transportation, and postal service
```

### Net Social Wage Components

```
NSW = NSW₁ + NSW₂
     = (E₁ - T₁) + (E₂ - T₂) × LS
     = the net social wage

Where:
NSW₁ = E₁ - T₁
NSW₂ = (E₂ - T₂) × LS
```

**Interpretation:**

- **E₁ - T₁**: Net difference between direct labor benefits and Social Security contributions
- **(E₂ - T₂) × LS**: Labor's share of net difference between social consumption expenditures and general taxes

### Comparison with Employee Compensation

Finally, we compare this net social wage to total employee compensation (EC):

```
EC = total cost to capitalists of hiring workers (Tonak 1984)
```

**Components of EC:**

This is the gross pay of workers, and is made up of:
- Wages
- Salaries
- Employers contributions for social insurance
- Other labor income

## ANALYSIS OF DEFICIT RELATIONSHIP (pp. 254-255)

### Figure 29.5 Overview

Finally, Figure 29.5 looks at the net social wage in relation to the total government budget deficit; both scaled by expressing them as a fraction of employee compensation.

**Sign Convention**: Note that a government deficit (an excess of expenditures over receipts) is plotted here as a positive number, to make it consistent with the sign convention used for the social wage.

**Budget Surplus**: Thus a negative budget deficit is a budget surplus, that is, a net tax receipt, while a negative net social wage is a net tax payment.

**Very Similar Behavior**: It is quite striking to observe that until the Reagan-Bush era, the two variables behave in very similar ways.

In the boom phase from 1952 to 1969, the net tax on labor is negative social wage) accounts for a substantial part of overall total government surpluses.

**Reagan and Bush Breaking the Nexus:**

It is Reagan and Bush who break this nexus by simultaneously expanding the relative budget deficit and also slashing the net social wage. Since the net social wage is negative for most of this period, it cannot be said to have any part in the corresponding budget deficits.

**Net Tax on Labor Actually Covers Defense:**

In fact, the net tax on labor actually covers almost 16 percent of defense expenditures between 1987 and 1989.

### Clinton Era (1989-1997)

**Phasing Out Budget Deficits:**

Clinton, by phasing out budget deficits and also the net social wage, effectively restores the historic relation between the two.

## SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS (pp. 254-256)

### Overview

The postwar history of advanced capitalist countries is marked by a tremendous extension in the role of the state.

**Great Expansion in Government Spending:**

In particular, the great expansion in government spending on social programs has given rise to the notion of the modern capitalist state as a welfare state.

**Not a Net Provider:**

But while this may be true, it does not follow that the welfare state is a net provider of goods and services, as some have tended to claim.

**Parallel Tax Rise:**

On the contrary, when one accounts for the parallel rise in taxation that is an equally characteristic feature of the modern state, then something surprising emerges.

### Key Empirical Finding

By and large, it is the taxes of the working population that essentially pay for the corresponding state expenditures on:
- Health
- Education
- Social Security
- Unemployment
- Public assistance
- Housing
- And a host of other social programs

### Average Net Balance

Over the whole postwar period, which is effectively the last half of the twentieth century, the average net balance between taxes directly paid of employee compensation and the social expenditures directly received by the corresponding population is **a mere 0.6 percent of total employee compensation**.

**Interpretation**: It is, in other words, **effectively zero**.

### Comparison of Net Social Wage

We call the concept that we deploy to make this comparison the net social wage. It is the difference between social benefit expenditures (health, education, welfare, housing, transportation, parks and recreation, transfer payments to workers, etc.) and taxes levied directly on the working population (the labor share of income taxes, Social Security, property and other taxes, etc.).

### Fluctuations Driven by Unemployment

We find that the net social wage fluctuates within fairly narrow boundaries, largely between **±4 percent of employee compensation** (Figure 29.3), and over the whole period from 1952 to 1997 its average is essentially zero.

**Year-to-Year Variations:**

But from year to year, its variations are substantially driven by variations in the rate of unemployment. This is because:
- A rise in the relative number of unemployed people induces increased relative state expenditures on income maintenance and unemployment compensation
- While the corresponding drop in the relative number of employed people reduces relative tax receipts (Figure 29.3)

**Mechanism**: This very same mechanism increases the relative budget deficit when the unemployment rate rises.

**Correlation**: For this reason, fluctuations in the net social wage also tend to be highly correlated with the budget deficit (Figure 29.5).

### Level and Trend Also Important

But there is more to the matter than the issue of cyclical fluctuations. The level and trend of the net social wage are also of great importance.

**Long Boom Finding:**

And here, it is striking that during the long boom of 1952-1969, the net social wage was actually nega-

[Text continues to next page]

Tive—that is, there was a net tax imposed on labor during this period.

**Boom Period Characteristics:**

But because it was a boom period with low unemployment and steadily rising real wages, benefits per worker rose more rapidly than did taxes, and over time the benefits received by labor became more and more consistent with their taxes.

### Unraveling of the Boom

All this began to unravel when the long boom ended. By the early 1970s the unemployment rate began to rise sharply, and it continued to rise upward until 1983.

**Economic Privation Period:**

This period of economic privation was attended by increasingly sharp attacks on the welfare state, on unions, and on other institutions that supported the working population.

**Real Employee Compensation:**

Real employee compensation per worker began to fall in the mid-1970s, and its growth remained anemic in the aftermath.

**Welfare State Dismantling:**

And as the welfare state was being dismantled, real benefits per worker were slashed, absolutely and relative to taxes, particularly in the Reagan-Bush era of 1980-1992.

**Net Social Wage Falls:**

Thus even though unemployment reached record highs in that period, the net social wage actually fell, and even became negative.

**"Tax-Cutting" Rhetoric:**

Workers were living at a reduced standard of living and yet paying a net tax to help sustain the benign welfare state, so long a favorite of social democratic theorists, has long since has begun to show its teeth.

**Rhetoric vs Reality:**

The rhetoric and reality of the times could not have been more discrepant. It is particularly ironic that the net tax on labor helped substantially offset the greatly expanded defense expenditures of this period.

### Critical Result of Attack on Labor

A critical result of this attack on labor, and its associated support for capital, is that it served to restore the conditions of accumulation:

**Profitability Rising:**

Profitability began to rise sharply after 1982, and has continued to grow ever since.

## NOTES (pp. 256-257)

### Note 1: Social Expenditures and Tax Shifting

On the side of social expenditures, if we were to count veteran's benefits and services and military retirements and disability (both of which we exclude as cost of war), and, on the side of taxes shift 50 percent of business taxes (corporate income taxes and indirect business taxes) to the labor account, our resulting estimates of the net social wage would fall between Miller's (1989) and O'Connor methodologies (Miller 1989, 85, Table 3).

### Note 2: Excluded Expenditures

The excluded expenditures consist of two kinds:

**(1) Central executive, legislative and judicial activities, international affairs, space, national defense, civilian safety, veteran benefits, and agriculture**, which are the expenses of reproducing and maintaining the system itself (what Marx calls the *faux frais* of capitalist society) [Marx 1977, 446]

**(2) Those such as economic development, regulation and services, net interest, and others and unallocables**, which represents expenditures directed mainly toward small businesses, related administrative activities, and interest payments to the highest income brackets.

**Treatment**: All of this group is therefore excluded from labor income and consumption.

### Note 3: Excluded Taxes

The excluded taxes also consist of two kinds: direct and indirect business taxes, and estate and gift taxes.

**Rationale**: Since the former is levied on business, and the latter almost exclusively on those with substantial nonlabor income and wealth, both are excluded from the labor account.

### Note 4: Marxian Terminology - Variable Capital

Within Marxian terminology this is the same as (nominal) variable capital if we abstract from the distinction between productive and unproductive labor.

**At a more concrete level**: Variable capital is the total employee compensation of productive labor alone.

**Strictly speaking**: We should remove the incomes of corporate officers and managers from employee compensation, and add in a wage equivalent for self-employed people.

**Simplification**: But since these two corrections appear to be offsetting, we ignore them in the present study.

### Note 5: Labor Share Calculations

As indicated in note 4, detailed estimates of the labor share that exclude corporate officers and other managers' salaries, and that add in the wage equivalent of most self-employed persons, would not significantly change the labor share.

**Wage Equivalent Impact:**

A variable capital labor share, in turn, would only affect NSW₂.

### Note 6: Unemployment and Benefit/Tax Ratios

When the unemployment rate rises, employee compensation falls and total benefits rise (since there are more people receiving them).

**Benefit Ratio Impact:**

Thus in the short run, the ratio of benefits to employee compensation, rises.

**Tax Ratio Impact:**

On the side of taxes, if all people paid the same tax rate, all taxes would go down, but the tax ratio would remain unchanged.

**Progressive Tax Effect:**

But in point of fact, a reduction of employee compensation moves people into lower tax brackets, and so the tax ratio actually falls (modestly) when the unemployment rate rises.

**Combined Effect:**

These phenomena make unemployment evident in Figure 29.2.

### Note 7: Budget Deficit and Labor Subsidy

Data on the combined budget deficit and labor subsidy can be derived from various years of BEA, *Survey of Current Business*.

**Defense Expenditures**: Data on defense expenditures are available from various years of BEA, *Survey of Cur-rent Business*.

## SUMMARY OF KEY FORMULAS

### Net Social Wage

```
NSW = NSW₁ + NSW₂

Where:
NSW₁ = E₁ - T₁
NSW₂ = (E₂ - T₂) × LS

E₁ = Direct labor benefits (entirely allocated to labor)
E₂ = Social consumption expenditures (partially allocated)
T₁ = Social Security taxes (paid entirely by labor)
T₂ = Personal taxes (partially allocated to labor)
LS = Labor share = wages and salaries / total personal income
```

### Net Social Wage Ratio

```
Net Social Wage Ratio = NSW / EC

Where:
NSW = Net social wage
EC  = Employee compensation (apparent wage)
```

### True Real Wage

```
True Real Wage per FEE = (EC + NSW) / (CPI × FEE)

Where:
EC  = Employee compensation
NSW = Net social wage
CPI = Consumer Price Index
FEE = Full-time equivalent employees
```

### Apparent Real Wage

```
Apparent Real Wage per FEE = EC / (CPI × FEE)
```

## HISTORICAL PHASES SUMMARY

### Phase 1: Postwar Boom (1952-1969)

**Characteristics:**
- Net social wage: Negative (workers are net taxpayers)
- Range: -2% to -7% of employee compensation
- Real wages: Rising steadily
- Unemployment: Low, stable around 5%
- Benefit ratio: Rising gradually from ~12% to ~20%
- Tax ratio: Rising gradually from ~12% to ~22%

**Dynamics:**
- Boom conditions allow gradual improvement
- Net social wage becomes less negative over time
- Stable growth, labor gains relative strength
- Benefits rise somewhat faster than taxes

### Phase 2: Crisis Onset and Peak (1969-1975)

**Characteristics:**
- Net social wage: Sharply positive peak (~+5-9% in 1975)
- Unemployment: Doubles from 3.6% to 8.5%
- Benefit ratio: Accelerates to ~33%
- Tax ratio: Continues rising but slower than benefits
- Real wages: Stagnation begins

**Dynamics:**
- Crisis triggers automatic increases in unemployment benefits and welfare
- Tax revenues decline relative to wages
- Net social wage rises as consequence (not cause) of crisis
- Budget deficits expand dramatically

### Phase 3: Reagan-Bush Attack (1975-1989)

**Sub-period 1: Ford-Carter (1975-1980)**
- Unemployment recedes somewhat
- Benefit ratio begins to fall
- Net social wage declines from peak

**Sub-period 2: Reagan Era (1980-1989)**
- Unemployment reaches all-time highs (10% in 1982-1983)
- Benefit ratio fails to rise despite unemployment
- Sharp cuts to welfare, social programs
- Union membership collapses
- Real wages fall
- Net social wage returns to negative territory
- Benefit ratio falls below 1966 levels by late 1980s
- Tax ratio continues climbing
- Defense spending expands dramatically
- Budget deficits explode

**Characteristics:**
- Net social wage: Returns to negative (-1% to -2%)
- By 1989: Among most negative in postwar period
- Workers living at reduced standards yet paying net tax
- Real wages: Falling, then stagnant
- Benefit ratio: Slashed from ~33% to below 1966 levels
- Tax ratio: Continues rising, reaches 41% above 1966

**Political Strategy:**
- Attack on welfare state justified by deficits
- Simultaneously expand defense spending
- Dismantle social safety net
- Undermine unions
- "Tax-cutting" rhetoric while workers pay more

### Phase 4: Clinton Neoliberal Era (1989-1997)

**Characteristics:**
- Net social wage: Returns to near zero (0.6% average)
- Unemployment: Declining through 1990s
- Benefit ratio: Modest evidence of raised noncyclical base
- Tax ratio: Continues rising as employment recovers
- Real wages: Modest recovery begins
- Budget deficit: Dramatically reduced, approaching balance

**Dynamics:**
- Profitability restored from Reagan era continues
- Accumulation resumes
- Unemployment falls
- Net social wage falls with it but not as sharply
- Some evidence benefits base raised modestly
- Tax and benefit ratios converge by 1997
- Budget discipline emphasized
- Welfare reform enacted
- Historic relationship between net social wage and deficit restored

**Key Finding**: By 1997, net social wage had essentially come back down to zero

## METHODOLOGICAL CONTRIBUTIONS

### Observed vs. Hypothetical Incidence

**Key Distinction**: This study focuses on **observed incidence** of taxes (what workers actually pay) rather than **tax-shifting incidence** (what they might have earned absent taxes)

**Implication**: If tax-shifting approach were used, net social wage would be even more negative, strengthening the conclusions

**Reference**: Miller (1988, 1989) uses tax-shifting approach

### Labor Share Method

**Purpose**: Allocate expenditures and taxes between labor and capital

**Formula**: LS = Total labor income / Total personal income

**Application**: Used to determine labor's portion of:
- Group II expenditures (social consumption)
- Group II taxes (personal taxes)

**Stability**: Labor share relatively stable over period (0.69-0.73)

### Group Classification

**Expenditure Group I**: Entirely allocated to labor
- Direct benefits (income support, Social Security, welfare)
- Housing and community services
- Labor training and services

**Expenditure Group II**: Partially allocated via labor share
- Education
- Health and hospitals
- Recreation and culture
- Energy
- Natural resources
- Transportation
- Postal service

**Tax Group I**: Entirely from labor
- Social Security contributions (employee and employer)

**Tax Group II**: Partially allocated via labor share
- Personal income taxes (federal, state, local)
- Motor vehicle licenses
- Property taxes (primarily homes)
- Other personal taxes and non-taxes

### Exclusions

**Expenditures Excluded**:
- Military retirement and disability (cost of war)
- Transfer payments to businesses
- General administration
- Defense
- Faux frais of capitalist society

**Taxes Excluded**:
- Corporate profit taxes
- Indirect business taxes
- Estate and gift taxes (levied on highest incomes)

### Comparison with Employee Compensation

**Key Innovation**: Express net social wage as proportion of employee compensation (EC)

**Rationale**: EC is the total cost to capitalists of hiring workers, the most meaningful denominator

**Components of EC**:
- Wages and salaries
- Employer contributions for social insurance
- Other labor income

## POLICY AND THEORETICAL IMPLICATIONS

### Critique of "Welfare State" Concept

**Main Finding**: Term "welfare state" is misleading if it implies net provision of goods and services to workers

**Reality**: Workers essentially pay for their own social programs through taxes

**Net Balance**: Over 45-year period, average net social wage is 0.6% of compensation - effectively zero

### Class Analysis

**Standard of Living Basis**: Real wage that workers can win from employers remains fundamental determinant of living standards

**Welfare State Impact**: Very limited impact on worker incomes despite massive expansion

**Class Struggle Centrality**: Rising wages during boom, stagnation during crisis forcibly remind us that class struggle and reserve army of labor continue to play central role

### State and Capital Accumulation

**State Not Neutral**: Attack on labor under Reagan-Bush served to restore conditions for accumulation

**Profitability**: Began rising sharply after 1982, continued through Clinton era

**Accumulation**: Eventually resumed, reducing unemployment by 1990s

**Political Constraint**: State intervention most constrained precisely when crisis makes it most needed

### Fiscal Policy

**Deficit-Social Wage Correlation**: Highly correlated because both driven by unemployment

**Not Causal**: Net social wage does not cause deficits

**Reagan-Bush Breaking Nexus**: Broke historical pattern by expanding deficits while slashing net social wage

**Defense Spending**: Increased defense expenditures the actual cause of expanded deficits, with net labor tax helping cover almost 16% of defense spending (1987-1989)

**Failed Premise**: Social democratic faith in welfare state as redistribution mechanism from capital to labor is empirically unfounded

**"Benign" Welfare State Myth**: Shown to have "long since begun to show its teeth"

**Reality**: Workers subsidize state even during supposed "golden age" of welfare capitalism

### Neoliberalism

**Clinton Era**: Neoliberal regime benefited from Reagan-Bush restoration of profitability

**Little Structural Change**: Showed little inclination to change structures put in place

**Modest Benefit Base Rise**: Some evidence of raised noncyclical benefit base, but with continued high taxes

**Budget Balance Priority**: Emphasis on deficit reduction over social spending

## GLOSSARY OF TERMS

### Key Concepts

**Net Social Wage (NSW)**: Benefits and income received by workers from the state minus taxes paid by workers to the state

**Apparent Wage**: Employee compensation (EC) - the total cost to capitalists of hiring workers

**True Wage**: Apparent wage plus net social wage - the actual income workers receive after accounting for state transfers

**Labor Share (LS)**: The share of wages and salaries in total personal income

**Observed Incidence**: Actual taxes paid by workers out of gross wages

**Tax-Shifting Incidence**: Hypothetical income workers might obtain in absence of particular taxes

**Faux Frais**: Marx's term for expenses of reproducing and maintaining the capitalist system itself

### Expenditure Categories

**E₁**: Government expenditures entirely allocated to labor (direct benefits)

**E₂**: Government expenditures partially allocated to labor via labor share (social consumption)

**Group I**: Income support, Social Security, welfare, housing, labor training

**Group II**: Education, health, hospitals, recreation, energy, natural resources, transportation, postal

### Tax Categories

**T₁**: Taxes paid entirely by labor (Social Security contributions)

**T₂**: Taxes partially paid by labor via labor share (personal income taxes, property taxes, etc.)

### Wage Measures

**Employee Compensation (EC)**: Wages + salaries + employer contributions + other labor income

**Real Wage**: Nominal wage deflated by Consumer Price Index (CPI)

**Per FEE**: Per full-time equivalent employee

**Constant Dollars**: Adjusted for inflation (1982 dollars used in this study)

### Key Ratios

**Benefit Ratio**: Labor benefits / Employee compensation

**Tax Ratio**: Labor taxes / Employee compensation

**Net Social Wage Ratio**: Net social wage / Employee compensation

**Deficit Ratio**: Government budget deficit / Employee compensation

## FINAL SUMMARY

This 2002 chapter extends the 1987 analysis of the U.S. welfare state through 1997, encompassing the entire postwar period including the Clinton era. The central finding remains robust: **the net social wage as a percentage of employee compensation averaged a mere 0.6% over 45 years - effectively zero**.

**Key Empirical Contributions:**

1. **Long-Run Pattern**: Social wage flows largely recirculate income among wage earners; the welfare state has very limited redistributive impact

2. **Three Phases Clearly Delineated**:
   - **Boom (1952-1969)**: Workers are net taxpayers, but gap gradually narrowing
   - **Crisis and Attack (1969-1989)**: Brief positive net social wage during crisis, then Reagan-Bush attack drives it negative again
   - **Clinton Era (1989-1997)**: Return to near-zero net social wage

3. **Unemployment as Driver**: Year-to-year fluctuations strongly driven by unemployment, not by policy choices about "generosity"

4. **Deficit Correlation**: Net social wage and budget deficits highly correlated because both driven by unemployment; Reagan-Bush broke this historical pattern by expanding deficits while slashing social programs

5. **Real Wage Fundamental**: Despite welfare state expansion, real wage from employers remains fundamental basis of workers' living standards

**Theoretical Implications:**

- Refutes social democratic claims about welfare state as major redistribution from capital to labor
- Supports classical Marxian crisis theory over "wage-induced profit squeeze" arguments
- Demonstrates state's limited autonomy - most constrained when intervention most needed
- Shows fiscal policy primarily recirculates income among workers, not from capital to labor

**Political Implications:**

- "Benign welfare state" is myth; workers essentially pay for their own social programs
- Reagan-Bush attack successfully restored profitability at workers' expense
- Clinton neoliberalism maintained this structure while restoring fiscal "discipline"
- Progressive strategy cannot rely on welfare state expansion; must focus on wage struggle and class power

**Methodological Contribution:**

Provides clear, replicable framework for calculating net social wage using official government statistics, distinguishing observed tax incidence from hypothetical tax-shifting, and properly accounting for both expenditures and taxes at class level.

**Historical Significance:**

Documents the rise and fall of U.S. welfare state over its full postwar trajectory, from modest New Deal origins through postwar expansion to neoliberal retrenchment, revealing that even at its peak the welfare state's redistributive impact was essentially nil.
