# Key Findings - Chunk 024
## The Net Social Wage in Turkey, 1980-2019

---

## Main Argument

The chapter presents an empirical application of the **Shaikh-Tonak net transfer method** to measure whether the Turkish welfare state is a net contributor to or net extractor from the working class.

**Central Finding**: Over 40 years (1980-2019), the Turkish state has been a **net extractor** from the working class, taking more in taxes than it provides in benefits.

---

## Quantitative Results

### 40-Year Averages (1980-2019)

```
Public Expenditure Benefiting Workers:  9.95% of GDP
Taxes Paid by Workers:                 11.08% of GDP
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net Social Wage:                       -1.13% of GDP
```

**Interpretation**: For every 100 units of GDP, workers receive 9.95 in benefits but pay 11.08 in taxes, resulting in a net loss of 1.13 units.

### Only 5 Positive Years (out of 40)

| Year | NSW (% GDP) | Context |
|------|-------------|---------|
| 1997 | +0.00% | Essentially zero, balanced |
| 2007 | +0.51% | Pre-crisis expansion |
| 2008 | +0.94% | Crisis response begins |
| **2009** | **+1.98%** | Peak - height of crisis intervention |
| 2010 | +0.91% | Continued crisis response |

**Pattern**: Positive NSW only during 2008 financial crisis period (Keynesian countercyclical policy)

### Worst Years (Most Negative NSW)

| Year | NSW (% GDP) | Context |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **2001** | **-3.13%** | Worst year - Turkish financial crisis |
| 2000 | -2.63% | Pre-crisis deterioration |
| 1991 | -2.51% | Gulf War period |
| 1992 | -2.42% | Continued instability |
| 1989 | -2.32% | Neoliberal intensification |

## Trends Over Time

### Period 1: 1980-1988 (Early Neoliberal Era)
- Tax burden: 22.82% → 36.4% of labour income
- Benefits: Relatively stable but low
- NSW: Negative but moderate (-0.42% to -2.12%)

### Period 2: 1989-1996 (Deepening Extraction)
- Tax burden: Sharp increase to 50.44% of labour income by 1996
- Benefits: Increased but not enough to offset taxes
- NSW: Increasingly negative (-1.14% to -2.32%)

### Period 3: 1997-2006 (Volatile)
- 1997: Brief equilibrium (NSW ≈ 0%)
- 1998-2001: Deterioration to worst point (-3.13% in 2001)
- 2002-2006: Gradual improvement but still negative

### Period 4: 2007-2010 (Crisis Response)
- **ONLY sustained positive period in 40 years**
- State acts as net contributor to working class
- Peak intervention in 2009 (+1.98%)

### Period 5: 2011-2018 (Return to Extraction)
- NSW returns to negative territory
- Recent years: -0.95% to -1.72%
- 2018: -0.05% (approaching balance)

## Methodological Framework

### The Shaikh-Tonak Net Transfer Method

**Core Equation**:
```
NSW = B - T

Where:
  B = (ls × B₂) + B₃
  T = T₁ + (ls × T₂)
```

**Key Innovation**: Uses **labour share coefficient (ls)** to allocate universal public goods and taxes

```
ls = Wages and Salaries / Personal Income
```

### Three-Category Classification

#### Public Expenditures (B)

**B₁ (Zero benefit to workers)**
- General public services
- Defence
- Public order and security
- Economic affairs (subsidies to capital)

**B₂ (Universal benefits, allocated by labour share)**
- Education
- Healthcare
- Transport
- Environmental protection
- Housing and community amenity
- Recreation, culture, religious services
- Fuel and energy

**B₃ (100% to workers)**
- Social security and social assistance
- General labour affairs
- Unemployment benefits

#### Taxes (T)

**T₁ (100% from workers)**
- Social security premia (employer + employee)
- Unemployment insurance fund deductions

**T₂ (From all, allocated by labour share)**
- Personal income tax
- Value-added tax (VAT)
- Special consumption tax
- Motor vehicle tax (private cars)
- Banking and insurance transaction tax
- Stamp duties
- Customs duties
- Property and enterprise revenues
- Fees and fines

**T₃ (Zero from workers)**
- Corporate income tax
- Inheritance and gift taxes
- Real estate tax
- Land tax
- Motor vehicle tax (commercial vehicles)
- Self-employed pension fund premia

## Methodological Challenges

### Data Issues

1. **Two Time Series**
   - Old series: 1980-1997
   - New series: 1998-2019
   - Discontinuity due to measurement method change
   - New series likely overestimates GDP → understates NSW ratios

2. **Classification Compromises**

   **Top Management** (bourgeois but receives "wages"):
   - Should exclude from working class
   - Cannot separate in national accounts
   - **Solution**: Include (assume small population)
   - **Effect**: Slight overstatement of benefits

   **Self-Employed** (petty bourgeoisie with labor income):
   - Should include portion as wages
   - Classified as "enterprise surplus" in national accounts
   - **Solution**: Exclude entirely
   - **Effect**: Understatement of working class size

   **Military Veterans**:
   - Should exclude benefits to retired military
   - Cannot separate from social security data
   - **Solution**: Include
   - **Effect**: Overstatement of B₃ benefits

### Alternative Calculation

**Primary Method** (in tables): Uses labour share for income tax allocation
- 40-year average NSW: **-1.13%**

**Alternative Method** (footnote 21): Uses direct wage income tax data
- 40-year average NSW: **-2.14%**
- Difference: Alternative shows **WORSE** outcome for workers

**Implication**: Primary method is actually more favorable to state; true extraction likely higher

## Theoretical Implications

### Class Analysis of the State

**Liberal Theory**: Welfare state redistributes from rich to poor
**Neoclassical Theory**: State inefficiently distorts market outcomes
**Social Democratic Theory**: Welfare state decommodifies labor, empowers workers

**Marxian Analysis (Shaikh-Tonak)**: Empirically test state's net effect on class relations

**This Study's Evidence**:
- Turkish state is **net extractor** from working class (35 of 40 years)
- Only positive during crisis when capital needs demand stimulus
- Welfare state primarily serves reproduction of capital, not workers

### Labour Share Coefficient (ls)

Critical methodological innovation:
- Allocates "public goods" by workers' share of national income
- More workers earn = more they benefit from universal services
- Less workers earn = more capitalists benefit

**Turkey's declining labour share** means:
- Even as education/healthcare spending rises
- Workers' share of benefits falls
- Capitalists' appropriation rises

### The Crisis Exception (2007-2010)

**Only positive NSW period coincides with 2008 financial crisis**

**Marxian Interpretation**:
- State intervenes to sustain aggregate demand
- Prevents collapse of consumption → protects capital accumulation
- Not "generosity" but crisis management
- Returns to extraction once crisis passes (2011+)

**Keynesian Countercyclical Policy**:
- Proves state CAN be net contributor
- But only does so when capital's survival requires it
- "Normal" operation is extraction

## Political Economy Context

### Neoliberal Transformation (1980-2019)

**1980**: Military coup, beginning of neoliberal restructuring
- Initial NSW: -0.42% (relatively moderate)
- Tax burden: 22.82% of labour income

**1980s**: Structural adjustment
- Dismantling of import-substitution model
- Trade liberalization
- Financial deregulation
- NSW worsens to -2.32% by 1989

**1990s**: Financial instability
- Multiple crises (1994, 2001)
- Tax burden reaches 50.44% of labour income (1996)
- Benefits rise but taxes rise faster

**2000s**:
- 2001 crisis: Worst NSW ever (-3.13%)
- IMF programs
- 2008 crisis: Brief reversal to positive NSW
- AKP government expansion of social programs (but also taxes)

**2010s**: Return to extraction
- Post-crisis austerity
- NSW back to negative
- Recent years approaching balance but still extractive

### Taxation Burden on Workers

**Key Shift**: From 22.82% (1980) to 50%+ (1996-2001) of labour income

**Mechanism**:
- Introduction of VAT (1985) - regressive consumption tax
- Social security premia increases
- Broadening of income tax base to wage earners
- Special consumption taxes

**Effect**: Workers pay roughly half their income in various taxes and contributions

## Implications for Policy and Theory

### For Development Economics

**Question**: Does welfare state reduce inequality in developing countries?

**Turkey Evidence**: Not necessarily
- State can extract more than it provides
- "Welfare" institutions may primarily serve capital reproduction
- Net effect requires empirical measurement, not assumption

### For Political Sociology

**Question**: Do workers benefit from state expansion?

**Turkey Evidence**: Depends
- Larger state doesn't automatically help workers
- Tax structure matters as much as spending
- Crisis periods show state capacity to help but normal operation is extractive

### For Marxian Theory

**Validates**: State as instrument of class rule
- Even "welfare state" can exploit workers
- Positive redistribution only when capital requires it (crisis)
- Normal operation: facilitate capital accumulation via taxation of workers

**Nuances**: Not simple "ruling class tool"
- Complex mediation through fiscal mechanisms
- Some genuine benefits to workers (B₃ categories)
- But net effect over time is extractive

   - 2007-2010 proves positive NSW is possible
   - State chooses extraction in normal times

3. **Focus on net transfers, not just spending**

   - Progressive taxation essential for redistributive outcome

1. **Measure net effects, not gross spending**
2. **Tax incidence crucial** - who actually pays matters
3. **Labour share critical variable** - affects benefit distribution
4. **Time series essential** - static analysis misses trends

## Conclusion

The Turkish welfare state, from 1980-2019, has been a **net extractor from the working class**, taking more in taxes than it provides in benefits in 35 of 40 years.

The only sustained positive period (2007-2010) coincided with the global financial crisis, suggesting the state becomes a net contributor only when capital accumulation requires demand support.

This empirical finding challenges liberal assumptions about the welfare state's redistributive effects and provides support for Marxian theories of the capitalist state as an instrument facilitating capital accumulation, even through ostensibly "pro-worker" welfare institutions.

The **Shaikh-Tonak net transfer method** proves effective for empirically measuring class effects of state fiscal policy and can be applied comparatively across countries and time periods.
