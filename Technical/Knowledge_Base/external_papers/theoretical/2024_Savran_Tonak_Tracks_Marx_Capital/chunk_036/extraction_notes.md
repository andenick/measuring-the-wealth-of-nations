### CHAPTER 14 CONCLUSION (Pages 331-333)

**Super-Exploitation Debate Continued** (Page 331):

**Empirical Finding - iPhone 10**:
> "Our recent work on the iPhone 10 estimated the rate of exploitation of the workers involved in its production as above 2000% (Tricontinental, 2019)."

**Critical Assessment of Smith's Super-Exploitation Theory**:

**Value transfer complexity**:
- Multiple sources (unequal exchange, FDI, debt, portfolio investment)
- Direction and net amount "rather complicated to determine"
- Cannot single out one source (e.g., higher S/V rates) and build theory around it

**Super-exploitation definition problem**:
- **Smith's definition**: Arises when South S/V > average North S/V
- **Critical gap**: "There is no single comparative estimate of the rates of surplus-value in the book itself, even though it claims that the very concept of super-exploitation not only serves as the basis of a new theory of imperialism but also initiates the renaissance of Marxism."

**Footnote 11**: According to Smith, unequal exchange between capitalist and laborer (workers paid below value of labor power) leads to super-exploitation

**Footnote 12**: "Skilling problem" in Smith, Ricardo, Marx
- Shaikh & Glenn (2018) paper: Equal rates of exploitation within country imply unequal wages
- URL: https://ideas.repec.org/p/new/wpaper/1811.html

**Footnote 13**: Research areas covered in:
- Shaikh & Tonak (1994): *Measuring the Wealth of Nations*
- Shaikh (2016): *Capitalism: Competition, Conflict, Crises* (more comprehensive)

**Theoretical Framework for Imperialism**:

> "To recapitulate, the theory of imperialism does need a theory of global competition, and one can be developed on the basis of Marx's notion of domestic competition as presented in Capital."

**Required Components**:
1. Theory of international trade
2. Theory of real exchange rate determination
3. Theory of price formation through **real competition**:
   - Between sectors internationally
   - Within sectors internationally

**Imperialism Redefined**:

- **INSTEAD**: Manifestations of uneven and historically determined nature of global capitalist development

**Value Transfer Role**:

**Key Insight**: Value transfer **perpetuates** (not causes) imperialist relations

**2008 Crisis Narrative** (Pages 336-337):

**Lehman Brothers Collapse**:
- September 2008: Bankruptcy sent "shock waves across the world"
- International financial system → "total collapse"

**Crisis Trigger - Housing Market**:
- **Geography**: US, UK, Spain, Ireland, others
- **Mechanism**: Mortgages pushed to consumers unable to pay back
- **Sub-standard mortgage market**: Unrealistic values
- **Derivative packaging**:
  - Collateralized debt obligations (CDOs)
  - Collateralized loan obligations (CLOs)
- **Cascade effect**: Mortgage collapse → derivative collapse → financial market collapse
- Banks and financial institutions "facing the precipice"

**From Finance to Real Economy**:
> "The name 'global financial crisis' was, at first, used exclusively to depict the profound crisis that the collapse of Lehman Brothers triggered in the world economy. However, it rapidly became a misnomer, as it became clear that the crisis was not peculiar to the sphere of finance but extended to the so-called real economy—in other words, the sphere of production."

**Real economy impact**:
- Steep, unprecedented decline in growth and investment
- Catastrophic unemployment rise
- **Worst cases**: Spain and Greece youth unemployment **>50% for years**

**"Great Recession" Terminology Critique** (Page 337):

**Authors' position**: "We refrain from using the term 'Great Recession'"

**Reason**: "This was a smokescreen meant to hide the true nature of the crisis"

**Oxymoron argument**:
> "A 'great recession' is something of an oxymoron. Recessions are conventionally defined as rather brief periods lasting longer than two quarters (or six months) that are fixedon a single economic variable, the rate of growth, when the latter enters the negative zone."

**Purpose of term**:
> "The appellation great recession was, in fact, invented to prevent state dignitaries, financial pundits, and the economics profession from using the 'D-word', i.e., Depression."

**Alan Greenspan's admission**:
- Federal Reserve chairman 1987-2006 (escaped before crisis hit)
- "Most orthodox defenders of rationality of markets and capitalism"
- Called it "once-in-a-century financial crisis"
- **Clear reference**: Comparison to Great Depression of 1930s

**Conclusion**: "'Great Recession' concedes the 'greatness' of the crisis to conceal the fact that it was a depression"

**Types of Capitalist Crises** (Pages 337-338):

**Business Cycles (Common)**:
- **Frequency**: Roughly once per decade
- **Literature**: "Business cycles" (specialist writing "outside central core of mainstream economics")
- **Culmination**: Recession (brief shrinkage period)
- **Resolution**: Market force adjustment + government policy assistance (post-WWII)

**Depression (Rare)**:
- **Duration**: Decade(s)
- **Cannot be overcome** through conventional market adjustment
- **Requires**: Radical convulsions in:
  1. Economic sphere
  2. Political sphere
  3. Ideological sphere
  4. Military domain

**Great Depressions (World-Level)**:

1. **"Long Depression"**: 1873-1896 (~23 years)
2. **Great Depression**: 1929-1945 (16 years)
   - Started: Wall Street crash 1929
   - Spread throughout 1930s
   - Lasted until WWII end (1945) for many European countries
3. **Current Crisis**: Authors' view = also a great depression

**Authors' position**:
> "In the opinion of many Marxist economists, including the authors of this notebook, the prolonged and profound crisis that we are experiencing today is also a great depression."

**Section 1: The Dimensions of the Current Crisis** (Pages 338-341)

**Differential Trajectories**:

**Imperialist Countries**:
- **Origin** of crisis
- Populations suffered: growth, investment, unemployment, labor productivity
- **United States** (unique trajectory):
  - Partial recovery after 2013
  - Edge over Eurozone/Japan until 2019
  - 2019: Began slowdown
  - Early 2020: COVID-19 pandemic impact + successive shutdowns

**Emerging Countries** (Argentina, Brazil, India, Mexico, Russia, South Africa, Turkey):
- **Completely opposite trajectory** from US
- Initial shock → quick recovery → extraordinary performance

**Turkey case study**:
- Year 1: Shrunk
- Years 2-3: **Highest growth rates country ever attained**

**Mechanism**:
- Imperialist countries: Cheap money policy (central banks) → reduced interest rates
- Finance flowed to emerging countries (higher earnings potential)
- "Bane of world in crisis proved to be boon" for emerging countries
- Economies "propelled by inflow of all types of foreign liquidity"

**2013 Reversal**:
- US economy began recovery
- Federal Reserve changed cheap money policy
- → **"Period of hard times for emerging countries"**

**Less/Least Developed Countries**:
- Suffered from fall in international trade
- Reduced demand for staple exports
- Higher decline in FDI
- Primary commodity price beating
- **Differential impact**: Energy importers vs exporters
- **Overall impact**: "Accumulation of mountain of foreign debt"

**China - Exceptional Case**:
- Considers itself developing country
- Has NOT suffered same fate as rest of world
- Maintained **extremely high growth rates for decades**
- Based on: Internal dynamics + interaction with world economy
- Even post-2013: Persisted as **single case** demonstrating "decoupling" from imperialist economies
