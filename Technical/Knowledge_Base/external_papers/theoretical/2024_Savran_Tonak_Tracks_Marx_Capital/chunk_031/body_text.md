# Chapter 13: Lean Production and Flexibility: The Highest Stage of Capitalist Control

## Pages 301-310 (Book pages 281-290)

### Continuation: Critique of Fordism (Pages 281-282)

Henry Ford's 1914 T-model innovation introduced the moving assembly line to car manufacturing. The assembly line originated in slaughterhouses where animals were "disassembled" on moving lines by different worker groups. The moving assembly line gives capitalists control over the entire factory's work pace, increasing labor intensity systematically.

However, to utilize the assembly line advantage, capital must: (1) possess workers' production knowledge, (2) eliminate worker skills, (3) divide work into small units, (4) design the work process itself, and (5) control job design. This makes "Fordism" essentially **Taylorism reinforced by the moving assembly line**.

Fordism as a concept lacks sufficiently specified characteristics as a labor process. The regime of "intensive accumulation" and "mass consumption" thesis are unconvincing. Fordism should be abandoned as a vacuous concept. Taylorism deserves reinstatement as the proper term for this management style and labor control method.

## Section 3: Lean Production - Capital Fighting "Idle Time" (Pages 282-290)

### Introduction to Lean Production (Pages 282-283)

Monopoly capital worldwide is establishing a production system with novel characteristics combining new business techniques and labor process innovations. First implemented in Japan from the 1960s, it spread globally in the 1980s-1990s.

**Terminology critique:**
- **"Post-Fordism"**: Least appropriate (since "Fordism" is meaningless)
- **"Japanese production system" / "Toyotism"**: Unnecessarily tied to ethnic/national-cultural specificities; obscures the system's universal character
- **"Flexible production"**: Corresponds to reality but prone to ideological distortion; hides that flexibility extends beyond production to labor power circulation
- **"Lean production"**: Most accurate term - impervious to ideological distortion; defined as production cleansed of all resource use beyond strict necessity. "Lean" like lean meat: robust, dynamic, energetic.

**Lean production's aim:** NOT to "humanize" production, but to consolidate capital's control over labor, increasing productivity and intensity while saving on constant capital.

**Two dimensions:**
1. Labor process and worker management (capital-labor relations)
2. Workplace fragmentation with supplier firm constellations (inter-capital relations with capital-labor consequences)

### The Contradictions of the Assembly Line (Pages 284-287)

Lean production addresses contradictions in moving assembly line systems that cause surplus-value loss:

#### Problem 1: Idle Time (Pages 284-285)

**Idle time** = total moments when workers don't perform useful acts for capital. Primary source: **"balancing of the line"** problem - incompatibilities and disharmony in work flow between work stations/workers/machines. Imbalances create either glitches or unused labor surplus.

Every non-productive moment is lost capital value. Management constantly attempts balancing, but each solution creates new imbalances.

**Lean production's central goal** (US Transportation Department report): "making each worker work 60 minutes every hour."

**Taiichi Ohno** (lean production's Japanese inventor - the "Taylor of lean production"): If only needed work is "real work" and the rest is waste, then:

> Present capacity = work + waste

True efficiency = zero waste + 100% work. In Toyota production system, must make only needed amount; manpower must be reduced to trim excess capacity and match needed quantity. (Ohno, 1988, p. 19)

This system aims to eliminate breathing time for workers - the Modern Times scenario of workers unable to wave away flies or scratch itches becomes reality.

#### Problem 2: Contradictions from Slowing the Line (Pages 285-286)

Capital normally maximizes line pace for highest labor intensity/productivity. When demand falls (economic crisis or other reasons), slowing the line becomes necessary to avoid unsold inventory accumulation.

In classical assembly lines, slowing creates idle time at every work station. Rational capital response: lay off workers and redistribute remaining workforce. But classical system difficulty: workers specialize in narrow task spectrums, making substitution difficult.

Lean production solution: worker replaceability solves this idle time problem.

#### Problem 3: Impossibility of Using Worker Experience and Knowledge (Page 286)

Taylorism expropriates worker production knowledge, gathering all design/knowledge on management's side, reducing craft (skilled) labor to simple labor.

However, workers' experience-born knowledge doesn't reduce to zero post-expropriation. This residual knowledge: (1) gives workers power vis-à-vis management, and (2) squanders potential for more rational production organization from capital's viewpoint (management remains alien to this knowledge).

#### Problem 4: Waste (Pages 286-287)

Classical system: all work stations maintain reserve stocks of pieces/inputs to prevent upstream glitches from stopping work - the **JIC (just-in-case) system** (contrasted with JIT). These rarely-used stocks mean idle capital.

Additionally, quality control occurs separately after production, allowing defective production to continue unnoticed for extended periods.

Lean production eliminates both waste types.

### JIT - Just-In-Time System (Pages 287-289)

**JIT** = key term of late 20th/early 21st centuries. First utilized in Toyota, then Japanese companies broadly - the central concept of lean production. Must NOT be taken as purely technical/financial method.

**Common (limited) definition:** Production without inventory - saves inventory costs and relieves side effects. Counterposed to JIC. This reduces JIT to one dimension: fighting waste via eliminating excess idle inventory that raises capital costs.

**Partial truth in common view:** JIT vs. classical inventory production:
1. Eliminates warehouse storage costs
2. Avoids idle capital stocks storage in factory
3. Per Ohno: "Mountains of parts... might pile up at the later process. At that point, workers spent their time looking for storage space and hunting for parts instead of making progress... - production" (Ohno, 1988, p. 13)

Production without inventory eliminates these nuisances, increasing worker productivity. (Lean production factories are notably cleaner/neater.) Beyond the last factor, production without inventory contributes to profitability by reducing circulating constant capital value.

**Note on capital sphere:** Some authors (Sayer 1985, cited by Tomaney 1990, p. 34; Smith 1994, p. 12) present this as circulation sphere savings - WRONG. Lean production improves production cost, not turnover rate.

**The crux of JIT:** NOT mere constant capital cost economy. **The real function = labor intensification. JIT's actual purpose = identification and elimination of idle time.**

#### JIT Mechanism: Identification and Elimination of Surplus Labor (Pages 288-289)

When line runs fast, unable workers/stations use inventory pieces (if available). Without inventory, they become hard-pressed to catch up.

**Visualization system (andon):** Green/amber/red signal lights at each station.
- **Green** = normal operation
- **Amber** = partial problem
- **Red** = cannot cope; line stops

**Classical system:** Green light always desirable (line never stops).

**Lean production paradox: Green is bad!** Management deduces constant green = superfluous labor surplus at that station. Solution: heap more work on worker OR draw worker away to another station UNTIL worker/station reaches yellow-light limit. This balances line tempo/pace.

**Essence of lean production = management by stress/pressure** (Parker & Slaughter, 1988).

**Ohno's analogy:** Eight-man boat (four left, four right). Rowing incorrectly causes erratic zigzagging. One rower rowing twice as hard upsets progress. Best method: everyone distributes force equally, rowing evenly at same depth. (Ohno, 1988, p. 24)

Ohno makes perfectly clear: real objective = "balancing of the line."

Pressure doesn't stop after discovering superfluous surplus - **there is always superfluous surplus!** Forever increasing tempo is lean production's nature.

**Kaizen** (Japanese) = "continuous improvement" in business/quality literature = pressure forever increases to increase work intensity.

In Japan (system's origin country), workers arrive before scheduled hours or use coffee/rest breaks to precaution against future failures. Lean production techniques don't just remove idle time - **they create new "work time"!** The workday undergoes elongation beyond contract stipulation ("elasticity of the workday").

**Summary:** JIT far exceeds inventory cost savings. It's the method for discovering "points of labor surplus" in work stations and intensifying work through "superfluous labor" reallocation. NOT a technical matter, but a method directly related to labor management for extracting ever more labor from workers.

### Task Mobility and Interchangeability (Pages 289-290)

During continuous line balancing: (1) new tasks given to surplus-labor stations, OR (2) if surplus too large, workers drawn away. Additionally, general line slowdowns require layoffs, necessitating other workers performing laid-off workers' tasks.

All this requires **worker interchangeability**. The classical system's narrow detail division of labor outlook must broaden - workers must perform multiple tasks at different times, directly required by capital's rationality.

However, replacement alone could be solved easily in classical systems (detail division tasks learnable in days). Real problem: **How to assign new tasks to drawn-away workers?**

**Lean production answer:** Radically changed work station design - individual workers work at different machines.

**Original assembly line:** Work station = group of workers with same machine type performing same task together.

**Lean production:** Several different machines placed at U-form work stations. Workers directed to work other machines during idle time from central task, performing sub-assembly chores. **"Modular system"** = bringing together several machines.

Workers now: work several machines (not single machine), perform several motions endlessly (not one single motion). Additionally:
- **Maintenance functions** (previously separate workers) now expected from production workers (additional job + assumption of cleaner machine upkeep)
- **Quality control** no longer separate function - shouldered by production workers

Result: waste of labor avoided.

**Task mobility = method of increasing labor intensity through idle time reduction.**

Researcher comment:
> "The joining together of previously separated individual areas of responsibility in a team opens up the possibilities for reducing the sum of time lost. Or, expressed differently to achieve the highest possible ratio of time..."
