## Opening Statement (Page 265)

Late twentieth-century and early twenty-first-century debate witnessed the frequent use of certain terms in both theoretical discourse and daily political discussion as keys to the epoch in which we are living: post-Fordism, flexible specialisation, the Japanese system of production, Toyotism, lean production, flexible production, total quality control, team work, human resources management, flat organisation, etc. The intention motivating the use of all these terms was and is to affirm that wholesale change has come over the traditional factory and the workplace and that the work process is now entirely different from what it was in the past. But not only that. Although there may be differences of emphasis regarding the different phenomena mentioned, those who make constant reference to these terms usually imply that the nature of capitalist production itself has changed radically, that it has undergone an essential transformation, that the theoretical concepts and categories of yesteryear are no longer useful in understanding the problems faced by the working class in our day and age.

One finds widespread the idea, within these approaches, that the new order that is emerging has gained a dynamic that promises to eliminate contradictions between classes. The basic problems that the working class faced in the past in the sphere of production, such as deskilling, detail work (the image of the worker constantly turning the same kind of screw), division of mental and manual labour, hierarchy in the workplace, the gap between management and the worker, the alienation suffered by the worker, all these, we are told, are in the process of disappearing. It is claimed that the new order that obtains at the workplace will serve the common interest of all classes.

To sum up, a series of developments in the relations between capital and the working class in the recent period are being interpreted to imply that an entirely new age is opening up in class relations and that this is good for all classes. These views are usually those of the intellectuals of the ruling classes. However, there are many on the left and within trade unions who come under the spell of the new thinking.

Hence, it is incumbent on Marxist theory to grasp what changes are at work in the workplace and in industrial relations, how these changes should be situated within the overall context of the class system, and to assess whether all this is really in favour of the working class, as this new school of thinking claims or not.

## Response from the Working Class and Left (Pages 266-267)

These new developments are being met in a somewhat different mood among the advanced sections of the working class and the more radical wings of the left. One cannot really claim that there is a full-fledged literature form this quarter, but there is a widespread malaise both within the class and among the intellectuals of the left.

### First Problem: Lack of Theoretical Compass

The class struggle elements cannot really situate the dynamics at the basis of all these new trends, from Total Quality Management to the fragmentation of the workplace and from non-standard work arrangements (on call, etc.) to company anthems. Although it is beyond doubt that class contradictions are continuing, the advanced section of the class is hard-pressed to explain all these new practices, which create the appearance of a radical change in the way capitalism has functioned so far. Under these conditions these vanguard elements do not feel they are in possession of a reliable compass to guide their day-to-day action.

### Second Problem: Loss of Power

It is a very common impression within the advanced sections of the working class and among socialists that as a result of the application of these new methods, the power of the working class vis-à-vis capitalists has been shaken to its very roots. Some think that this kind of loss of power has weakened or even perhaps destroyed entirely the classical methods of class struggle. This leads either to despair and demoralisation or the understanding that old methods and instruments of struggle are no longer capable of achieving anything and should be replaced by new ones. Here reflection merges into impression, impression into emotion and emotion into intuition. It is very difficult to say that a fully scientific and rational discussion is taking place. It is as if the left is trying to find its way in a city where all street signs have been removed.

An attentive investigation into the changes in the workplace and in the field of industrial relations carries thus great importance, ideologically, politically, theoretically and from the practical point of view. This chapter is an attempt to shed light on this area through the use of the Marxist method.

## Chapter Scope and Objectives (Pages 267-268)

### Delimiting the Contours

Of course, one cannot take up all the diverse questions mentioned above in the space of a single chapter. Some of these concepts are so ideologically laden, let us hasten to add, that they even defy definition for their protagonists We should then carefully delimit the contours of this chapter.

### Three Objectives

**First Goal:** To explain as systematically as possible the new forms of domination, of work management and of labour process practices established by the capitalist class in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries and to bring out their inner connections and integral nature.

**Second Goal:** To show that these new forms of domination can be perfectly and adequately explained on the basis of the categories that have been developed by Marxism (starting with Marx himself) and by pursuing the logic established thereby. Two distinct points will have been established as an outcome of this second procedure:

1. The view, advanced in bourgeois theories about this period and in daily political discourse, that the new relations imply a fundamental change in the nature of capitalism and that this new setup cannot be comprehended within the framework of the old concepts and categories will have been refuted.

2. We will have demonstrated that the extreme worries witnessed within the advanced sections of the working class and the ranks of the left are really unfounded.

**Third Goal:** To show that the new relations, as a whole, have been devised as part and parcel of the grand offensive that the international capitalist class has launched on the working class since the 1980s. If this is true, the view propagated by bourgeois thinking that a new kind of harmony is in the process of replacing the conflict between the classes will have been rebutted. In effect, the chapter will try systematically to demonstrate that each new form aims at dividing the working class and divest it of its power so as to make it possible for capital to extract a growing amount of surplus-value in the context of the long-term depressive crisis that set in in the mid-1970s and put an end to what was known as the "long post-war boom".

## Limitations and Scope (Page 268)

### What This Chapter Does NOT Cover

Drawing attention to the limits of the chapter will help readers find their way more easily. This chapter consciously refrains from polemicising against bourgeois theories of the "new" relations between capital and the wage worker as well as those theories on the left side of the political spectrum that attempt to show these relations as either inevitable or beneficial to the working class. The reason for this is not only the fact that this is necessary in order to keep the chapter down to a manageable size.

Because our fundamental aim is to bring out what is veritably new within capitalism and grasp the class character of these novelties, we keep away from polemics to the extent possible.

On the other hand, the chapter restricts its scope to the depiction of a general picture of the relations between classes in these early decades of the twenty-first century. Yet we have already pointed out that there is another dimension to the debate on the left: the question of what is to be done in this new situation, of how to struggle, under these new conditions, for the interests of the workers. This is a vast field. We hope to take up this question in detail in the future.

To clarify an area in which ideology and capitalist class interest parade as social science and where sometimes even the finest minds are struck by confusion is a task that is worth one's while on its own. One single chapter cannot carry the burden of this additional task.

### Methodological Warning

Finally, a warning is in order. The effort to grasp the new forms and relations of capitalism on the basis of Marxist theory leads us to start the chapter by an exposition of the fundamentals of the Marxist theory in the sphere of production relations and the labour process. It is true that the theoretical framework laid out is no easy subject, but as Marx said in the preface he wrote specifically for the French edition of Capital, "there is no royal road to science". Let us then delve into these fundamental concepts and categories.

## Section 1: The Real Subsumption of Labour Under Capital (Pages 269-270)

### Opening Quote (Page 269)

> *A spider conducts operations which resemble those of the weaver, and a bee would put many a human architect to shame by the construction of its honeycomb cells. But what distinguishes the worst architect from the best of bees is that the architect builds the cell in his mind before he constructs it in wax. At the end of every labour process, a result emerges which had already been conceived by the worker at the beginning, hence already existed ideally.*
> — Karl Marx

### The Production Process of Capital

The process of production of capital, the very heart of the capitalist mode of production, is the dialectical unity of two different processes:

1. **Valorisation Process (Self-Expansion):** A process geared towards the expansion of capital by means of the additional value appropriated through the extraction of surplus-value from workers.

2. **Labour Process:** A process in which the subjective condition of production (human labour) is brought together with the objective conditions (means of production).

The production process of capital qua valorisation process comes about on the basis of relations that are peculiar to capitalism. In order for capital to expand itself, the objective conditions of production have to have been converted into capital and labour power, as the capacity of expending labour, into wage labour.

On the other hand, the labour process, to the extent that it is indispensable for the existence and survival of all human societies, is a category that is common to human history in general. However, in different modes of production, the labour process itself changes its form and character depending on the level of development of productive forces and the relations between the classes. However, capital infuses the labour process with an entirely different character that is radically different from what it was and changes it in a revolutionary manner.

### Evolution of Capital's Control

At the dawn of its rise, capital takes over the labour process in the form it finds it. Since it cannot change the productive forces of humanity and the manner of producing overnight, it has no other alternative. It moulds the labour process according to its own needs only over time. In other words, it puts the labour process in a form in which it becomes adapted to increasing surplus-value and thus the expansion of capital at the highest pace possible.

Only when the labour process is thus reshaped can capital be considered to be standing on its own feet. Only when the labour process, in other words, has been branded with the logic of the valorisation process of capital can we speak of the rise to dominance of the specifically capitalist mode of production, not only in the sense of being based on the relation between capital and wage labour, but also in the sense of the labour process now coming under the control of capital.

### Formal vs. Real Subsumption

**Formal Subsumption of Labour Under Capital:** Marx calls the stage in which capital makes use of the labour process as it finds it when it still has not moulded that process in line with its own needs the "formal subsumption of labour under capital". Here the concept "form" shows that the domination of capital is limited to the level of social relations peculiar to this society.

**Real Subsumption of Labour Under Capital:** When capital has succeeded to shape the labour process according to its own needs, a new stage has been reached, named by Marx the "real subsumption of labour under capital". The concept "real" here implies that capital has now gone beyond the mere level of social relations peculiar to capitalism and subordinated to its own design labour in its relation to nature.

### Historical Development (Page 270)

The history of the labour process and the capital-labour relation within the labour process is the history of the construction of real subsumption, that is to say, the subordination of labour by capital step by step to itself. We will see throughout this chapter that capital has veritably appropriated the production process, which is the primary relationship of humanity to nature.

The production process, which is controlled by labour at the dawn of capitalism and which capital can only try to shape from the outside, is now controlled by capital in a direct manner and from within. Naturally, this is not a type of control that is one-sided and without contradiction and friction, in which capital imposes its will as if in a vacuum, doing whatever it wishes to do. ## Key Concepts Introduced

1. **Post-Fordism and Related Terms:** Lean production, flexible specialization, Toyotism, total quality control, team work, human resources management, flat organization
2. **Formal Subsumption:** Capital controls through social relations but hasn't reshaped the labor process
3. **Real Subsumption:** Capital reshapes the labor process itself to maximize surplus-value extraction
4. **Valorisation Process:** The self-expansion of capital through surplus-value
5. **Labour Process:** The bringing together of human labor with means of production
6. **Dialectical Unity:** The production process combines valorisation and labor process

## Theoretical Framework

The chapter establishes that understanding contemporary workplace changes requires:
- Marxist categories remain valid and applicable
- New forms represent deepening of capitalist control, not transcendence
- Changes are part of capital's offensive since 1980s, not class harmony
- Real subsumption represents capital's appropriation of labor's relation to nature
