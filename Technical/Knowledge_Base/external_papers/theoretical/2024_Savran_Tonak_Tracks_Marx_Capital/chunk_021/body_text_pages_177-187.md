## Page 178: Chapter 8 - Section 3: CONCLUSION

This article presents POA as one of the sources of profit in capitalist economies. In addition to identifying and situating POA among other profit flows, we have attempted to operationalize it in the real estate and finance sectors. In the empirical part of the article, we estimate POA in those sectors in Turkey for the period 2010–2019. As indicated earlier, the extent of estimated profits based on POA in those sectors is not insignificant. These findings confirm our position that utilising POA in exploring capitalist dynamics would be a fruitful endeavour. Moreover, the recent privatisation activities of the current government and its favouritism towards ideologically close capitalist groups will make POA-based empirical investigations more relevant.

Research on the existence of potential POA in Turkey may be extended towards multiple venues. For example, current legislation allows the Ministry of Science and Technology to allocate almost free land for investment purposes in three cases: (1) regional development, (2) priority sector investments and (3) strategic sector investments. We can treat free public land transfers to capitalist firms as another source of POA. A vast tract of public land in Gemlik was recently allocated to TOGG (Türkiye Otomobil Girişim Grubu), a special favour for business.[^18] Large amounts of land may be transferred to other firms in the same way. Because of the fact that those firms have the possibility of reselling the land to a third party in the future, they may earn POA. Investors that locate their operations in "industrial zones," in addition to receiving many other incentives including tax breaks and subsidised credit, may have access to tracts of public land at rock-bottom prices. In the last two decades the total land allocated for industrial zones has reached almost 24 thousand hectares, 240 million square metres. Even assigning a very low figure of 1000 TL per square metre in current 2023 prices, the approximate total value of such land would be 240 billion TL.[^19]

[^18]: TOGG (Türkiye'nin Otomobili Girişim Grubu) is a Turkish automotive manufacturer of electric cars headquartered in Gebze, Kocaeli Province, Turkey. The company was founded as a joint venture by five Turkish companies in 2018 (see erdoganin-besbabayi̇gidine-hediye-edildi-7230).

[^19]: Obviously, we do not infer that the estimated total value is all POA, but given the fact that the sale prices are rock-bottom in the industrial zones, a big portion of that total value can be inferred as a potential source of POA.

## Page 179: Chapter 8 - Conclusion (Continued)

As in many developing capitalist economies, a large informal sector exists in Turkey where petty commodity producers are organised as family-owned micro firms. Those firms mainly depend on working capital from the large capitalist firms with whom they have an outsourcing relationship. In most cases, the interest rate charged for such borrowed capital is even greater than the standard lending rate by the banks. This is also a case in which a relatively large POA might exist.

Regarding the empirical estimation of POA, we suggest additional refinement to make our initial findings more robust. First, better methods of calculating specific prices can be explored. In the article, we use province-based averages. Locating the specific plots sold by the public authorities would make estimations more accurate.

A different method of calculating public prices from the one we use in the article based on the Milli Emlak database may be pursued. Since there is a time lag between the announcement of public land sales and the completion of the purchase, it is best to individually calculate the public price for each piece of plot sold rather than relying on the annual reports of DGNP. Lastly, incorporating the share of the total worker loans in "overhead costs" into the POA calculations may be considered. However, this refinement may not be entirely plausible as the balance sheets of each bank operating in Turkey are not accessible. However, considering the top eight to ten banks in BIST (Stock Exchange) would be sufficient to get a representative estimation.

The specific instances of POA generation that we have taken up in this article are of universal relevance. Land privatisation is not at all peculiar to emerging countries or less-developed countries. Nonnegligible amounts exist in advanced countries and probably the highest in formerly centrally planned economies, where urban land in particular had been nationalised totally. As for bank loans to households, advanced economies would by far surpass emerging economies such as Turkey in this area, since debt-driven consumerism is so much more a structural characteristic of those economies. In this sense, reflection on POA at a conceptual level is relevant to all economies.[^20]

### Section 1: The Concept of Exploitation

Before we delve into the discussion, it would be fruitful to dwell on the concept of exploitation. As a concept, exploitation has been a mainstay of political discourse and academic discussion widely, in particular in work that emanates from empirical sociology in the United States. Our use of the concept is more specific, geared towards the aim of making the Marxist concept of exploitation functional. People subscribe to socialism on the basis of the belief that capitalism is a social order that is unjust through and through, which itself derives from the material fact of the exploitation of workers by capitalists. Exploitation immediately recalls to mind the production of surplus-value and its appropriation by the capitalist class. It is natural to find that once the argument moves from this level of generality into grasping more concrete phenomena that one should confront problems both of a theoretical and empirical nature.

Marx's answer to this question is the theory of surplus-value, which he put forth as his own discovery without recourse to excessive modesty. The most general and abstract form of this theory was developed in *Capital*, Volume One. As is widely known, the first hundred or so pages of *Capital* are devoted to the systematic working out of concepts such as the commodity, value, exchange-value, labour, labour power, etc. It is only thanks to these concepts that a theoretical basis for a well-calibrated discussion of surplus-value is made possible.

In this article, we shall not undertake the task of defining these concepts. Nor will we go into a detailed and technical discussion of the theory of surplus-value based on these concepts. We will only provide a definition of surplus-value (and surplus time) and proceed to the distinction productive and unproductive labour (or activity), which distinction we shall abbreviate as PUPL.

To put it in another way, the worker, having worked in the first half of the 8-hour working day to create value sufficient for the reproduction of a value magnitude (equivalent to his or her wages), by continuing to work as a result of the labour contract he or she has signed with the capitalist, makes it possible for the latter to pocket the extra value ("surplus-value") thus created (through the utilisation of the use-value of labour power, i.e. of the power of that activity which creates all value). It is this latter portion of the working day (4 hours) that is the surplus labour time that forms the basis of the material source of the surplus-value produced within the activity of production.

Because the capitalist has appropriated this extra value created by the worker, the capitalist has exploited the worker. In this specific instance, the rate of exploitation is 100 per cent (4 hours/4 hours). Marx calls this the rate of surplus-value as well as the rate of exploitation.

A full comprehension of the distinction productive and unproductive labour requires clarity regarding the constitutive concepts labour and production. Let us start from the simpler one: the concept *activity of expending labour*, as in the sense in which it is used here, stands out as one of the foremost activities within social reproduction. In that sense, because it is conceived here as a part of the process of social reproduction, it takes on a much more specific meaning than the mere exertion of physical effort.
