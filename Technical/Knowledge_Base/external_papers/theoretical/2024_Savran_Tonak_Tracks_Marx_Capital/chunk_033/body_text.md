## Historical Context: Japanese Capital's Response to Western Competition

Western capital felt the threat of Japanese competition only in the 1980s (or perhaps the 1970s). Japan, however, had always felt the oppressive competition of the West since the 1860s, when capitalism made its first leap forward in this country. This persisted after the devastation of World War II.

Japanese capital passed the period approximately between 1945 and 1975 trying to catch up and cope with Western and particularly US capitalism, given the difference in productivity between them and its own industry. This is the most immediate driving force behind lean production.

### Ohno's Perspective: "Catching up with America"

Taiichi Ohno describes the motivation behind the Toyota production system:

> "Once I heard a man say that a German worker could produce three times as much as a Japanese worker. The ratio between German and American workers was 1-to-3. This made the ratio between Japanese and American work forces 1-to-9. I still remember my surprise at hearing that it took nine Japanese to do the job of one American. … But could an American really exert ten times more physical effort? Surely, Japanese people were wasting something. If we could eliminate the waste, productivity should rise by a factor of ten. This idea marked the start of the present Toyota production system."

What Ohno means by eliminating "waste" is to have workers work more by reducing idle time. What set Japanese capital in motion was the competition of US capital. But when Japanese capital did move, what it did was attack Japanese workers and develop techniques that would increase the rate of exploitation. From the point of view of the individual capital, it is competition that makes compliance with the general laws of capital an imperative. Here the law in question is to increase the amount of surplus-value produced.

### The Spread of Lean Production

The spread of lean production occurred in the context of a world market tightened by the general economic crisis in the mid-1970s and thus in a situation of increasing competition between countries and capitals. It was due to the need of US and European capital, now left behind Japanese capital, to adopt lean production as a technique that raises the rate of surplus-value. During this fratricidal war of individual capitals, the real aim is to raise the rate of surplus-value, and to atomise the working class so that its gains can be taken back after the post-war period which was rather more favorable to the working class.

### Conclusion on Lean Production's Role

**Lean production is a component part of the general strategy of assault that international capital has been implementing since the early 1980s in order to shift the burden of the worldwide general economic crisis on the shoulders of the working class and the broad masses of the labouring population.**

The elements of this general strategy consist of:
- Neoliberal economic policy ("the free market")
- Deregulation
- The privatisation of state- and municipal-owned enterprises
- The privatisation and commodification of social services
- De-unionisation
- Policies of flexibility and lean production

## Lean Production and Increasing Surplus-Value

The fundamental aim of the capitalist in organizing the production process is the creation of surplus-value beyond the production of the equivalent of the value of variable capital, a sum that is paid to the worker as his or her wages.

### Two Methods of Surplus-Value Production

**1. Production of Absolute Surplus-Value**

Given the value of labour power, to increase total amount of labour expended by the worker. This takes two forms:

a) **Extending working time**: Struggles over the length of the working day and working week are struggles over the amount of surplus-value and the rate of surplus-value (the ratio of surplus-value to variable capital).

b) **Raising labour intensity**: If the worker works, within a definite span of time, at a higher tempo (higher pace), this means that in the same chronological time span he or she has expended more labour. Since what determines the value of commodities is the amount of labour socially necessary for each commodity, the more labour expended the more value produced. Given the wage paid to the worker, this will increase the amount and the rate of surplus-value.

**2. Production of Relative Surplus-Value**

Given the working time and labour intensity, this derives from the appropriation by the capitalist of a higher amount of the labour expended due to the fall in the value of variable capital. Since the value created by labour spent over a workday is divided into variable capital and surplus-value, to the extent that the value of variable capital falls, a greater part of the value produced will become surplus-value.

Relative surplus-value can be produced in two different manners:

a) **Pushing real wages down**: When real wages fall, naturally the amount of labour necessary for their production will also fall proportionately and thus the value of labour power. Although the flexibility of wages makes this a very convenient method in the short run, there are definite limits because it is not possible to push down real wages below the subsistence level (not only of the worker but also of the people the worker has to look after) for a long time.

b) **Increasing labour productivity**: This means producing each commodity in a shorter time span, with a lower amount of labour, and hence making each commodity cheaper. When the value of commodities falls, since all commodities will become cheaper, under the assumption of constant real wages, the value of variable capital will fall and thus the surplus-value produced and the rate of surplus-value will rise.

### How Lean Production Increases Both Forms of Surplus-Value

Lean production increases both absolute surplus-value production and relative surplus-value production compared to the classical Taylorist system.

#### Absolute Surplus-Value Production Under Lean Production

One of the basic characteristics of lean production is to use the worker's time more for the capitalist. This extends the workday, the work week and the work year, thereby provoking the rise of surplus-value.

In large monopoly corporations or corporations that implement lean production techniques, it is clear that by:
- Coming to work earlier in the morning
- Leaving work late in the evening
- Continuing to work in rest breaks
- Continuing to work during their annual leave period
- Doing a lot of unpaid overtime work

Workers contribute to the production of surplus-value. In those companies and countries that implement lean production techniques, working time is systematically longer than in other companies and countries.

In small and medium enterprises that are part of the supply chain system, in small workshops of the sweatshop type, in the homes where the putting-out system is applied, because the workers' collective is unorganised and defenceless, because there is less of a legal protection, etc., working times are much longer than both large companies and relative to the level society as a whole has reached on such questions. This makes possible an incredible amount of extra surplus-value produced.

#### Relative Surplus-Value Production Under Lean Production

Lean production equally contributes to the production of relative surplus-value. What is important here is not the contribution of new vanguard technologies in the recent period (these would anyway have been made use of whether lean production was implemented or not). What we need to dwell on is the contribution that lean production makes to the production of relative surplus-value through methods peculiar to itself.

**Impact on Wages**

The fact that lean production has divided the work collective both inside the workplace and between workplaces has surely reduced the level of wages necessary to pay to employ a certain number of workers. All research shows that the wages of:
- "Untypical" labour contracts (now entirely typical): term contracts, temp work, part-time, on call, etc.
- Workers employed by subcontractors

are systematically lower than the core of the working collective in large corporations. If we remember that wages are incomparably lower in productive units that are part of the supply chain than in the monopoly company, it becomes clear that, overall, capital has successfully brought down wages and thereby increased surplus-value extracted.

**Labour Productivity Increase from Restructuring**

To distinguish this increase in labour productivity from that of labour intensity (to be discussed shortly as the most important factor), consider two examples:

1. **JIT vs. JIC (Just-In-Time vs. Just-In-Case)**: If the previous system of JIC (production with inventory) led to the accumulation of inventory in work stations and consequent problems of clutter, to which the workers had to tend during some of their working time, and if by removing this clutter and mess, JIT (alongside the many other advantages it creates for capital) makes it possible for the workers to devote their time more and more to production, this means there is an increase in productivity.

2. **Quality Supervision**: In the old system the job of quality supervision was done at the end of the production process by operatives other than production workers, which led to the discovery of a very high number of defective and faulty products or pieces. Even if we assume that all the defects could be repaired and the pieces in question set right (in that case there will be no wasting of constant capital, for instance in the form of wasted raw materials), the labour that had been expended for the defective and faulty products and pieces is being expended all over again during this rectification process, which obviously should be considered an important source of waste of production labour. The task mobility system, which stipulates the performance of the quality supervision by the production worker, to the extent that it converges on the "zero defect" goal (of course this is just a goal and 100% zero defect can never be attained), represents the elimination of a waste of productive labour and is again an improvement in labour productivity.

In neither case is there an assumption that the worker works faster, at a higher pace than before, which means there is no increase in labour intensity. The worker is simply producing more usable goods at the same pace and during the same time span as before. This clearly means that these two cases are improvements in labour productivity in the pure sense of the term. To the extent that the increase in labour productivity spreads to the social level, it reduces the value of the variable capital, and increases the amount and rate of surplus-value produced.

#### The Most Important Method: Increasing Labour Intensity

All the other methods, however important they may be, are all secondary. **Lean production is a method that tries to meet capital's voracity for surplus-value by constantly pushing up the pace of work for the worker and forcing the limits of what is humanly possible in terms of the pace of work.**

At the basis of this production system lies the goal of eliminating each single moment that is a loss for the production of surplus-value. **Stress** or **the balancing of the line through pressure** has been devised precisely so as to get rid of these unused moments. This dynamic always replicates itself at a higher level.

Behind **Kaizen** or the concept of "continuous improvement" lies capital's ambition to avoid missing even the smallest particle of the worker's labour. **Task mobility**, or, in other words, the innovation involving the working of each worker with more than one machine as well as taking care of maintenance and quality control, aims to gobble up all those moments that would otherwise have remained unreachable for capital.

**This is the real objective of production without inventory: to fulfil a production quota with as few workers involved as possible, at a level that forces all limits up until the end.**

### Summary Assessment

If this is true, then lean production is, in this stage of the development of world capitalism where the system is going through a general economic crisis, a successful initiative from the point of the bourgeoisie that meets the objective of increasing the production of surplus-value through a method of control of business management and of the labour process.

## The Flexibility Onslaught

Perhaps the most commonly circulating term in economic and business life in recent times is "flexibility". "Globalisation" is certainly gone, but "flexibility" seems to be here to stay. A great majority attribute a positive import to the concept.

### Classification of Flexibility Types

The most appropriate method of dealing with flexibility in a systematic manner is classifying the types of flexibility that the capitalist class strives to promote according to their relation to the production process and the circulation process. In other words, the two kinds of flexibility—the one the capitalists feel the need for when organizing lean production and try to institute—should be analysed separately from the forms of flexibility the same class wishes to impose upon the sale and purchase of labour power, or, in other words, in the labour market.

The most important benefit: from the point of view of the working class, what needs to be done in order to limit and eventually repel the flexibilities relating to production should focus mostly on the workplace while fighting the flexibilities concerned with the sale and purchase of labour power should concentrate on struggles at the level of society at large.

### Flexibilities Related to Lean Production (Production Process)

The types of flexibility that are related to lean production can be classified into four categories:

**1. Functional Flexibility**

The two preconditions of the flexibility that is needed for the identification of the superfluous surplus labour at work stations and processes and to reallocate it around the factory or within the work station have already been singled out: **Task mobility** and **interchangeability**.

**2. Time Flexibility**

Under lean production, the frontier between worktime and leisure becomes fuzzy, with the former encroaching upon the latter in various forms. The most important factors that create this "flexibility" are obviously production without inventory and TQM. Human resources management may also be added.

**3. Supply Flexibility**

Monopoly capital has great room for manoeuvre vis-à-vis its suppliers:
- It has a choice of different suppliers
- It has the possibility of having each piece produced by the bidder with the lowest price (offshoring to low-wage producers in faraway countries)
- In certain industries, this has even led to the formation of a new branch of activity called "supply chain management", for the purpose of coordinating the relations between the main corporation and the innumerable small enterprises that are scattered all around the world

Even if the monopoly corporation is working with a single supplier, that tête-a-tête between the main company and the supplier implies flexibility from the angle of the corporation and rigidity from that of the supplier.

### Flexibilities in the Labour Market (Circulation Sphere)

In the sphere of circulation, that is to say in the sale and purchase of labour power or, in other words, in the labour market, the forms of flexibility may be enumerated as follows:

**1. Flexible Employment**

As an overall outcome of long social struggles, standard forms of employment (permanent contract, compliance with the legal workweek, etc.) had become the norm to differing extent in different types of countries up until the 1970s. This was certainly not an absolute rule but tendentially true nonetheless. However, within the flexibility onslaught of recent decades non-standard employment forms such as temp work, part-time work, term contract, etc. have made a big leap forward. **Contingent work is living its heyday.**

**2. Numerical Flexibility**

What makes possible to reduce the number of workers when demand sags is the creation of a peripheral belt on temporary labour contracts. To this should be added the fact that in countries where lean production is being implemented abundantly even the core fraction of the workers do not have much of a legal guarantee with respect to job security.

Among the advanced countries:
- The United States is in the forefront of countries with scant job security
- In Japan, although the core worker enjoys a substantial level of job security thanks to the nengko system, the law recognises the powers of the employer to terminate the labour contract whenever it deems this necessary

**3. Flexibility of Labour Contract**

Whereas in the past workers who worked for the same factory or workplace worked there as the members of a single collective worker, today work is being separated into different categories and, starting from services that are auxiliary to the main work of the company such as security, cleaning, food, etc., many tasks are being turned over to subcontracting companies, leading to divisions within the work collective. Thus, it is the capitalist who flexibly decides what conditions (directly or mediated by the subcontractor) will determine the relationship between the main company and elements of the work collective.

**4. Wage Flexibility**

In order to divide the work collective, lean production also tries to create considerable differentiation among the workers in terms of wages and salaries. This requires the periodic reassessment of the payment to the worker according to criteria such as:
- The degree of dedication to work
- The level of allegiance to the company
- Submissiveness, etc.

A high level of bonuses within the remuneration package is very handy for management. In the Japanese system, the share of bonuses can rise all the way to 40% of overall remuneration. It is no wonder then that research indicates that the country with the highest wage flexibility among advanced countries is Japan.

**5. Flexibility of Social Services and Rights**

Within the historical development of capitalism, class struggle, on the one hand, and the instinct of protecting the system against countries where capitalism has been abolished, on the other, led to the provision of many services free of charge by the state (what passes off as a "welfare state" in ideological terms). However, in recent decades, the international bourgeoisie, following in the footsteps of the United States where this aspect was always less advanced than other countries, is truncating these services one by one and recommodifying them.

The obverse is the offering of some of these services by companies as a component of the wage bill. This is a system that divides the working class across enterprises and forces the workers of each enterprise towards allegiance to their own company.

The most aggressive form of flexibility in this area is the so-called **"cafeteria" system** implemented by some companies in the United States, in which each individual is remunerated "according to need". It is clear that all of this has but one overriding purpose: **to divide the working class or in even stronger language to atomise it**.

## Is Flexibility Positive?

It is easily comprehensible that bourgeois ideologues should present flexibility in a positive light since it serves the needs of capital. It is incomprehensible, on the other hand, that people active in unions and people on the left of the political spectrum should attribute a positive value to what is in fact a malediction for the working classes. The word itself in its connotations is of some assistance to these muddle-headed defenders of lofty causes. The opposite of flexibility is, after all, rigidity. Who wants rigidity?

### Creating Elementary Lucidity

In this murky twilight zone where the meaning of a word in other contexts is abused to confuse people, let us first try to create some elementary lucidity.

**First Question: Flexibility for whom?**

The answer to this question is striking: **In an overwhelming majority of the cases, the flexibility in question is a one-way relationship: It is always the capitalist who enjoys the flexibility.**

### Case Study 1: Numerical Flexibility

There is no disagreement on the meaning of the term: it means quite simply that the company can adjust and regulate the size of the workforce in response to changes in demand.

An important aspect: no regime of industrial relations historically speaking has ever put an obstacle in the way of companies in hiring new workers when demand becomes higher than before. That would have been ludicrous since it would have contravened all social rationality. Hence, what we are talking about in terms of flexibility here is not the recruitment of new workers when demand increases but **redundancies in the case of a fall in demand**.

This way we have identified the substance of this form of flexibility with great precision: **This is simply freedom for the company to lay off workers without facing any obstacles.**

Now the crucial question: what kind of flexibility is this for the worker? Even common sense tells us that the summary layoff of the worker, when considered from the point of view of the worker, is simply gross ruthlessness. This means that anyone who regards numerical flexibility in a positive light is taking the point of view of the capitalist. Since this judgement is marked by the prejudices of a certain social class, we equally rightfully declare from the viewpoint of the other class in question, the working class, that it has very negative consequences.

### Case Study 2: Time Flexibility (Annualisation of Working Time)

Let us take a different example from the field of time flexibility: the annualisation of the calculation of working time.

**The capitalist's need**: the calculation of having workers work short hours when demand is low and long, indeed very long hours when demand is high.

**What is in it for the worker?**

**Short hours**: Who would not want to work short hours at some point in life? But is the worker choosing the moment of short hours on his or her own will? Of course not. **There is no flexibility whatsoever here from the point of view of the worker.**

**Long or very long hours**: This is doubly negative for worker:
1. The very long hours demanded of the worker are deleterious for the health of the worker
2. If working time had been calculated on a weekly basis rather than arbitrary allocation of the overall time throughout the year by the company, the worker would have received a lot of overtime pay, whereas now there will be no such pay
