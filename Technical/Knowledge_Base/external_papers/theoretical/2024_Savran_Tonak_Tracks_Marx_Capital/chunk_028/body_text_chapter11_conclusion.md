When we compare Germany with the United States and Spain, we see that the Motor Vehicles, Health, Food and Machinery sectors have continued to be the most central sectors in the German economy in the 1995–2005 period. These sectors are much more robust in facing the global crisis in the sense that they are tradeable and thus the German economy could insulate itself from the devastation of the global crisis compared to the US economy thanks to the export capacity of these central sectors.

In contrast, the Spanish economy hosted Construction, Trade and Hotels-Restaurant as the most central sectors and these sectors dramatically collapsed due to the adverse effects of the global crisis. The United States, on the other hand, suffered more from the extraction of surplus-value from the productive sectors by the ever-expanding unproductive sectors.

## 6 Conclusion

The Marxian distinction between production and non-production activities is essential to understanding the dynamics of capitalism. Network analysis of I-O tables reconstructed in a Marxian framework reveals different and important patterns across countries.

From the classical Marxist point of view, surplus-value production is the very basis of the existence and expansion of unproductive economic activities such as finance and trade. It was inevitable that at some point there could be a shortage of appropriated surplus-value to support the ever-growing trade and finance. This underlying structural contradictory situation could have manifested itself by any triggering phenomenon.

## Page 250

We have used network analysis to uncover the structural differences among the United States, Germany and Spain in the last decade preceding the global crisis both in the conventional and in the Marxian framework. These structural differences reflect the extent of interdependencies among sectors, which in turn lead to different rankings of sectors in terms of the counting betweenness centrality measure.

We find that United States had vulnerabilities as it had mostly unproductive sectors as top-ranking sectors, which means that potentially a shortage of appropriated surplus-value to support these sectors would be more likely.

In Germany, the top-ranking sectors were both productive and tradeable. Thus, Germany was the least affected. However, in Spain the dominance of Construction as well as the high-ranking non-tradeable sectors have made the effects of the crisis more dramatic.

Although this study does not directly address the relations between productive and non-productive sectors, it has attempted to determine sectoral centrality in terms of the economy as a whole by focusing on the changes experienced in productive sectors. Therefore, it should be seen as the first study that draws attention to the crisis-triggering mechanisms inherent in the real sector, both in today's crisis and in the future, by determining which sectors stand out in terms of centrality.

In feature research we will extend our analysis to cover developing economies and trace the structural transformations in these economies both before and after the global crisis.
