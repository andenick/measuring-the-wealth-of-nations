## 1. John Ruskin - Critique of Division of Labour

**Source**: *The Stones of Venice*, Section II, Chapter 6
**Cited by**: Harry Braverman (1998, 78–79n)
**Location**: Pages 292-293 (printed 272-273)
**Topic**: Human degradation under division of labour

### Full Quotation:

> We have much studied and perfected, of late, the great civilised invention of the division of labour; only we give it a false name. It is not, truly speaking, the labour that is divided; but the men: divided into mere segments of men-broken into small fragments and crumbs of life; so that all the little piece of intelligence that is left in a man is not enough to make a pin, or a nail, but exhausts itself in making the point of a pin, or the head of a nail. Now it is a good and desirable thing, truly, to make many pins in a day; but if we could only see with what crystal sand their points were polished-sand of human soul, much to be magnified before it can be discerned for what it is---we should think there might be some loss in it also. And the great cry that rises from all our manufacturing cities, louder than the furnace blast, is all in very deed for this---that we manufacture everything there except men ...

### Significance:
Poetic and moral critique of how division of labour fragments human beings. Ruskin uses the same pin factory example as Adam Smith but reaches opposite conclusion: technical efficiency comes at cost of human dignity and wholeness. Note the phrase "we divide not the labour but the men" - emphasizes dehumanization over productivity gains.

## 2. Adam Smith - Defense of Division of Labour

**Source**: *Wealth of Nations* (1977 edition, pp. 21–22) [Originally 1776]
**Location**: Page 293 (printed 273)
**Topic**: Productivity gains from division of labour

### Full Quotation:

> This great increase of the quantity of work which, in consequence of the division of labour, the same number of people are capable of performing, is owing to three different circumstances; first, to the increase of dexterity in every particular workman; secondly, to the saving of the time which is commonly lost in passing from one species of work to another; and lastly, to the invention of a great number of machines which facilitate and abridge labour, and enable one man to do the work of many.

### Significance:
Classical political economy's positive assessment of division of labour. Smith identifies three mechanisms: (1) increased dexterity through specialization, (2) time saved by not switching tasks, (3) facilitation of mechanization. Contrast with Ruskin's critique. Note Smith also uses pin factory example - became canonical illustration in economics.

## 3. Charles Babbage - The "Babbage Principle"

**Source**: Babbage (1963 [1832], pp. 175–176)
**Quoted by**: Braverman (1974, 79–80)
**Location**: Page 294 (printed 274)
**Topic**: Economic rationale for division of labour beyond technical efficiency

### Full Quotation:

> Now, although all these are important causes, and each has its influence on the result; yet it appears to me, that any explanation of the cheapness of manufactured articles, as consequent upon the division of labour, would be incomplete if the following principle were omitted to be stated.
>
> That the master manufacturer, by dividing the work to be executed into different processes, each requiring different degrees of skill or of force, can purchase exactly that precise quantity of both which is necessary for each process; whereas, if the whole work were executed by one workman, that person must possess sufficient skill to perform the most difficult, and sufficient strength to execute the most laborious, of the operations into which the art is divided.

### Significance:
Exposes class interest dimension of division of labour. Even if productivity were equal, capitalists benefit by employing cheap unskilled labour for fragmented tasks instead of expensive skilled workers for complete jobs. Makes explicit that division serves valorization, not just technical efficiency. "It transpires in naked form that it is not a question of the increase in the productive forces of humanity, but one that is intimately tied with the class interest of the capitalist."

## 4. Karl Marx - Machinery and Real Subsumption

**Source**: Marx (1976, pp. 548–549) [*Capital*, Vol. 1]
**Location**: Page 295 (printed 275)
**Topic**: How machinery completes the inversion of worker-tool relationship

### Full Quotation:

> Every kind of capitalist production, in so far as it is not only a labour process but also capital's process of valorization, has this in common, that it is not the worker who employs the conditions of his work, but rather the reverse, the conditions of work employ the worker. However, it is only with the coming of machinery that this inversion first acquires a technical and palpable reality. Owing to its conversion into an automaton, the instrument of labour confronts the worker during the labour process in the shape of capital, dead labour, which dominates and soaks up living labour-power. The separation of the intellectual faculties of the production process from manual labour, and the transformation of those faculties into powers exercised by capital over labour, is, as we have already shown, finally completed by large-scale industry erected on the foundation of machinery.

### Significance:
Central theoretical statement on real subsumption. Marx argues:
1. All capitalist production inverts normal tool-use relationship
2. With machinery, this inversion becomes "technical and palpable" (not just social)
3. Machines embody "dead labour" (past labour congealed in capital)
4. Dead labour "dominates and soaks up" living labour-power
5. Design/execution separation "finally completed" by machinery

Note connection to later Taylorism discussion: machinery establishes technical basis, Taylorism systematizes and consolidates.

## 5. Frederick W. Taylor - "Systematic Soldiering"

**Source**: Taylor (2005, p. 13); also quoted in Braverman (1998, p. 68)
**Location**: Page 297 (printed 277)
**Topic**: Workers' resistance through control over work pace

### Full Quotation:

> The greater part of systematic soldiering ... is done by the men with the deliberate object of keeping their employers ignorant of how fast work can be done. So universal is soldiering for this purpose, that hardly a competent workman can be found in a large establishment, whether he works by the day or on piece work, contract work or under any of the ordinary systems of compensating labor, who does not devote a considerable part of his time to studying just how slowly he can work and still convince his employer that he is going at a good pace.

### Significance:
Taylor's frank acknowledgment of class struggle over control of labour process. Workers use their knowledge and control to resist intensification. Key points:
1. "Systematic" soldiering - organized, deliberate resistance
2. "Competent workman" - skilled workers have most control
3. Universal practice across payment systems
4. Workers study how to maintain appearance while slowing pace

This diagnosis becomes rationale for entire Taylorist project: wrest control from workers to eliminate resistance at its root. Taylor "knew very well that to the extent that the worker can exercise control over the job, he or she will invent a host of methods to slow down the work tempo."

## 6. Frederick W. Taylor - Planning Department Concentration

**Source**: Quoted by Braverman (1998, p. 88)
**Location**: Page 299 (printed 279)
**Topic**: Transfer of "brainwork" from workers to management

### Full Quotation:

> It must be borne in mind, however, that, with the exception of the study of unit times, there is hardly a single item of work done in the planning department which is not already being done in the shop. Establishing a planning department merely concentrates the planning and much other brainwork in a few men especially fitted for their task and trained in their especial lines, instead of having it done, as heretofore, in most cases by high priced mechanics, well fitted to work at their trades, but poorly trained for work more or less clerical in its nature.

### Significance:
Taylor's candid admission that Taylorism doesn't create new work but transfers existing work from workers to management. Key admissions:
1. Planning work was already being done - by workers themselves
2. "Concentration" is the change, not innovation
3. Transfer from "high priced mechanics" to planning specialists
4. Subtly denigrates workers' planning ability ("poorly trained for... clerical... work")

Shows that Taylorism is fundamentally about control and deskilling, not technical progress. "As can be seen, the 'principles' of Taylor... do not create any change in the substance of the work done, but simply end up passing the control over the labour process from the worker to the capitalist."

## Summary of Quotation Functions

### Critical Tradition (Ruskin, Marx)
- Emphasize human cost and class domination
- Expose contradictions of capitalist progress
- Moral/theoretical critique

### Apologist Tradition (Smith, Babbage, Taylor)
- Defend capitalist innovations
- Acknowledge mechanisms transparently
- Inadvertently expose class interests

### Author's Strategy
Uses both traditions dialectically:
- Smith/Babbage/Taylor reveal class interests despite intentions
- Ruskin/Marx provide critical framework
- Juxtaposition exposes contradictions in capitalist self-justification

### Rhetorical Effect
Letting capitalist theorists speak for themselves ("in naked form," "in all frankness") gives critique more power than pure theoretical argument. Taylor especially damning in his own words.

## Thematic Connections Across Quotations

### Pin Factory Motif
- **Smith**: Pin factory as productivity triumph
- **Ruskin**: Same example as human tragedy
- **Babbage**: Pins as cost-reduction opportunity

**Effect**: Shows how same phenomenon can be read from capital's or labor's perspective.

### Control/Knowledge Theme
- **Marx**: Intellectual faculties separated from manual labor
- **Taylor #1**: Workers use knowledge to resist ("systematic soldiering")
- **Taylor #2**: Planning transferred from mechanics to management

**Arc**: Knowledge as terrain of class struggle; capitalist victory through knowledge expropriation.

### Inversion/Domination
- **Marx**: Conditions of work employ the worker; dead labour dominates living
- **Taylor**: Management must control to prevent resistance
- **Babbage**: Capitalist purchases labor as commodity input

**Theme**: Fundamental inversion of human-tool relationship under capitalism.

### Stages of Intensification
1. **Smith**: Division of labour increases productivity
2. **Babbage**: Division reduces costs via deskilling
3. **Marx**: Machinery establishes technical domination
4. **Taylor**: Scientific management consolidates total control

**Progression**: Each stage deepens real subsumption.

## Usage Notes for Researchers

### Citation Protocol
When citing these quotations:
1. Check original sources for full context
2. Note whether quoted directly from original or via intermediary (Braverman)
3. Maintain nested attribution (e.g., "Ruskin, cited in Braverman, quoted in Savran")

### Historical Context
- **Smith (1776)**: Pre-industrial revolution, manufacture stage
- **Ruskin (1850s-60s)**: Mid-Victorian critique, romantic anti-capitalism
- **Babbage (1832)**: Early industrial capitalism, mechanization spreading
- **Marx (1867)**: Mature industrial capitalism, theoretical synthesis
- **Taylor (1903-1911)**: Monopoly/imperialist capitalism, systematization

### Theoretical Lineages
- **Political Economy**: Smith → Babbage → Marx (critical appropriation)
- **Labor Process Theory**: Marx → Braverman → Savran (critical continuation)
- **Management Theory**: Taylor → Drucker (contested terrain)
- **Cultural Critique**: Ruskin → Chaplin (artistic resistance)
