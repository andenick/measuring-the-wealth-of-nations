# Key Concepts Glossary - Chunk 030 (Pages 291-300)

## A-D

### Babbage Principle
**Definition**: The principle articulated by Charles Babbage (1832) that detail division of labour allows capitalists to purchase only the precise quantity of skill needed for each fragmented task, thereby reducing costs by employing unskilled labour for most operations instead of skilled workers for the entire job.

**Source**: Page 293/273, Babbage (1963, 175-176)

**Significance**: Demonstrates that division of labour serves capitalist class interests directly, not just technical efficiency.

---

### Cooperation
**Definition**: The first stage of capitalist organization of labour, where many workers are brought together under one roof to work collectively, increasing productivity through coordination even before technological changes.

**Source**: Page 291/271

**Significance**: Initial stage of subsumption before detail division and machinery.

---

### Craft Worker/Craft Unionism
**Definition**: Skilled workers who possess the totality of knowledge necessary to produce a product; unions organized around specific skilled trades and professions.

**Source**: Pages 298/278, 300/280

**Significance**: Target of Taylorist deskilling; craft unions lost power as workers were deskilled in 20th century.

---

### Dead Labour vs. Living Labour
**Definition**: Marxist distinction between labour already congealed in machinery/capital (dead labour) and the active labour-power of workers (living labour). Under capitalism, dead labour dominates and "soaks up" living labour.

**Source**: Page 295/275 (Marx quote)

**Significance**: Describes the inversion where workers serve machines rather than machines serving workers.

---

### Deskilling
**Definition**: The systematic elimination of craft knowledge and skills through fragmentation of tasks and transfer of production knowledge to management. Workers reduced to simple, repetitive operations.

**Source**: Page 299/279 (first consequence of Taylorism)

**Significance**: Central consequence of Taylorism; transforms skilled craft workers into unskilled operatives.

---

### Design vs. Execution (Separation of)
**Definition**: The severing of the unity between planning/conceptualizing work (design) and actually performing it (execution). Under capitalism, design becomes management prerogative while workers only execute.

**Source**: Pages 292/272, 298/278, 299-300/279-280

**Significance**: Destroys "defining characteristic of the production activity of human beings"; second major consequence of Taylorism.

---

### Detail Division of Labour (Technical Division)
**Definition**: Capitalist innovation where each worker performs only a minute fragment of the overall production process, as opposed to completing an entire product. Breaking down of tasks into smallest possible units.

**Source**: Pages 292-295/272-275

**Contrast**: Social division of labour (different occupations in society)

**Examples**: Pin production (Smith, Ruskin, Babbage); worker endlessly tightening screws

**Significance**: "Severs the unity of the design and the execution phases of the work process"

---

## E-L

### Formal Subsumption (implied contrast)
**Definition**: Earlier stage where capital takes over existing labour processes without fundamentally transforming them; workers retain control over how work is done even while working for capital.

**Source**: Implied throughout by contrast with "real subsumption"

**Significance**: Precedes real subsumption; capital controls framework but not internal process.

---

### Imperialist Stage of Capitalism
**Definition**: Early 20th century stage characterized by monopoly capital, giant corporations spreading globally. Historical context for emergence of Taylorism.

**Source**: Pages 291/271, 296/276

**Significance**: Taylorism arises as management method appropriate to monopoly capital's large-scale production.

---

### Industrial Revolution
**Definition**: Late 18th century transformation marked by introduction of machinery into production, dramatically increasing productivity and establishing technical basis for real subsumption.

**Source**: Pages 291/271, 295/275

**Periodization**: "Second half of the eighteenth century"

**Geography**: Western Europe, particularly Britain

---

### Job Design
**Definition**: Taylorist practice of breaking down work operations into minute units and recombining them in ways determined by management, not workers. Planning function transferred to industrial engineering departments.

**Source**: Page 298/278

**Significance**: "Planning and thereby the control of the work has been totally wrested away from the worker and turned over to the capitalist."

---

### Labour Intensity
**Definition**: The pace, tempo, and exertion level of work; how hard workers work per unit time.

**Source**: Page 295/275

**Contrast**: Labour productivity (output per unit time)

**Significance**: Machinery and Taylorism increase both productivity AND intensity; intensity increases exploitation even without new technology.

---

### Labour Productivity
**Definition**: The quantity of output produced per unit of labour time; productive powers of labour.

**Source**: Pages 291/271, 295/275

**Significance**: Machinery increases productivity "by leaps and bounds"; distinct from but related to labour intensity.

---

## M-R

### Manufacture Stage
**Definition**: Stage of capitalist development before Industrial Revolution, characterized by detail division of labour but still using hand tools rather than machinery. Workshop production.

**Source**: Page 295/275

**Example**: Adam Smith's era pin factories

---

### Monopoly Capital
**Definition**: Large-scale corporate capitalism characterized by giant firms dominating markets; precondition for Taylorism due to economies of scale.

**Source**: Page 296/276

**Significance**: "The methods of Taylorism being quite expensive ones, these become feasible for the capitalist only at the level of large-scale production."

---

### Real Subsumption of Labour Under Capital
**Definition**: Stage where capital has fundamentally transformed the labour process itself to serve valorization needs. Control over production transferred from worker to capital through machinery, division of labour, and scientific management. Workers no longer control pace, methods, or planning of work.

**Source**: Throughout; pages 291/271, 295-296/275-276

**Periodization**: "Begins with the industrial revolution of late eighteenth century, advances step by step throughout the nineteenth century, and reaches a level of maturity in early twentieth century"

**Systematization**: "Taylorism... may be considered as the systematisation of the real subsumption of labour under capital" (page 291/271)

**Key marker**: "The worker is now an extension of the machine" (page 295/275)

---

## S-Z

### Scientific Management
**Definition**: Frederick Winslow Taylor's self-description of his management system; "high-sounding term" critiqued as ideological mystification of capital's control methods.

**Source**: Pages 296-298/276-278

**Critique**: "Was not concerned with technology" (Drucker); about control, not science

---

### Simple Labour (Unskilled Labour)
**Definition**: Labour that requires no special training or craft knowledge; undifferentiated labour-power. Result of deskilling process.

**Source**: Pages 293/273, 294/274, 298/278

**Contrast**: Skilled labour (craft workers with specialized knowledge)

**Significance**: Taylorism aims to reduce all labour to simple labour to increase capital's control and reduce costs.

---

### Skilled Labour
**Definition**: Labour requiring craft knowledge, training, and specialized abilities. Workers who control tempo and rhythm of work based on their expertise.

**Source**: Pages 293/273, 298/278

**Fate**: Target of deskilling under Taylorism; "competent workman" whose control must be eliminated.

---

### Social Division of Labour
**Definition**: Distribution of different types of work (occupations, professions) across society. Individuals perform complete activities but society divides functions (hunting, farming, artisanship, etc.).

**Source**: Page 292/272

**Contrast**: Detail/technical division (fragmentation within production process)

**Historical note**: Exists throughout history; capitalism creates detail division as something new.

---

### Systematic Soldiering
**Definition**: Taylor's term for workers' deliberate slowdown of work pace to maintain control over production and prevent employers from discovering maximum possible work rate.

**Source**: Page 297/277 (Taylor quote)

**Significance**: Form of workers' resistance that Taylorism aimed to eliminate by transferring control to management.

---

### Taylorism
**Definition**: Labour management and control system developed by Frederick Winslow Taylor in early 20th century. Systematizes real subsumption through time and motion studies, job design, and transfer of all production knowledge to management.

**Source**: Pages 296-300/276-280

**Alternative name**: Scientific Management

**Core works**:
- *Shop Management* (1903)
- *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911)

**Three Basic Principles** (page 298/278):
1. Transfer workers' production knowledge to management
2. Separate design from execution, give design to management
3. Ensure each labour step is planned by management and execution constantly supervised

**Method**: Time and motion studies

**Four Consequences** (pages 299-300/279-280):
1. Expropriation of production knowledge, deskilling
2. Fragmentation between design and execution
3. Divorce of worker from science
4. Loss of power for unions

**Precondition**: Monopoly capital, large-scale production

**Key feature**: "Accepts the level of technological development as given. It is not an outcome of new technologies being implemented" (page 296/276)

**Spread**: Extended beyond industry to white-collar work, telecommunications, banking, insurance, retail, government offices

---

### Time and Motion Studies
**Definition**: Taylorist method of breaking down worker operations into smallest possible details, assigning time spans to each unit, and determining total time for operations. Basis for transferring production knowledge to industrial engineering/planning departments.

**Source**: Page 298/278

**Pioneer**: Frederick Winslow Taylor

**Purpose**: Enable management to control every aspect of production process

---

### Valorisation/Valorization
**Definition**: Capital's process of self-expansion; creation and appropriation of surplus-value. The dominant process that moulds the labour process to serve capital's needs.

**Source**: Pages 291/271, 294/274, 295/275

**Relationship**: "The valorisation process of capital is moulding the labour process according to its own needs" (page 294/274)

---

**Quote cited**: Scientific Management "was not concerned with technology. Indeed, it took tools and techniques largely as given" (1992, 192-193)

**Significance**: Even pro-capitalist consultant acknowledges Taylorism is about control, not technology

### Marx, Karl (1818-1883)
**Work cited**: *Capital* Vol. 1 (1976 edition, pages 548-549)

**Topics**: Machinery, valorization, real subsumption, inversion of worker-tool relationship

**Key quote**: On machinery creating "technical and palpable reality" of capital's domination

---

### Ruskin, John (1819-1900)
**Work**: *The Stones of Venice*, Section II, Chapter 6

**Contribution**: Poetic critique of division of labour: "It is not, truly speaking, the labour that is divided; but the men"

**Famous phrase**: "We manufacture everything there except men"

**Tone**: Moral/aesthetic critique of industrial capitalism's effect on human dignity

---

### Smith, Adam (1723-1790)
**Work**: *Wealth of Nations* (1776/1977 edition)

**Contribution**: Pin factory example; three reasons for productivity increase from division of labour

**Pages cited**: 21-22

**Significance**: Classical political economy foundation; contrast with Ruskin's critique

---

### Taylor, Frederick Winslow (1856-1915)
**Background**: "Offspring of a rich family," renounced college to become factory apprentice, rose from worker to foreman to manager

**Trajectory**: "Passed to the other side of the fence" - used inside knowledge of workers' behavior against them

**Works**:
- *Shop Management* (1903)
- *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911)

**Goal**: "Turn the control over the job... to the capitalist and management"

**Self-description**: Planning department "merely concentrates the planning and much other brainwork"

**Significance**: Systematized real subsumption; namesake of Taylorism

## Conceptual Relationships

### Three Stages of Capital's Control (Sequential)
1. **Cooperation** → Many workers under one roof, basic supervision
2. **Detail Division of Labour** → Fragmentation of tasks, separation of design/execution begins
3. **Machinery** → Technical basis for controlling pace, real subsumption established
4. **Taylorism** → Systematization and consolidation of all previous developments

### Dual Effects of Machinery
1. **Labour Productivity** ↑ (more output per time)
2. **Labour Intensity** ↑ (faster pace, harder work)

**Both serve valorization**: More surplus-value extracted

### Taylorism's Targets and Effects
**Target**: Skilled craft workers' control over production

**Method**: Time and motion studies, job design, planning departments

**Effect**: Deskilling → simple labour → capital control → increased surplus-value

### Division of Labour Types
**Social**: Occupational distribution in society (always existed)
- Hunter, farmer, artisan each does complete activity
- Based on different people doing different whole jobs

**Detail/Technical**: Fragmentation within production (capitalism's innovation)
- Same job broken into fragments
- Different people do incomplete fragments
- Severs design from execution

## Analytical Framework Summary

This chunk deploys Marxist labor process theory to analyze the historical transformation from craft production to Taylorized scientific management as the consolidation of capital's real subsumption of labor. The analysis emphasizes:

1. **Class conflict** over control of production process
2. **Historical materialism** - stages of development
3. **Critique of ideology** - "Scientific" Management mystifies class domination
4. **Technology as social relation** - not neutral, serves valorization
5. **Worker resistance** - systematic soldiering, craft knowledge
6. **Degradation of labour** - deskilling, fragmentation, alienation

The theoretical apparatus draws primarily on Marx's *Capital* filtered through Braverman's modern labor process theory, with supporting evidence from classical political economy (Smith, Babbage) and cultural references (Ruskin, Chaplin).
