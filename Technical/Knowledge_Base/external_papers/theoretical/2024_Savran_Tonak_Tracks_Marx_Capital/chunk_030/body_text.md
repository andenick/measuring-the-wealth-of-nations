The establishment of the subsumption of labour under capital does not come at a single step but matures over various stages. Naturally, under the impact of the operation of the law of combined and uneven development the stages are different in different countries. In certain countries that come late to capitalism, it may not even be possible to distinguish between the different stages of development. But in the case of those countries of Western Europe where capitalism ascended for the first time on the historical stage it is possible to separate out these stages within the historical process. In Western Europe, and in particular in Britain, the growth of the real subsumption of labour under capital begins with the industrial revolution of late eighteenth century, advances step by step throughout the nineteenth century, and reaches a level of maturity in early twentieth century, that is to say with the onset of the imperialist stage of capitalism. Taylorism, arising at this stage and spreading rapidly, may be considered as the systematisation of the real subsumption of labour under capital.

We have already indicated that capital takes over the labour process in the shape in which it finds it, making do with the forms and degree of development the latter has undergone in late feudal society. Of course, due to the needs of surplus-value production (in other words, of valorisation), once it has brought together a certain number of workers under a single roof, capital is forced to bring certain changes to the labour process that it has taken over: working hours are made systematic and are extended; the intermissions are shortened and reduced in number, thus providing for the continuity of the production process; discipline is imposed on the work process through monitoring and supervision. In other words, even from the very beginning, the worker starts to lose the independence that he or she enjoyed in the labour process of the artisan or in agricultural activity. But this supervision is always fixated yet on the framework, or the envelop so to speak, of the production process. This latter process itself is still executed under the control of the worker, according to the knowledge, skills and planning of the worker. Again, in this first stage, the advantages of many workers being brought together to work come into play (cooperation) and increase the productivity of labour.

The first great leap forward for capital consists in establishing the division of labour. That diverse activities be tended to by the different members of the clan or tribe or family is a phenomenon encountered throughout history. However, capitalism creates a type of division of labour that is different from that implemented in previous human societies (and still goes on in capitalism as well) and later generalises it. The division of labour of previous societies relies on the same individuals carrying out each single activity (whether these be hunting or agriculture, the different branches of artisanship or domestic labour) in its integrity. A hunter or a mother caring for her child, as well as the farmer or the ironmonger, each possesses the totality of all the skills and knowledge necessary to complete the process and plans his or her work and executes it.

As Marx indicates in the passage quoted at the beginning of this section, before these producers create a product at the end of the labour process, they have already designed that outcome in their mind. It is only that they have taken upon themselves one or more out of the many activities that society carries out in order to survive within a social division of labour.

As opposed to this social division of labour, capitalism creates, within the labour process a detail (or technical) division of labour. This is based on each worker taking over a minute detail of the overall work that is to be done. It therefore severs the unity of the design and the execution phases of the work process, which is the defining characteristic of the production activity of human beings. What defines the detail division of labour is the breaking down of all the tasks necessary for a production activity (say the production of a car or an electronic instrument) into minute parts and then making each of these minute tasks the duty of a different worker. In such a situation, because the worker will forever be busy doing the simple job accorded to him or her, it is neither necessary nor possible for that worker to become part of the task of planning the process. A nineteenth-century author John Ruskin summarises the situation perfectly:

> We have much studied and perfected, of late, the great civilised invention of the division of labour; only we give it a false name. It is not, truly speaking, the labour that is divided; but the men: divided into mere segments of men-broken into small fragments and crumbs of life; so that all the little piece of intelligence that is left in a man is not enough to make a pin, or a nail, but exhausts itself in making the point of a pin, or the head of a nail. Now it is a good and desirable thing, truly, to make

It is no coincidence that Ruskin should choose the production of a pin as his example. Adam Smith, who made detail division of labour the lynchpin of his entire theory of capitalism, in his famous book the *Wealth of Nations* (1876) defended this type of division of labour on the basis of the example of workers doing minute tasks for the production of pins in the following manner:

> This great increase of the quantity of work which, in consequence of the division of labour, the same number of people are capable of performing, is owing to three different circumstances; first, to the increase of dexterity in every particular workman; secondly, to the saving of the time which is commonly lost in passing from one species of work to another; and lastly, to the invention of a great number of machines which facilitate and abridge labour, and enable one man to do the work of many. (Smith, 1977, 21–22)

Here we also need to touch upon the contribution of another theoretician of the division of labour and of the capitalist labour process. For this contribution, which has gone down in the history of economic thought as the "Babbage principle", demonstrates in naked form that the detail division of labour, far from being simply a technical innovation as it is usually presented, is a measure that serves directly the interests of capital. Charles Babbage, whose work Marx considers as one of the main sources of labour process studies, stated in his work of 1832 that, by breaking down a certain job to its constitutive parts, detail division of labour makes it possible to employ simple, unskilled labour (as opposed to skilled labour) for each of the tasks thus created and thereby bring down the cost of production for the capitalist because skilled labour always costs higher than simple, unskilled labour.

After having surveyed the reasons cited by Adam Smith and other writers before him with respect to the uses of detail work, Babbage concludes:

> Now, although all these are important causes, and each has its influence on the result; yet it appears to me, that any explanation of the cheapness of manufactured articles, as consequent upon the division of labour, would be incomplete if the following principle were omitted to be stated.
>
> That the master manufacturer, by dividing the work to be executed into different processes, each requiring different degrees of skill or of force, can purchase exactly that precise quantity of both which is necessary for each process; whereas, if the whole work were executed by one workman, that person must possess sufficient skill to perform the most difficult, and sufficient strength to execute the most laborious, of the operations into which the art is divided.

The example cited by Babbage (taken, just as Adam Smith, from the production of pins) makes the following point crystal clear. Even if a group of skilled workers could have produced the same quantity of pins as a group of unskilled workers that have been employed as a result of detail division of labour, in other words even when we abstract from the technical superiority of detail division of labour, because the wages of unskilled labourers are less costly than those of skilled labourers, the production based on detail division of labour costs the capitalist less and thus the surplus-value the capitalist appropriates increases (Braverman, 1998, 80). So, it transpires in naked form that it is not a question of the increase in the productive forces of humanity, but one that is intimately tied with the class interest of the capitalist.

In other words, as we have been repeating from the very beginning, the valorisation process of capital is moulding the labour process according to its own needs. When we come to analyse Taylorism below, we will see further that the effort of capital to reduce all labour to simple (unskilled) labour does not only intend to increase savings from the payroll for the capitalist, but serves much more profound purposes.

A third stage of the capture by capital of control over the labour process is the use of machinery in production. The use of machinery spread with the Industrial Revolution of the second half of the eighteenth century and increased the productive powers of humanity to an unprecedented level. With the use of machinery in production, a much greater quantity of products can be produced when compared to what the same number of workers were able to produce before, all other conditions remaining constant of course. In other words, labour productivity increases by leaps and bounds. But alongside this general advantage of machinery, the use it has been put to under capitalism has brought forward another characteristic of machines. Machinery makes it possible to bring the worker's pace of work under the control of capital, thereby making it possible to accelerate this pace. This means that alongside the rise in labour productivity, labour intensity also increases. No longer is the worker the judge of his or her movements on the basis of the knowledge and preference of this human being. The worker has to work in accordance with the tempo and the rhythm of a machine or a system of machines that has been determined outside that worker's will and deliberation. The worker is now an extension of the machine and of the capitalist embodied, so to speak, in that machine. Thus, capital has now captured the control of the labour process and established veritably the real submission of labour under capital. This is how Marx describes the situation born of the advent of machine production:

> Every kind of capitalist production, in so far as it is not only a labour process but also capital's process of valorization, has this in common, that it is not the worker who employs the conditions of his work, but rather the reverse, the conditions of work employ the worker. However, it is only with the coming of machinery that this inversion first acquires a technical and palpable reality. Owing to its conversion into an automaton, the instrument of labour confronts the worker during the labour process in the shape of capital, dead labour, which dominates and soaks up living labour-power. The separation of the intellectual faculties of the production process from manual labour, and the transformation of those faculties into powers exercised by capital over labour, is, as we have already shown, finally completed by large-scale industry erected on the foundation of machinery. (Marx, 1976, 548–549)

### 2. Taylorism: Real Subsumption Goes "Scientific"

Taylorism (labelled by the high-sounding term "Scientific Management" by its proponents) is the new labour management and control method of capitalism in the age of imperialism, that is the age when gigantic corporations have spread their tentacles around the world. It is no coincidence that this new management style should have arisen in the imperialist stage. The methods of Taylorism being quite expensive ones, these become feasible for the capitalist only at the level of large-scale production. For this reason, monopoly capital is a precondition for Taylorism.

There is also another point that we need to stress from the beginning as we will have to confront certain questions when we come to a discussion of lean production. Taylorism is a business management and labour control method that accepts the level of technological development as given. It is not an outcome of new technologies being implemented. This point is important, for relating Taylorism to a technological model will result in the reduction of a complex whole of methods that was developed for the refinement and consolidation of control by capital over labour to a technical question. It is such a naked fact that technology is not the central area of interest for Taylorism that one of the key ideologues of capitalism of the late twentieth century, business management consultant

Peter Drucker concurs in the fact that Scientific Management "was not concerned with technology. Indeed, it took tools and techniques largely as given" (Drucker, 1992, 192–193).

Frederic Winslow Taylor, the pioneer of "Scientific Management", was an American who renounced the opportunity of a bright college education despite being the offspring of a rich family and joined a factory as an apprentice to rise through the post of worker to the position of a foreman. This trajectory made it possible for Taylor to gain much more knowledge and experience on what goes on in the workshops of the factory than the ordinary college graduate, who, having received a BA in business administration or engineering, started a career at a factory at management level.

Taylor had gotten to know the behaviour of the working class on the job from the inside, so to speak. Having become foreman first and a manager later, or, having, in his own words, passed "to the other side of the fence", his entire system had as its basis in the use of this knowledge and experience on workers' pattern of behaviour for the benefit of the management. To put it in a nutshell, Taylor knew very well that to the extent that the worker can exercise control over the job, he or she will invent a host of methods to slow down the work tempo, which will then bring down labour intensity and increase costs (Taylor, 2005, 32).

> The greater part of systematic soldiering ... is done by the men with the deliberate object of keeping their employers ignorant of how fast work can be done. So universal is soldiering for this purpose, that hardly a competent workman can be found in a large establishment, whether he works by the day or on piece work, contract work or under any of the ordinary systems of compensating labor, who does not devote a considerable part of his time to studying just how slowly he can work and still convince his employer that he is going at a good pace.

Faced with this situation, Taylor's whole aim was, in order to solve the problem at its root, to turn the control over the job, that is to say the control over the production activity to the capitalist and management, the capitalist's representative vis-à-vis the workers. At the stage of history in question, detail division of labour has developed to a certain level and machinery has made it possible to determine the pace of work. On the

other hand, though, skilled craft work continues to rule under machine-propelled production. Those who control the tempo and rhythm of work are not the unskilled workers who expend simple labour and are dependent workers in the factory, but these skilled workers. That is precisely why Taylor himself talks about the "competent workman" in the passage quoted above. The whole aim of Taylor's system, then, is to eliminate the control exercised by the skilled worker on the production process and turn control over to management. All his literary activities and his practical work are geared towards this goal. His most important writings include *Shop Management* (1903) and *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911).

In his effort to wrest control over the production process away from the worker and turn it over to management, Taylor puts forth three basic principles:

1. To transfer the knowledge of the workers on the production process to management.
2. To separate the design and the execution of the job and to wrest the former away from the worker and turn it over to management.
3. To see to it that each step of the labour process is planned in detail by management and that execution is constantly supervised.

The basic method that was developed in order to implement these principles is named "time and motion studies". Taylor was the pioneer in this field, to which others later contributed as well. This method relies on the breaking down of the operations that the worker performs while working on a task into its smallest details, assigning a time span for each unit thus separated and determining in how long a total time span is needed for the entire operation to be finished when all the units have been reunified. This is to be carried out by the industrial engineering or planning departments to be newly established and to take over the functions of design and planning. The bringing together of the most minute units in diverse ways in order to be distributed to the different workers in the workshop is called "job design". Once job design has become the prerogative of management, this means that the planning and thereby the control of the work has been totally wrested away from the worker and turned over to the capitalist.

As may be seen, these are principles that may be implemented at all technological levels. The only condition here is that the growth in surplus-value that results from the rise in labour intensity be sufficient to justify the additional costs that the activity of the new industrial engineering and planning, etc. departments impose on the enterprise. Taylorism was implemented gradually over time by monopoly capital. Hence, the twentieth century saw a great leap forward with respect to the consolidation of the real submission process. The consequences of a widespread implementation of Taylorism can be summarised in the following manner:

1. **The expropriation of the worker with respect to the knowledge of production and the consequent deskilling**: Taylorism has put an end to the existence of the craft worker who possessed the totality of the knowledge necessary to produce a certain product. Throughout the twentieth century capitalism gradually deskilled workers and saw to it that simple labour became the basic form of labour in every branch of production. Naturally, this development will be accompanied by the rise in detail division of labour. The figure of the worker endlessly tightening screws has become a part of popular culture along with Taylorism.

2. **Fragmentation between design and execution**: The capitalist labour process was more and more subjected to a polarisation,

Before momentarily suspending this discussion on Taylorism, it might be in order to touch upon a certain point so as to avoid misunderstanding. Taylorism was not a management style that the capitalist class implemented only in industry. Throughout the twentieth century the principles and the methods of Taylorism spread slowly by slowly to all the different branches of economic activity. Today white-collar office work, telecommunications companies, banks and insurance companies, retail stores, even government offices of different stripes all have their own methods in the exercise of the principles of Taylorism. This implies that Taylorism is an important management and labour control method that has an impact on not only the industrial worker but workers in all branches of economic activity including more and more public employees.
