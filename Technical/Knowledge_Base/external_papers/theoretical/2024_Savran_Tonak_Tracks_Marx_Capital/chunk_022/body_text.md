## Section 3: The Empirical Situation (Page 190)

At the end of Chapter 9 of Volume One of *Capital*, Marx says: **"The rate of surplus-value is … an exact expression for the degree of exploitation of labour power by capital, or of the worker by the capitalist"** (Marx, 1976, 326). In other passages in *Capital*, Marx obtains the rate of exploitation, in the same manner as we have above, by dividing surplus working time by necessary time. However, it is mostly the *identity of the rate of surplus-value and the rate of exploitation* that has remained in people's mind.

However, unproductive labourers are also compelled to exchange their labour power with capital, or, in other words, by having the use-value of labour be consumed by capital and through the appropriation of surplus labour time is exploited by capital. This has not received the interest it deserves either in its general form or in the form of calculating concretely the rate of exploitation of unproductive workers empirically. This situation is partially due to the imprecisions involved in the theoretical debate on PUPL and partially the fact that even the more fundamental categories of the labour theory of value (*constant and variable capital, surplus-value* etc.) were not brought up for empirical measurement for a long time.

As far as we are aware, one of the first measurements of the rate of exploitation of unproductive workers was carried out by Shaikh and Tonak (1994) for the US economy for the period 1948–1989. The formulation that was developed during that measurement process has formed the basis of the estimate we have reached in using the Turkey data in this article. Here is the formula offered by Shaikh and Tonak (1994):

**E_up = {[(h_up / h_p) / (ec_up / ec_p)] * [1 + (S/V)]} - 1**

Here,

- E_up = the rate of exploitation of unproductive labourers
- H_up = the working time of unproductive workers
- H_p = the working time of productive workers
- Ec_up = The total wage bill for the unproductive workers
- ec_p = The total wage bill of productive workers
- (S/V) = the rate of exploitation of productive workers = the rate of surplus-value

## Section 4: Conclusion

When we look at the entire period overall and when we break down workers as productive and unproductive, we see that rates of exploitation diverge. In reality, we can say that this divergence has created a structural aspect during this period. The rate of exploitation in the unproductive sphere is lower than that in the productive sphere.

It is obvious that this sort of result is in need of explanation and must be related to the structural changes Turkey is going through. It transpires that the rate of exploitation in the commercial sector hovers several times higher above that in the productive sphere. This, too, is an outcome of structural change. Another very important observation regarding the commercial sector is that the high rate of exploitation there also shows great turbulence. One reason for this may be that the commercial sector is directly open to the vagaries of the cycle of economic expansion and contraction. Another reason is that the activity in the commercial sector is based on unorganised, unregistered, part-time labour without a modicum of job security. In other words, the modalities of employment in this sector provide the capitalist with the possibility of changing the rate of exploitation extremely easily and swiftly. We believe that our ordinary life experience also confirms this observation.

As the figures above display, a similar "flexibility" exists for the finance industry as well. The highest levels of fluctuations in the rate of exploitation (especially when the rapid rise after 2000 is taken into consideration) have been experienced in the finance industry alongside trade. It is interesting that the financial sector is also one where, until recently, the right to strike was legally banned. A revision of the relevant provision now allows for the right to strike. However, most bank workers are organised in company unions that work hand in glove with the bank they work for and negotiations are of the most superficial kind. It is also an industry where layoffs and unpaid overtime are easily and legitimately used measures. All of these make it possible for capital in the finance industry to manipulate the rate of exploitation easily in times of crisis. When we take up the entire period and compare it with other sectors, we see that the rate of exploitation in the public sector displays a more stable course. Again,

If those who wage class struggles become aware of this situation, this may act to expand the possibilities of successful struggles. ### Statistically Defining Productive Labour

The labour-hour magnitude worked in each branch of activity is obtained from the micro data sets of the Household Labour Force Surveys of the Turkish Statistical Institute and the aggregate sectoral wage magnitudes from the system of national accounts.

### Note on the Inclusion of the Self-Employed Within the Broad Definition of Labour

Those individuals employed within the population whose "position at work" is "waged, salaried or paid daily wages" are aggregated under the heading "labour". The "self-employed" correspond to the category petty commodity producers and force us to make a choice. These will either be taken up together with "employers" since they are entrepreneurs and own their means of production, as part of the unproductive sphere, or as workers who exploit their own labour since they participate in production activity and (together with unpaid family labour) will be included in the broad definition of "labour". In the literature on the advanced capitalist economies, the significance of the choice derives mainly from theoretical consistency.

The choice made does not really have an empirical importance for those countries. The reason is that in these economies the sphere of petty commodity production is extremely narrow. How the "self-employed" are taken up is, on the contrary, of structural importance when we come to a country such as Turkey. There are reasons why the self-employed should be considered within the broad definition of labour in this type of country. Economic activities in agriculture and, to a certain extent, in the commercial sector, are carried out in the form of petty commodity production. The sphere of petty commodity production involves a section of the population that produces for the market, has no other choice but to accumulate by exploiting its own labour more and more, and is gradually eliminated.

When we consider the question from the point of view of hours worked, we see that total time worked for the self-employed corresponds to 60 per cent of the hours worked by labour narrowly defined in 1988

### The Division of Wage Remuneration Between Productive and Unproductive Sectors and Occupations

Although it is possible to apply the ratio productive occupations/unproductive occupations directly to the sectoral wage remuneration for each sector, for a more realistic estimation, we considered the use of information regarding the breakdown of hourly wages between productive and unproductive occupations as the better way to proceed. For this, the only source we have is the Household Budget Survey for 1994. For the only source that uses the ISCO-68 (International Standard Classification of Occupation) on which we base our definition of productive occupation is this 1994 Household Budget Survey.

We reach the total wage remuneration for productive labour by applying the ratios obtained by multiplying the two ratios to annual sectoral labour force remuneration.

### Note on the Imputation of Wages to the Self-Employed

The self-employed is a section of the population that set aside an imputed share of the income produced for their own consumption at the average current wage level and save and invest what remains over and above this level, constantly having to increase the rate of exploitation of their own labour in order to increase this latter part. When imputing wages to the self-employed, the reference that we take is the average level of hourly wages paid for the relevant year and the relevant sector. Hence the distinction productive/unproductive labour is also taken into consideration for this section of the population.

That is why we can say with certainty that this real expansion in the unproductive sphere was not possible to capture entirely through our estimated data set.
