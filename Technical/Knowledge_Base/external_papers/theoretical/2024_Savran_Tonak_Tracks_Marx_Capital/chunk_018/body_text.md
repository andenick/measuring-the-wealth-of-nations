## 2 Impact of the October Revolution

Bohor Israel's pioneering effort was followed by a series of initiatives taken to translate *Capital*, again in abridged form, in the 1930s. Two historic moments occurred between these two dates. On the one hand, in a world-historic event, the October Revolution of 1917 opened a new era for socialism and Marxism, not only in its homeland, Russia, but the whole world. On the other hand, and of more regional significance, a new Republic of Turkey had been established on the ruins of the Ottoman Empire in 1923. The two events were the product of a form of combined development in that the Turkish republicans of Mustafa Kemal were supported by the new Soviet state in their struggle against the military occupation of what remained of Ottoman territory by British, French and other imperialist powers and also lesser forces. Despite this, the new republic, which soon took the form of a one-party state, heavily repressed the communist movement.

The latter was organically linked to the October Revolution and its products, the Soviet state and the Communist International (Comintern). The Communist Party of Turkey [Türkiye Komünist Fırkası, TKF, first, and Türkiye Komünist Partisi, TKP, later] was founded in 1920 in Baku, today capital of the former Soviet Republic of Azerbaijan, not Turkey proper. Nazım Hikmet, a Turkish poet of worldwide renown and one of the foremost Turkish communist organisers of his day, was educated in the Moscow of Lenin and Trotsky in the early 1920s. According to certain sources, Capital was translated into Turkish by several authors in the early years of the October Revolution. Among these perhaps the most important is the mention of the name of Mustafa Suphi, the founding leader of Turkish communism. However, hard evidence on these alleged translations has not been discovered yet (Alkan, 2014a, Footnotes 15 and 16).

So the next round of translations of *Capital* will have to be dated, until such discovery, to the 1930s. In the earlier part of the 1930s, when, face to the Great Depression, the fledgling Turkish bourgeoisie adopted planning, the Soviet Union was a source both of inspiration and finance. This might have encouraged Turkish communists to publish the works of Marx and Engels, including Capital.

This was a period when young Turkish intellectuals and workers were being introduced to Marxism through the lyric poetry of Nazım Hikmet. In his poem "Şair" [The Poet], this one dating to 1923, his Moscow days, in describing his poetry, Nazım first says that the gazel he likes most is the Anti-Dühring of Engels before writing the following verses: "I am a poet/and I've written as many poems as it rains during a whole year/ but I'm waiting to become/the hafiz of Capital/in order to start/my masterpiece"<sup>3</sup> (Nazım Hikmet, 2008, 113–114).

It was in this context that a succession of summary translations of Capital saw the light of day in the 1930s. The first was that by Haydar Rifat (later Yorulmaz), a jurist and one of the prominent translators of his time. Rifat's translation, published in 1933, was a summary by Georges Deville, based on the 14-tome publication in French of Capital (the Alfred Costes edition). However, Haydar Rifat's translation was harshly criticised by Kerim Sadi, a prominent communist of the time. This may be at least one of the reasons why Haydar Rifat discontinued his work on the translation, whereas his original intention was to translate all three volumes (Alkan, 2014a; Bilgi, 1989; Satlıgan, 2011).

The critic, Kerim Sadi, himself put out a 32-page summary translation of Capital in 1936. Kerim Sadi was the nom-de-guerre of the communist intellectual Nevzat Cerrahlar. This was based apparently on the German original as well as a French edition, prepared by Paul Lafargue, Marx's son-in-law, and published in 1893<sup>4</sup> (Alkan, 2014a; Bilgi, 1989).

The same year, 1936, also saw the publication of a summary translation published by yet another prominent communist, Suphi Nuri İleri. This was the translation of the extremely popular summary of Volume I made by an Italian socialist, Carlo Cafiero. Cafiero's summary was subsequently translated into French by J. In 1937, probably in the most serious initiative taken in this area in the interwar period, Hikmet Kıvılcımlı, a maverick communist and a Marxist thinker in his own right, undertook the translation of Capital from the German edition, checking it against the French and Russian translations as well. The conjecture has even been made (Satlıgan, 2011, p. 59) that Kıvılcımlı started to learn German because, during his first attempt to translate Capital in the late 1920s, he had discovered errors in the French translation (Alkan, 2014a). Following Marx's advice regarding the French edition, Kıvılcımlı decided to publish Capital, Volume I in instalments, in an effort to reach out to workers and give them "an instalment of Capital for the price of a daily newspaper".

This rather feverish activity in translating Capital into Turkish, albeit in abridged form, which saw four translations in the five-year period from 1933 to 1937 came to an abrupt end in 1938. Turkey had been moving for some time away from the Soviet Union and towards Nazi Germany, a trend that was going to last until the last years of World War II. This culminated in a series of attacks on the communist movement. In 1938, the one-party regime proceeded to ban certain Marxist works that had been published in preceding years. Capital was on the list of prohibited works.

This was the beginning of a quarter-century-long crossing of the desert until the rise of the mass movement of the 1960s and the 70s. And although the post-war atmosphere forced Turkey into an overture towards a multi-party system, this took the form of an illiberal transition in which the new power structure proved to be even less tolerant towards communism, for now, Turkey's strategic orientation was to come under the roof of NATO.

Capital only returned to bookstores in 1965, after a decision of the new Constitutional Court in 1965 to the effect that the prohibition of propaganda of class rule (i.e. Marxism) would henceforth not include "scientific works". But this court decision owed much to the new atmosphere within the country.

## 3 Capital Comes to Turkey as a By-Product of Class Struggle

The full-scale entry of *Capital* into Turkey came in the 1960s and 70s, which also happened to be the golden age, so to speak, of class struggle in twentieth-century Turkey. These two decades between the two coups d'Etat of 1960 and 1980 formed a special period of extraordinary vibrancy. Starting even as early as 1961, enjoying an almost continuous crescendo of strikes and actions of all sorts, combined with the formation and growth of a new militant brand of trade unionism, working-class combativity reached its climax in the two days of 15–16 June 1970, when up to 150 thousand workers practically took over the city of Istanbul and its industrial vicinity in protest against a new attempt by parliament to obstruct militant trade unionism. Proletarian awakening was not, however, the only novel trend. There was a rise of social and political struggles across the board: in addition to the workers, the peasantry, the oppressed Kurdish people, and in 1968 in tandem with the rest of the world, the student body all joined the fray. It was under these conditions that the intelligentsia, as well as the youth, turned to theory.

Interestingly enough, the first translation of Capital published in this period paid homage to the efforts of the rather isolated communist intellectuals of the 1930s and was a sign of historical continuity. It will be remembered that one of the abridged translations of the 1930s was that done by Suphi Nuri İleri of the widely read Carlo Cafiero summary of Capital, Volume I. Now, immediately upon the heels of the decision of the Constitutional Court, İleri's son, Rasih Nuri İleri published in 1965 a new translation of the same book, omitting certain introductions and additional texts, under the title Kapital. Özet [Capital. A Summary].

The first full translation of Capital, Volume I, was published between December 1965 and April 1967 in five tomes by Sol Yayınları [Left Books] (or Sol for short), an Ankara publishing house that has remained the foremost publisher of Marxist classics to this day. Thus Volume I became available in Turkish in complete form only on the centenary of its publication in German! This volume was translated from its German original, with occasional comparison with the English translation, by Mehmet Selik, professor of history of economic thought, who was also simultaneously working on a dissertation on Marx's value theory. Selik then translated a part of Volume III before Volume II (a curiosity that may be explained by the fact that because the translator was simultaneously

A reactionary military intervention established an interregnum between 1971 and 1973, which saw the publisher Sol brought under persecution. This created a cleavage between the translator and the publisher. Impatient to see his translation in printed form, Selik turned it over to a newly established publishing house, Odak Yayınları, which published Volume I in two tomes in 1974, followed subsequently by the first part of Volume III later. It would transpire much later that Selik had translated Volume II as well, but this saw the light of day only in the twenty-first century (see below).

Enraged, Muzaffer Erdost, publisher of Sol, turned to another translator, Alaattin Bilgi, for a new translation of Capital. Bilgi, somewhat an autodidact who had studied philosophy at university, passionately devoted himself to the task, churning out volume after volume in the space of three years. Thus, Volume I was published in his translation in 1975, Volume II in 1976, and Volume III in 1978, as out on the streets Turkey was going through the heady days of class struggle and a hardly disguised civil war between the left and the fascist movement. Despite his limited initial knowledge of economics, Bilgi, with his full dedication to his noble task, provided succeeding generations with the first, and for three more decades the only, full translation of Capital. He also wrote a popularised summary of the book he was now so familiar with (Bilgi, 1992).

The dispute between Selik and Sol has left behind a bitter controversy, whose legacy has since haunted the relationship between two main translations of Capital, the Selik and the Bilgi translations. We cannot go here into the intricacies of the controversy that has arisen as a result, which would sound like hair-splitting to a foreign audience. We simply did not wish to ignore or hide this sharp controversy. We will have to return to it briefly later on in the chapter.

## 4 The Twenty-First Century: Capital Gains a Life of Its Own

By the end of the 1970s, *Capital* and Marxist economics had already made headway in the academia, in public intellectual life and in the popular imagination. From then on, the book and the ideas it inspired started to have a life of their own, rather independent of the varying fortunes of class struggle. Even in the repressive political atmosphere of the military dictatorship of the early 1980s, which brought to an abrupt end the feverish tempo of class struggle and fiercely shattered all militant trade unionism and left-wing politics, at least for a time, Capital was being sold and taught as opposed to the earlier periods of repression. Marxist political economy and heterodox economics progressively gained ground at universities. Many left-wing educational associations, in particular Bilar, which was founded under the military in the 1980s to cater to the needs of socialist teachers and students, made place for a study of Marxist economics.

Possibly the most salient instance of this kind of learning environment was created, somewhat later, at the so-called Free University [Özgür Üniversite], which, despite the impression created by its name, is really a counter-university of sorts, a quasi-Marxist educational association. Especially in its Istanbul branch, Capital reading groups (covering not only Volume I as is customary but all three volumes) have been organised intermittently for over a decade now by Marxist academics (including the authors of this chapter) for students of all ages and from all walks of life.

An extremely successful Symposium was held in Istanbul in November 2007, on the occasion of the 140th anniversary of the publication of Capital, Volume I. The venue was, quite significantly the auditorium of the Oil, Chemical and Rubber Workers' Union, fully packed for the two days of the symposium. Panels were held on the architecture and logic of Capital, its relevance for the working class and unions, the connection of the working class movement in Turkey to Capital, the impact of Marx's magnum opus on the social sciences in Turkey, the actuality of Capital in our day, the empirical measurement of value categories, and the relation of the left in Turkey to Capital. Among the speakers were academics, unionists, and political leaders of the left. (A companion Symposium was to be organised in 2008 in Ankara, Turkey's capital city, on the occasion of the 160th anniversary of the publication of the Communist Manifesto.)

Not much research exists on the relation of the various currents of the political left to a study of Capital, be it at the level of the intellectual input that can be derived from a study of Marx's masterpiece for strategy and programme or at the level of the education of grassroots militants. On the basis of the circumstantial evidence available to the authors, it might be said that, of late, there are signs that certain political currents, especially those that still attribute great importance to political work within the ranks of the proletariat and claim allegiance to classical Marxism, are taking up at least a rudimentary teaching of the ideas in Capital to their younger militants. It is interesting, as well, that after the two symposia on Capital and the Communist Manifesto, a large conference was held by the political left this time, in the same union auditorium where the Capital symposium had been held, in 2009 on the so-called "global financial crisis" and its implications for class struggle. What is of special interest here was the fact that many currents represented tried to base their analysis and arguments on the ideas of Marx as expounded in Capital.

The influence of Capital on the popular imagination has reached such levels that a humoristic book has been published under the title *Laz Kapital* (Okumuş, 2006).<sup>5</sup> The Laz are a people of controversial origin who inhabit the eastern part of Turkey's Black Sea region to the north of the country. They are known for their easily irritable character as well as their humour, making fun of their own idiosyncrasy even more than others do. So Laz Kapital was meant to be a satirical critique of capitalism and of all the former leftists who lost their faith in Marxism upon the collapse of the twentieth-century experiments in socialist construction. It became so popular that its author was invited to address many left-wing meetings.

The prestige of Capital reached new heights after the onset of the so-called "global financial crisis" in 2008. A full-day conference was organised on the occasion of a new edition of Capital (of which more in a moment) in 2011 in a large auditorium of a university. A combined translation from the German of Volume III by Selik and Özalp appeared in 2015. For all three volumes, Oktar Türel, professor emeritus of economics, played an important role in checking against the English translation.

All in all, this was a new complete edition of Capital that rivalled the Sol-Bilgi edition. This revived the earlier controversy. ## 5 Intellectual Reception of Capital: Four Generations and Three Schools

Capital has had a strong impact on the intellectual life of the Turkish left. Marxist political economy, or in other words the critique of political economy, has been an important tradition since the 1960s. One can delineate four generations of Marxist economists since the mid-1950s and, very broadly speaking, three different schools.

• The pioneering effort of Sencer Divitçioğlu, who started propagating the Marxist ideas he adopted in France while he was working on his doctoral degree in the 1950s (Divitçioğlu, 2012), a time of reactionary atmosphere in the country as explained above is worth noting. He is known to have defended Marx against Keynes in the context of a controversy in a widely read journal in 1955 (Boratav, 2007) He was one of the two major proponents of the relevance of Marx's concept of the Asiatic mode of production for Turkey. The other was İdris Küçükömer, his friend and colleague at Istanbul University, where both were professors. They presented an alternative view of Ottoman and Turkish history on this basis. In the 1970s, Divitçioğlu was the most ardent defender of the Sraffian/neo-Ricardian theory among Marxist economists in Turkey. From the mid-1980s on the late Divitçioğlu explicitly abandoned Marxism.

His intervention in the 1960s on the nature of the mode of production and in particular of the peasantry lacks none of the rigour of the Frank-Laclau debate of the same period.
