• The fourth generation came of age during the twenty-first century, but has done some important work on questions pertaining to different aspects of *Capital*, extending from the use of the dialectic all the way to the empirical measurement of value categories.⁶

Very broadly speaking three schools of thinking may be discerned:

• **Neo-Ricardians:** their main spokesperson was Sencer Divitçioğlu, who believed that the so-called "transformation problem" was the Achilles' heel of Marxist political economy and abandoned Marxism because he saw a failure in all attempts to solve that "problem".

• **Keynesian-developmentalists:** Yılmaz Akyüz, Erinç Yeldan, Şevket Pamuk, Çağlar Keyder at an early stage and Yalçın Küçük in at least some of his work, conceive of Marx's political economy as linear development of classical political economy, only at a higher level of consistency, and regard the distribution of the product produced (income) and economic development as the ultimate questions that political economy is concerned with.

• **Proponents of a value-theoretical approach:** Sungur Savran, Nail Satlıgan, E. Ahmet Tonak, and many in the fourth generation regard Marx's critique of political economy as a supersession of the limitations of the classical economy in its Smithian and Ricardian forms and study the mode of production as the historical entity that should be the centre of attention rather than the (derivative) distribution of income between the various classes.

Korkut Boratav can be placed between categories 2 and 3. On the question of the neo-Ricardian controversy, he has always been orthodox. On the other hand, he subscribes fully to the distribution- and development-focused approach of category 2.

### 6 A BOOK WITH A FUTURE

All in all, in the half-century that has passed since the full publication of Volume I in 1965–1967, *Capital* has had a successful trajectory in Turkey as a book. There are currently two major translations of the complete work available. One is the Alaattin Bilgi translation, published by Sol in the 1970s, which has been churning out reprints endlessly since then. The other one is the combined product of Mehmet Selik, Nail Satlıgan and Erkin Özalp, published at various stages in partial form by Sol and Odak in the 1960s and 70s, but in complete form only in the course of the twenty-first century as a new edition by Yordam. The history of this translation is not as straightforward as that of the previous one. Selik did the translation in the 1960s and 70s, while Satlıgan and Özalp edited his translation as well as translated the parts he had never finished.

With regard to print figures and reprints, the Selik translation of Volume I was published in five tomes by Sol between December 1965 and April 1967 in 5000 copies. Muzaffer Erdost, the publisher, says that by the time he was arrested by the military regime in May 1971, almost all the copies had been sold. We know that Selik had this translation published in two tomes in 1974, but unfortunately, no information exists regarding the number of press runs for this edition. Making the plausible assumption that this edition was printed in the same number of copies, we can conclude that the first complete translation of Volume I was printed in 10,000 copies and an equal number were sold. With the appearance of the Bilgi translation in Sol and, later, the Selik-Satlıgan edition in Yordam, this figure has risen considerably. Since Sol had a press run of 10,000 for the first and a run of 5000 for the most recent 11th editions, and since Yordam reports that 13,000 copies were sold in 9 reprints, it may be concluded that the print (and sales) figure for the period between 1975 and 2016 was around 73,000. The first edition of Volume II of the Bilgi translation (1975) had a press run of 10,000. As of 2016, the 9th reprint (5000 copies) had been reached. As for Yordam, this publishing house has brought out five editions of Volume II since 2012 and sold a total of 6500 copies. Hence, it may be inferred that Volume II was printed in 56,500 copies overall and that all of these were sold. The first edition of Volume III of the Bilgi translation (1978) had a press run of 10,000 copies and the most recent 8th reprint had a run of 3000. ## 1 THEORETICAL BACKGROUND: SURPLUS-VALUE AND POA

Marx's theory of profit is based on the production of surplus-value presupposing an exchange between equivalents.² Capital pays the worker a wage as the equivalent of the value of his or her labour power and takes possession of the surplus-value produced, thus appropriating surplus labour. This appropriation presumes productive consumption of the use-value of the labour power that Marx identifies as a peculiar commodity with a unique capacity of producing more value than its own (Marx, 1977).

This is the most abstract level of analysis regarding the formation of profit in the capitalist mode of production. As Marx's analysis becomes less abstract through the volumes of *Capital*, we are introduced to more concrete manifestations of surplus-value such as industrial profit, ground rent, interest and profit of enterprise. Figure 1 presents in detail various monetary expressions of surplus-value generated by the production activities of labour in productive sectors, which also funds nonindustrial profit.
