## Figure 1: Developing Economies FDI Share with and without China

**Location:** Page 323

**Type:** Line graph

**Description:**
This figure displays two trend lines showing the share of Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) flowing to developing economies from 1990 to 2018:

1. **Red line (upper):** "Developing Economies FDI Share" - includes China
2. **Blue line (lower):** "Developing Economies FDI Share (except China)" - excludes China

**Key Features:**
- **X-axis:** Years from 1990 to 2018
- **Y-axis:** Share proportion (0.00 to 0.60)
- **Time period:** 1990-2018

**Key Trends:**
- Both lines show general upward trends over the period
- The gap between the two lines represents China's share of FDI to developing economies
- Notable volatility in the late 1990s and early 2000s
- The lines diverge significantly after 2010, indicating China's growing importance
- In 2018, developing economies (including China) reached approximately 54% of global FDI
- Excluding China, the share drops to approximately 44% in 2018
- A dip is visible around 2016-2017 in both lines
- The steepest increase occurs from 2016-2018

**Data Source:** Based on data in UNCTAD (2019)

**Context:** This figure illustrates the first-ever phenomenon in 2018 where FDI inflows to developing economies exceeded those to developed economies, reaching 54% (including China). However, UNCTAD considers this an anomaly due to the anomalous fall in FDI to developed countries rather than a structural shift.

## Figure 2: Leading Factors for Foreign Direct Investment

**Location:** Page 324

**Type:** Horizontal stacked bar chart

**Description:**
This figure displays survey results from 754 executives of major multinational corporations regarding factors they consider important for foreign direct investment decisions. Each factor is shown with two categories: "Important" (hatched pattern) and "Critically Important" (dotted pattern).

**Factors Listed (from top to bottom):**

1. **Political Stability & Security:** 29% Important + 58% Critically Important = 87% total
2. **Legal & Regulatory Environment:** 40% Important + 46% Critically Important = 86% total
3. **Large Domestic Market Size:** 42% Important + 38% Critically Important = 80% total
4. **Macroeconomic Stability & Favourable Exchange Rate:** 43% Important + 35% Critically Important = 78% total
5. **Available Talent & Skill of Labour:** 48% Important + 25% Critically Important = 73% total
6. **Good Physical Infrastructure:** 46% Important + 25% Critically Important = 71% total
7. **Low Tax Rates:** 40% Important + 18% Critically Important = 58% total
8. **Low Cost of Labour & Inputs:** 35% Important + 18% Critically Important = 53% total

**Key Observations:**
- Political stability and security is the most important factor (87% combined)
- Legal and regulatory environment is nearly as important (86%)
- Low cost of labour and inputs ranks LAST among the factors (53%), contrary to common assumptions on the Left
- Institutional and market factors dominate over cost factors
- The distribution between "Important" and "Critically Important" varies, with political/legal factors having higher "Critically Important" percentages

**Data Source:** Based on data in World Bank (2018)

**Significance:** This data contradicts the common assumption that capital flows to the South primarily because of low wages. Instead, political stability, legal environment, and market size are more critical factors in FDI decisions.

## Figure 3: Foreign Profits as Percentage of Total US Profits and Domestic US Wages, 1998-2018

**Location:** Page 325

**Type:** Dual line graph

**Description:**
This figure shows two trend lines over the period 1998-2018:

1. **Solid line (left axis):** "Foreign Profit as % of Total U.S. Profits"
2. **Dashed line (right axis):** "Foreign Profit as % of Domestic Wages"

**Key Features:**
- **X-axis:** Years from 1998 to 2018
- **Left Y-axis:** Percentage scale from 0.00 to 0.70 (for foreign profits as % of total US profits)
- **Right Y-axis:** Percentage scale (for foreign profits as % of domestic wages)

**Key Trends:**

**Foreign Profits as % of Total US Profits (solid line):**
- Starts around 0.25 (25%) in 1998
- Rises steadily to peak around 0.50 (50%) in 2006-2007
- Sharp decline during 2007-2009 financial crisis, dropping to approximately 0.20 (20%)
- Gradual recovery after 2009
- Fluctuates between 0.20 and 0.40 in the 2010s
- General trend: fluctuation between 20-50% (excluding crisis period)

**Foreign Profits as % of Domestic Wages (dashed line):**
- Remains relatively stable throughout the period
- Stays consistently low, fluctuating around 6-7% of total wages
- Shows less volatility than the profits line
- Minimal impact from the 2007-2009 crisis compared to the profits line

**Data Source:** 
- Corporate profits from NIPA Tables 6-16 B-D: line 2, domestic industries; line 6, receipts from rest of the world
- Employee compensation from NIA Table 1.13, line 6, compensation of employees
- Methodology from Post (2010, 21)

**Significance:** 
- This figure challenges the "labour aristocracy" thesis by showing that repatriated profits from the South represent only approximately 6-7% of total US wages
- This calculation assumes the extremely unrealistic scenario that ALL transferred foreign profits are fully allocated to US workers while US MNCs retain none
- Considering that only about 50% of "foreign profits" originate from countries in the South, their actual contribution varies between 10-25% of total US profits
