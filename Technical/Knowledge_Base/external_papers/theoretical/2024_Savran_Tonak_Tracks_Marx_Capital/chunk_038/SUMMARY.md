## Overview
This chunk provides a comprehensive overview of Marxist crisis theory, contrasting it with mainstream economic approaches and distinguishing between different schools within Marxist thought. The authors explain Marx's fundamental contributions to understanding capitalist crises and critically evaluate competing Marxist theories.

## Main Topics Covered

### 1. Creative Destruction and Revolution (pp. 371-372 / 352)
- Critique of Schumpeter's "creative destruction" concept as limiting crisis to destruction of means of production
- Argument that crises can also destroy relations of production, potentially leading to revolution and new classless society
- Creative destruction formulated to preclude radical transformation

### 2. Marx's Theory of Crisis: Introduction (pp. 372-373 / 352-353)
- **Marx's rejection of Say's Law**: Unlike Ricardo, post-Ricardians, marginalist revolution (Menger, Walras), and neoclassical economists
- **Keynes' underestimation of Marx**: Bourgeois prejudices led Keynes to praise Gesell over Marx
- **Gesell reference**: Finance minister of Bavarian Soviet Republic (1919), author of *The Natural Economic Order* (1916)
- Keynes predicted future would learn more from Gesell than Marx - proven wrong

### 3. Marx's Critique of Say's Law (pp. 372-373 / 353)
- **Say's error**: Reduced mediated unity between sale and purchase into unmediated relationship between supply and demand
- **Key insight**: Sellers can delay purchases, hoarding money
- **Money as mediator**: Sale and purchase are separate transactions mediated by conversion from commodity form to money form and back
- **Crisis possibility**: If sufficiently large number of sellers hoard money, demand shortfall triggers crisis

### 4. Crisis as Problem and Solution (pp. 373 / 353)
- **Dual nature**: Crises reveal contradictions in capital accumulation concealed during profit-making periods
- **Moral depreciation**: Means of production become uncompetitive, cannot serve as basis for competitive effort
- **Agreement with Schumpeter**: On destructive aspect containing solution

### 5. Devalorisation Process (pp. 373-374 / 354)
- **Two required solutions to major crisis**:
  1. Raise general rate of profit
  2. Eliminate means of production subject to moral depreciation
- **Valorisation** (*Verwertung*): Production adds new value through living labour expenditure
- **Devalorisation** (*Entwertung*): Loss in value, potentially complete; capital in plant/machinery becomes worthless
- **Fundamental importance**: Elimination of old, obsolete, insufficiently productive equipment

### 6. Triggers vs. Causes (pp. 374 / 354)
- **Critical distinction**: Triggering factors differ from crisis itself
- **1973-74 oil crisis example**: Oil price rise triggered crisis, but real cause was falling profit rate due to replacement of living labour by machinery/automation
- **2007-09 subprime crisis example**: Subprime mortgages detonated collapse in financial system expanded beyond productive base
- **Bourgeois economics error**: Attributing root cause to triggering factor creates public confusion

### 7. Overproduction Question (pp. 374-375 / 355)
- **Dialectic**: Real processes causing crises differ from outward manifestations
- **Glut of unsold commodities**: Starting form of crises, but not essential cause
- **Common error**: Deducing crises are essentially overproduction crises from appearance of unsold goods
- **Warning**: Confusing essential processes with outward appearances leads to misunderstanding

### 8. Role of Finance in Crises (pp. 375-376 / 355-356)
- **Real dynamic**: Located in productive sphere, not finance
- **Finance's contradictory role**: Not hotbed of crisis but plays fundamentally important role in unfolding
- **Two methods of additional capital**: Bank credit and stock market funds
- **Bank credit**: Deposits create new tranches of credit surpassing original deposits
- **Stock market**: Duplication of value embodied in means of production

### 9. Fictitious Capital and Over-Credit (pp. 375-376 / 356)
- **Fictitious capital**: Separates from original capital, can reach magnitudes incommensurate with productive capital
- **Over-credit mechanism**: Credit carries economic activity beyond rationally possible confines
- **Finance's utility in crisis**: Blows new life into struggling units, sustains activity, prevents bankruptcies
- **Cost**: Rising debt accumulation
- **Disproportionality**: Financial flows reach magnitude disproportionate to productive base
- **2008 pattern**: Later the crash, worse the outcome - will likely repeat in short/medium-term

### 10. Three Rival Marxist Theories (pp. 376-377 / 356-357)
- **Selection criteria**: Theories advanced to explain current crisis or 1974-75 depressive wave
- **Three theories**:
  1. Profit-squeeze theory
  2. Underconsumption theory
  3. Law of tendential fall of profit rate

### 11. Profit-Squeeze Theory (pp. 377-378 / 357-358)
- **Core claim**: Crisis caused by strengthening of labour, rising wages squeezing profit rate
- **Status**: Popular at end of post-war boom (mid-1940s to mid-1970s), now largely out of favour
- **Common ground with falling profit rate**: Both attribute crises to fall in profits
- **Key difference**: Profit-squeeze attributes to class division of product; falling rate to changing composition of capital
- **Initial plausibility (mid-1970s)**: Shrinking reserve army of labour, strengthened working class, labour unions, wage rises
- **Theoretical problem**: Change in income distribution rectifiable in short period through capitalist functioning

### 12. Marx on Capital's Control of Labor Supply (pp. 377-378 / 358)
- **Counter-mechanism**: More machinery-intensive techniques shed labour, increase unemployment, bring down wages
- **Marx's insight**: Capital controls both demand for and supply of labour force
- **Implication**: Impossible to force wages above value of labour power for meaningfully long period
- **Empirical refutation**: Profit-squeeze may be true in initial crisis phase but cannot explain years-long major crises
- **2008 evidence**: Wages not high enough to squeeze profits - had stagnated for decades
- **Verdict**: Influential but influence has not lasted

### 13. Underconsumption Theory Overview (pp. 378-379 / 358-359)
- **Historical and current impact**: Great influence on Marxist thinking
- **Scope**: Extended beyond crisis theory to explain overall functioning of capitalist system
- **Holistic approach**: Consumption and demand as main drivers of capitalist mode of production
- **Departure from Marx**: Marx showed production of surplus-value/profit as driving force
- **Public influence**: Almost common-sense to point to lack of demand when crisis debated

### 14. Underconsumptionist Schools and Figures (pp. 379 / 359)
- **Rosa Luxemburg (1871–1919)**: Developed imperialism theory, criticized Marx's reproduction schemes in Volume 2
- **Monthly Review school**: Paul Sweezy, Paul Baran
- **French regulation school**: Michel Aglietta, Robert Boyer, Alain Lipietz
- **Social structure of accumulation school**: David Gordon, Michael Reich, Richard Edwards, Thomas Weisskopf, Sam Bowles
- **Other influential figures**: David Harvey

### 15. Underconsumptionist Question (pp. 379 / 359)
- **Central question**: Where does demand for goods corresponding to surplus-value come from?
- **Logic**: Value produced exceeds capital replacement + worker consumption
- **Problem**: Capitalists won't consume entire surplus product
- **Various solutions**:
  - **Luxemburg**: Middle strata consumers (reminiscent of Malthus)
  - **Baran/Sweezy**: State expenditure and unproductive expenditures (advertising)
  - **Others**: Permanent arms economy (popular 1960s-70s)
  - **Regulation/SSA schools**: Welfare state as element of Fordist regime
  - **Harvey**: Subsidiary factors
  - **Regulation/SSA**: Institutions and macroeconomic policy shifts

### 16. Marx's Answer: Expanded Reproduction (pp. 379-380 / 360)
- **Overlooked solution**: Capitalist accumulation itself provides demand
- **Simple reproduction**: Capitalist class consumes entire surplus-value as revenue (heuristic device)
- **Expanded reproduction**: Capital accumulation; capital expands production scale using surplus-value to purchase new means of production and hire new workers
- **Demand creation**: New workers paid wages to consume additional goods beyond existing workers
- **Simple conclusion**: Accumulation creates sufficient demand to consume surplus-value under normal conditions
- **Bukharin's criticism**: If you exclude expanded reproduction at outset, impossible to explain it later (Luxemburg & Bukharin, 1972)

### 17. Problems with Underconsumptionism (pp. 380 / 360-361)
- **Cannot explain periodicity**: Even if correct about lack of demand, cannot explain recurrence at periodic rhythm
- **Example (Monthly Review)**: Hard to understand why constant pump-priming fails periodically
- **Static framework**: Whatever state economy is in continues forever until discrete change
- **Requirement**: Must explain why change comes periodically - none have addressed this
- **Political consequence**: Tends to propose wage increases as solution to crises

### 18. Political Implications of Underconsumptionism (pp. 380 / 361)
- **Reformist policy**: Trying to convince capitalists wage increases serve their best interests
- **Illusion of common interests**: Between main classes
- **Reality**: Severe economic crisis deepens contradictions between capitalists and workers
- **Effect**: Sedative blunting working class will to fight

### 19. Marx's Critique of Wage-Increase Solution (pp. 380 / 361)
- **Marx quote**: Crises always prepared by period when wages rise and working class receives greater share
- **Logical problem**: If wage increases prevented crises, periods of rising wages should avert crisis
- **Verdict**: Underconsumptionism does not stand scrutiny

## Key Theoretical Contributions

### Marx's Original Insights
1. **Rejection of Say's Law**: Money mediates sale/purchase, allowing hoarding and crisis
2. **Dual nature of crisis**: Both problem and solution for capital accumulation
3. **Devalorisation concept**: Mechanism for eliminating obsolete productive capacity
4. **Triggers vs. causes**: Fundamental distinction in crisis analysis
5. **Finance's contradictory role**: Not source but amplifier of crises
6. **Control of labor supply**: Capital controls both demand and supply sides
7. **Expanded reproduction**: Accumulation itself creates demand for surplus-value

### Critical Analysis of Rival Theories
1. **Profit-squeeze**: Valid only for short-term phases, not sustained crises
2. **Underconsumption**: Fails to recognize accumulation as demand source, cannot explain periodicity, has reformist political implications

## Important Terminology Defined

- **Valorisation (*Verwertung*)**: Production adding new value through living labour
- **Devalorisation (*Entwertung*)**: Loss in value of capital, potentially complete
- **Fictitious capital**: Capital separated from productive base, disproportionate to original
- **Over-credit**: Credit extending beyond rational economic capacity
- **Simple reproduction**: Surplus-value entirely consumed as revenue
- **Expanded reproduction**: Capital accumulation through surplus-value reinvestment
- **Moral depreciation**: Obsolescence of means of production
- **Reserve army of labour**: Unemployed workers
