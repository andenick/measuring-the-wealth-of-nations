## Chapter 13: Lean Production and Flexibility (Conclusion)

### Pages 311-313: Part-time Work and Flexibility

The analysis demonstrates that flexibility is not beneficial for workers but only for companies. Part-time work, often presented as accommodating workers' needs (particularly women caring for children or elderly), is actually a capitalist strategy for dividing and de-unionizing the working class.

**Empirical evidence from United States (1976-1990):**
- Part-time jobs increased at 7% per annum
- Full-time jobs increased at only 2% per annum
- From 1970-1990: voluntary part-time employment increased 50%
- From 1970-1990: involuntary part-time employment increased 150%

**Cornell University study conclusion:** The growth in part-time employment stems from increased "involuntary" part-time employment, not voluntary. Explanations should focus on the demand side of the labor market (i.e., companies, corporations, capitalists).

**Reality of part-time work for workers:**
- No flexibility for workers who take jobs involuntarily
- Lower remuneration
- Reduced chances of promotion
- Loss of health insurance benefits
- Difficulty joining unions (harming entire work collective)

### Pages 312-313: The Opposite of Flexibility

The opposite of flexibility is not "rigidity" but rather the embodiment of working-class rights gained through decades and centuries of struggle, including:
- Maximum workweek reductions (40, 35, or 30 hours)
- Prohibition of child labour
- Privileges for women as oppressed section
- Limitation of employer powers regarding redundancies
- National labour laws and ILO directives

**Key argument:** Flexibility in the sale and purchase of labour power is the application of neoliberal deregulation to industrial relations. Standing against flexibility means defending rights acquired through historical struggles and fighting atomization of the working class.

Flexibility undermines unionization efforts, offering advantages to the bourgeoisie in class struggle.

### Section 8: Conclusion (Pages 313-315)

The chapter's analysis demonstrates three major points:

**First:** New business management and labour process control methods are completely consistent with capital's nature. They represent the most ruthless forms of capitalist class ambitions, not a radical change or supersession of capitalism. The concept "post-Fordism" is completely senseless. Current techniques are the logical conclusion and apex of Taylorism/Fordism, not their negation.

**Second:** All dimensions of lean production and flexibility bear the mark of class struggle between capital and labour. Class struggle is the very essence of these measures, complementary to neoliberal and globalist policies in production and labour markets. The view that these methods establish class harmony is false.

**Third:** Lean production and flexibility will be important determinants of international class struggles in the coming period. The working-class movement must study these techniques and recognize what they represent.

### Pages 314-315: Against Pessimism

**Current challenges acknowledged:**
- Capital has seized initiative worldwide
- Weakening of unions in some countries
- Division and fragmentation of working class
- Divergence of short-term interests among different class fractions
- Quasi-atomization through bourgeois attacks

**Historical perspective:**
- Conditions have never been easy
- Past: strong unions controlled by parasitic bureaucracy tied to state interests
- Past: divisions between "labour aristocracy" of imperialist countries and poorer sections
- Present: working class in imperialist countries suffers from their countries' policies
- Each period has its difficulties and possibilities

**Marxist view of history:**
The working class does not grow stronger each year to rise to power "naturally" (this is the unrealistic dream of reformists like Bernstein and late Kautsky). For Marxism, class struggle and revolution move in constant ebb and flow, sometimes falling behind from setbacks or defeats, sometimes leaping forth with unstoppable vigour. Revolution will triumph irreversibly when capitalism reveals it can no longer serve humanity's advance or meet the planet's population needs.

## Chapter 14: "New" Imperialism: Moribund Competitive Capitalism

### Page 317: Introduction

**Context:** In the age of globalization, imperialism has lost theoretical prestige. Recent theories focus on political manifestations (wars, invasions) or economic consequences (inequality, poverty) rather than underlying mechanisms.

**Author's focus:** The role of uneven economic relations between North and South in constituting the basis of political domination. Sources of domestic and international unevenness are built-in characteristics of capitalist development driven by the profit motive.

**Value transfer mechanisms:** Must be viewed as means of reproducing unevenness among capitalist economies sustained by global capital accumulation processes.

### Page 318: Critique of Harvey and Classical Imperialism

**Harvey's argument (contested):** Classical interpretations of imperialism (Bukharin, Luxemburg, Lenin) no longer have explanatory power; direction of value transfer has reversed in recent years.

**Author's counter-argument:**
- Cannot agree with Harvey's dismissal of earlier contributions
- Harvey's speculation about reversal of value transfer is theoretically and empirically unsound
- Imperialism is alive and well
- Imperialism constrains autonomous economic development in the South
- Imperialist countries continue extracting wealth from the global South (despite empirical difficulty of estimation)

**Most newer conceptualizations since Lenin:** Based on idea of power, specifically power of large monopolistic firms. Lenin's claim: capitalism had reached its final stage—monopoly capitalism—which was imperialism.

### Pages 318-320: Competition vs. Monopoly

**Baran and Sweezy reconceptualization:** Took monopoly as absence of competition; argued for inapplicability of Marx's theory of value. This has dominated most Left analyses of modern capitalism.

**Author's argument:** Competition intensified rather than disappeared as capitalism developed through centralization and concentration of capital. Claims about monopolistic capitalism rely on implied notion of free/pure/perfect competition versus earlier capitalism—a notion bearing no relation to Marx's conception of real competition as war among capital units (and among workers).

**Marx's conception of competition:**
- War-like competition operates domestically and internationally
- Central regulating process functioning through nations
- Driven exclusively by profit
- Produces cycles and crises
- Increases reserve army of labour on world scale

**Role of the state:** Has become more (not less) significant as capitalism expanded globally. Primary function: create fertile ground for profit-making, both domestically and internationally. This goes hand-in-hand with ideological framework where concepts of freedom (capital mobility) and competition are central and used fetishistically.

**David Gordon quote:** "The razor's edge of capitalist competition has sharpened: local enterprises are increasingly constrained to match the lowest-cost producers in competitive global markets or risk getting their wrists slashed."

**Contemporary ideology:** Every country must submit to dictates of world competition. Without "world-class" products, living standards will stagnate or decline catastrophically.
