## Team Work and Quality

### Knowledge Extraction from Workers

Task mobility increases worker replaceability, advantaging capital during line balancing and layoffs.

Taylorism's aim was breaking skilled worker domination over the labour process, making capital the sole ruling force. Success was achieved, but this cut capital off from benefiting from worker knowledge derived from daily experience and observation.

Ohno, like Taylor, emphasized shop floor knowledge despite living in a different historical period. Both received formation "on the bench, in production departments, immersed in production" and rose within companies on this basis.

**Ohno on the Production Plant:**
> "The production plant is manufacturing's major source of information. It provides the most direct, current, and stimulating information about management... The time that provides me with the most vital information about management is the time I spend in the plant, not in the vice president's office." (Ohno, 1988, 20)

### Functions of Team Work and Quality Circles

**Primary Function - Knowledge Extraction:**
Teams and quality circles aim above all for extracting knowledge based on experience and observation of ordinary workers on the factory floor. Team leaders and lower-level managers act as go-betweens to transmit this knowledge to top management.

The suggestion schemes are most important. Whether suggestions from workers through quality circles are accepted or not, once made, the knowledge forming the suggestion's basis is no longer solely the worker's—it becomes management knowledge as well.

Worker knowledge extends to time and motion studies. Once a worker advances a suggestion in this area, they lay the basis for a rise in work pace. In all pre-lean production labour process arrangements, workers' struggles against work pace increases were capital's fundamental problem. This represents a major gain for capital and bitter irony for the working class.

**Second Function - Savings on Variable Capital:**
Quality supervision previously executed by special operatives is now carried out by production workers under lean production. Combined with JIT, this is an important step toward "zero defect" systems.

In work environments without inventory, downstream workers determine during their own quality control that defective/faulty pieces have arrived from previous stations. Every worker must conduct quality control with great attention.

**The Concept of "Quality":**
"Quality" penetrated popular culture in late twentieth century. Consumer rights concern played a rightful part, but capital also contributed through "consumer satisfaction" in TQM training and propaganda.

Within TQM, quality is not exclusively concerned with finished products reaching final consumers. Industrial inputs used at different production process stages are equally central to the quality debate. Evidence: TQM literature's "customer" concept covers not only final product buyers but also "internal customers"—departments or work stations using inputs at subsequent production stages.

"Customer satisfaction" means department/work station satisfaction at subsequent levels—the social significance is totally different. If eliminating defective/faulty production brings extreme additional burden to workers, quality provision is realized at worker expense. Workers and unions should determine attitudes to quality accordingly and struggle to ensure quality costs are shouldered by capital, not workers.

**Third Function - Corporate Allegiance:**
Suggestion systems create the impression that workers' ideas are now esteemed at the workplace, creating the illusion that workers have power to impact the production process.

The entire "quality" hype—awards, glitter at award distribution meetings, ISO documents spreading "success" to wider ranks—triggers pride in workers for their corporations. The end result: perception of capitalist enterprise and workers as "family," exactly what capitalists sought.

Competition feelings with other companies' workers sharpen while contradictions between classes within one's own company recede to background. These tendencies developing in line with TQM nature are reinforced by more "democratic" atmospheres and ideological instruments like "company anthems" and similar ploys.

**Fourth Function - Peer Pressure:**
TQM creates peer pressure on individual workers regarding productivity and work discipline. The combination of team work and lean production leads to ironic consequences.

**Absenteeism Example:**
When a worker doesn't come to work, another isn't substituted as in previous systems. Lean production is by definition production without substitution or buffers. Although team size reduces that day, there's no reduction in previously set production quotas. The entire burden of the absent worker falls on teammates' shoulders.

Result: pressure on every worker to reduce absenteeism to minimum to avoid coworker ire. (In Japan, absenteeism is much less common than Western countries.)

More generally, every time workers shirk duty, more burden falls on colleagues. At least in Japan's form, under lean production conditions, workers exert pressure on each other toward rising labour intensity. This is the exact opposite of colleague pressure in earlier historical periods.

In the past—whether pre-Taylorist or Taylorist periods lasting until lean production—workers collectively exerted pressure on colleagues toward keeping work pace low to avoid raising general labour burdens. Those outside this sentiment were either boss darlings or asocial personalities. Under lean production, things have turned opposite.

### The Most Insidious Aspect: Conquest of Worker Mind

**Working Time vs. Leisure - Class Differences:**
From the working time/leisure relation viewpoint, urban petty-bourgeoisie and working class are in very different positions (leaving aside peasantry, whose time relationship differs totally from townsfolk).

Worker working time is, at least consequent to certain class struggle gains, defined much more clearly than petty-bourgeois time. The petty-bourgeois—whether convenience store owner keeping shops open from seven morning into late night or artisanal carpenter working long weekend hours to deliver bookcases on time—extends working hours so long as to bring alive Marx's observation that the petty-bourgeois exploits their own labour.

**Mental Relationship to Work:**
After work, workers surely bear workplace marks and, once at the pub, share anger with friends about tense overseer discussions. But under normal conditions, workers don't reflect during leisure on how work could get done better, machines kept cleaner, or how companies in dire straits could be saved. Perhaps the opposite is true: workers reflect how bosses or management can be beaten back on contentious issues.

The petty-bourgeois mind constantly drifts to work, especially if things aren't going smoothly.

**The Commodification of Labour Power:**
That workers, opposed to petty-bourgeois, have work relationships confined to working hours is entirely consistent with labour power being a commodity sold for temporary time spans to perform production activity, with time spans limited by work contracts and, nowadays, by law (at least partially).

Capitalists can use workers' bodily and mental capacity only within official workdays. Or could until lately!

**Lean Production's Change:**
Lean production introduces change through quality circles' existence. To the extent suggestion schemes systems lead workers to reflect on how production activity can be better performed at workplaces, it results in situations where working hours become theoretically unlimited (except sleeping hours) and transforms leisure time into time spent for capitalists.

Workers identify their interests with capitalists and devote, in voluntary manner, leisure time to questions of how to increase further labour productivity and labour intensity.

**This is TQM's most dangerous aspect: it becomes an instrument for the conquest of the mind of the worker.**

## 4. The Fragmentation of the Work Force

### Core vs. Periphery Division

As all observers stress, lean production systematically divides the work collective. Workers in factories where lean production is implemented are divided into two categories: core and periphery.

**The Core:**
- Composed of workers with relatively higher job security
- Relatively better economic fortunes

**The Periphery:**
- Layer devoid of job security
- Works on term contract, part-time, or as temps
- Employed by subcontractors
- Suffers much worse economic conditions

Every working class division, whatever temporary benefit this brings to layers at the spectrum's higher end, harms the entire class, including eventually those at top.

### Economic Basis of Core Layer Allegiance to Enterprise

Beyond ideological, psychological, and cultural ("enterprise culture") factors, the economic status accorded to core workers forces, so to speak, allegiance.

**First Factor - Job Security (Ironic Pride of Place):**
In the Japanese system, workers are provided lifelong employment (the nengko system). This has been underlined by majority researchers and plays important psychological roles in raising worker fidelity to enterprises.

**But the reverse of the medallion:** Because enterprises start the nengko system from the first career hierarchy rung (hiring young workers), if a worker who started working for a company resigns to seek jobs elsewhere, there's risk of not finding jobs (or finding very low quality jobs creating risk of falling from core to periphery).

This kind of system pushes workers to embrace enterprises completely.

**Second Factor - Enterprise Dependency:**
Given low levels of social services provision by the Japanese state, workers become dependent on enterprises.

Fundamental factor very effective in Japan and United States (less so in continental Europe): social security (health care, pensions, etc.) is provided by enterprises. In both Japan and United States, when workers are laid off or quit, there's no guarantee of health care provision and retirement contribution.

Housing provision to workers by some companies or educational facilities for workers' children play similar roles.

**Third Factor - Individualized Wage System:**
Worker allegiance to enterprise also has roots in extremely individualized wage systems in lean production. Part of wages depend on superior performance assessment, mostly done based on subjective factors like workers' "positive attitude."

Workers are forced, to receive decent wages, to declare allegiance to enterprises out loud. If workers are deemed to harbour "negative attitude" and seen fit only for lower wage levels, workers are coerced economically into enterprise allegiance.

**Fourth Factor - Japanese Trade Union System:**
Japanese unions are usually organized at workplace levels. Hence union and union bureaucracy futures become considerably tied to enterprise attitudes and indeed future health.

### Peripheral Layer Problems

Problems faced by peripheral working class layers are so clear as to defy need for explanation. These layers' problems will be examined within general frameworks when taking up capital's flexibility assault.

## 5. The Fragmentation of the Workplace

### Historical Context

In the long economic crisis period lasting from mid-1970s to present, one of capital's most important weapons has been fragmenting the workplace.

**Objective:** Prevent confrontation between great worker masses with single bosses or, in other words, break down organizing power of large workers' collectives.

**Forms of Fragmentation:**
1. Within same spatial locations by arranging for different worker subsets to work for different bosses: the nucleus/periphery division system
2. Division of physical production space

This second dimension moves from lean production qua series of techniques working to refine and consolidate control of labour process and workers' collectives toward the second dimension: its aspect as relationship between different capitals. But even at this early stage, this dimension takes inter-capitalist relation forms (relationship forms between different enterprises) while also intimately tied to class relations (relations between capital and wage labour).

### Changes in Company Strategies During Crisis

**Pre-Crisis (Long Post-War Boom):**
Before crisis onset, during the long post-war boom lasting approximately three decades or even since beginning of twentieth century, one fundamental tendency of monopoly capital was bringing under its control production of major inputs for final products produced by particular companies.

This was effort to bring under control as much of entire production activity chains finally producing as end results companies' major final products. This kind of capital centralization, labelled "vertical integration," was only one part of "finance capital" formation—amalgams of companies active in very different productive activity branches (from mining and manufacturing to tourism and catering) but also unproductive activities (export and import, wholesale and retail, and most importantly finance).

**Crisis Era - Two Contradictory Tendencies:**
Crisis onset from mid-1970s on brought forth two different tendencies at once—contradicting each other with net impacts changing from industry to industry.

**First Tendency - Mergers and Acquisitions:**
Mergers and acquisitions spree reached gigantic proportions from 1980s on. These operations sometimes bring all sub-branches of certain industries under same group control, thus carrying vertical integration to new heights.

**Second Tendency - Focus on Basic Competencies:**
Big corporations adopted new strategic orientation within crisis: focus on "basic competencies," specialize, then outsource many functions corporations carried out themselves in past to other companies.

This new strategic orientation was at the basis of "supply chain" models of concatenation between different capitalist producers that then became internationalized quickly.

**Historical Development:**
In the past, this model was rather peculiar to automotive industry with major brands having established very complex webs of relations with the "components industry." But now many other industries have taken this system over and supply chains have gone global.

First-rank suppliers to major brands establish, in their turn, input supply relations with other less powerful companies around the world.

**Franchising System:**
Somewhat different is the "franchising system" whereby in services, trade, media, tourism, etc., brands such as McDonald's, Benetton, Starbucks, CNN, etc. create international chains—but this time not upstream (input provision domain for factories) but downstream (merchandising, marketing, and catering areas for products).

### Consequences for Capital-Labour Relations

**Most Important Consequence - Labour Cost Reduction:**
This kind of productive structure brings down labour costs considerably. Most suppliers or franchisees are small- and medium-scale operations—environments in which capital is more successful in preventing unionization. So wages and miscellaneous labour costs are lower.

**Example:** In Japan, the difference between wage levels of main companies and suppliers has been calculated on average as 33–44% (Dohse et al., p. 118)—obviously against supplier company workers.

**Implication:** If inputs had been produced within main companies, total wage bills would have been higher and surplus-value extracted would have been that much smaller. This means this specific lean production structure accrues great advantages to capital in terms of higher surplus-value appropriation.

The additional cost arising from loss of economies of scale is dwarfed by advantages to capital provided by this special wage structure.

**Extreme Forms - Putting-Out/Cottage Industry:**
In industries such as textiles, ready-made garments, footwear, and leather products, things go so far as return to "putting-out" or "cottage industry" practices of early pre-industrial capitalism stages.

Here the principle is same as relations between large companies and "components industry," but relationships are even more lucrative for capital, since putting-out or sweatshop system workers are:
- Not only not unionized but usually unregistered
- Working without any social security
- Working long hours for starvation wages
- Many are irregular migrant workers in the most precarious state in which a working class section finds itself in this first quarter of twenty-first century

## 6. "Lean and Mean": Class Struggle Under Lean Production

### Critique of International Competition Explanation

In post-Fordism literature developed since 1980s, the standard explanation for lean production's rise is rising international competition from 1970s on. According to this explanation, Japanese companies on one hand and newly industrialized countries on the other started threatening US and European companies, which then had to turn toward lean production.

**First Problem - West-Centrism:**
Here we face first and foremost a strange, even incredible kind of West-centrism. Perhaps in strictly narrowed down frameworks, Japan and newly industrialized countries' competition might explain why Western countries adopted lean production (although even here serious problems exist).

But since the system was first implemented, as everyone admits, by Japanese companies, how is this "theory" supposed to explain lean production adoption by Japanese companies? Is it perhaps again Japanese companies' competition that also forced Japanese companies to pass over to lean production?!

Apparently, those providing these "explanations" and those taking their word for it are not even aware of what they're talking about!

**Second Problem - Abstraction from Class Struggle:**
Even more important: This explanation takes up a change that has left so many indelible marks on class struggle in abstraction from class struggle itself.

The international competition explanation tries to capture the question within competition of capitals frameworks. As matter of fact, competition is a sphere in which capital's general laws are imposed on individual capitals, in which laws in question become necessities for individual capitals.

**The Real Aim:**
The real aim of lean production methods is none other than bringing the working class under further control by capital so as to increase surplus-value amounts and raise surplus-value rates.
