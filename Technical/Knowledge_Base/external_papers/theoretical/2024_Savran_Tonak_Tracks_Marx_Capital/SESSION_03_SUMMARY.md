**Section 5: Conclusion** (pages 224-226)

The empirical findings demonstrate that Turkey's net social wage (NSW) was generally **negative** from 1980-2019, with only 5 years showing positive values. Key conclusions:

1. **Pseudo-Welfare State Confirmed**: The share of benefits to labor averaged 9.95% of GDP, while taxes from labor averaged 11.08%, resulting in average NSW of **-1.13% of GDP**.

2. **Neoliberal Austerity Pattern**: The negative NSW is consistent with neoliberal policies that:
   - Reduce collective consumption expenditures
   - Shift tax burden from capital to wage earners
   - Provide limited, arbitrary social protection
   - Increase retirement requirements (1992, 1999, 2008)

3. **Political Repression Enabled Economic Extraction**:
   - **September 12, 1980 military coup** created anti-union legal structure
   - **AKP (Justice and Development Party)** from 2002: promised democracy but became despotic
   - Unionization nearly impossible (vanguard workers immediately fired)
   - All major strikes banned for nearly a decade
   - Workers unable to fight for wages or net social wages

4. **AKP's "Pro-Poor" Myth Demolished**: Despite claims of being pro-labor and consistent with Islamic values, fiscal data shows the AKP delivered **less** to workers than it appropriated in taxes.

5. **Future Research Needed**: The concept of **"net social profit"** should be measured to show state intervention favoring capital (beyond just measuring net social wage).

### PART III: Capital and 21st Century Capitalism

**Chapter 11: Recasting Input-Output Networks in a Marxist Framework**
*Authors: Alper Duman and E. Ahmet Tonak*

**Purpose**: Show structural differences between economies using Input-Output (I-O) networks with **Counting Betweenness (CB) centrality** measures applied to Marxian-reconstructed I-O tables.

**Section 1: Explanations of the Global Crisis**

**Mainstream vs. Marxian Approaches**:

*Mainstream*: Identifies causes in policy mistakes—deregulation, debt accumulation, incorrect monetary policy. Individuals make errors that create crises.

*Marxian*: Focuses on structural aspects of the real economy above individual decisions. Crisis causes rooted in:
1. **Secular decline in profitability** (post-WWII period) due to:
   - Intense competition among capitals
   - Conflict between capital and labor
   - "Labor-shedding, productivity-increasing technologies" → generalized declining average profitability
   - **US Example**: Average rate of profit declined **48%** between 1948-1980 (Shaikh & Tonak, 1994)

2. **Neoliberal restoration attempt** (1980s-1990s):
   - Thatcher/Reagan policies to reverse declining profitability by increasing rate of surplus-value
   - **US Example**: Average profitability increased **8.3%** from 1980-1989 due to **17.9%** increase in rate of surplus-value
   - US profitability **peaked in 1997**, subsequent peaks never surpassed this level (Roberts, 2011)

3. **Growing importance of unproductive activities** (post-1980):
   - Finance and trade sectors expanded relative to productive sectors
   - Unproductive activities sustained by transferred values from productive sectors
   - Long-run dependency creates crisis vulnerability

**Underconsumption vs. Profitability Debate**:

Some Marxists/left Keynesians argue: Underconsumption (wages lagging productivity) → inability to sell goods → unrealized surplus-value → declining profitability.

**Authors' different approach**: Link structural falling rate of profit + expansion of unproductive activities → sector vulnerabilities revealed by centrality measures in I-O networks.

**Section 2: Input-Output Networks and Centralities**

**Why I-O Tables over NIPA**:
1. **Total product** (I-O) vs. **net product** (NIPA)
2. I-O tables include inputs → more insight into inter-industry relations
3. Two nations with same net product per labor unit can have different input requirements
4. Total product more informative for productivity, employment, resource use comparisons
5. Better for structural change analysis in crises across economies

**Marxian Reconstruction Needed**: Classification of sectors as productive vs. unproductive essential to understanding capitalist dynamics.

**Literature Review**:

- **Long & Plosser (1983)**: RBC framework—sectoral shocks transmitted through I-O networks affect aggregate volatility (but results depend on aggregation level)
- **C. I. Jones (2011)**: Intermediate input suppliers facing shocks affect all sectors; explains growth differentials
- **Acemoğlu et al. (2010, 2012)**: I-O network structure effects on aggregate volatility; "hubs" and asymmetric secondary link degrees; sectors as hubs propagate shocks without decay
- **Gabaix (2011)**: Power-law firm size distribution → granular firm-level shocks → aggregate volatility ("Nokia Effect")
- **Carvalho (2007)**: Propagation depends on firm and sector level interdependencies
- **Burlon (2011)**: Mixes Gabaix/Carvalho arguments—propagation depends on both firm-level (conglomerate externalities) and sector-level (substitutability/complementarity) interdependencies
- **Fisher & Vega-Redondo (2006)**: First to calculate centrality measures in cross-country I-O networks; find few central sectors (Wholesale/Retail Trade, Finance, Business Services, Motor Vehicles, Machinery); **Real Estate ranked first** in CB measure for US 2003 (107.5 vs. 57.8 for Admin/Support Services)—retrospectively significant given real estate bubble
- **Blöchl et al. (2011)**: Propose two new centrality measures for sector centralities; relate to development status

**Section 3: Input-Output Networks and the Counting Betweenness Centrality Measure**

**I-O Network Structure**:

Adjacency matrix **F** where cell F_ij = input flows from sector i to sector j.

- 10-15% of cells are zero (no interactions)
- Self-loops significant (e.g., US 2005: 64% of Motor Vehicles intermediate inputs used by same sector)

**Data Source**: OECD Stan Input-Output Tables (ISIC Rev. 3 classification)

**Why Counting Betweenness (CB) over Conventional Measures**:

Conventional centrality (shortest-path, betweenness) inadequate because:
1. I-O networks nearly complete → shortest-path doesn't make sense
2. Networks are directed and weighted
3. Self-loops important (conventional measures ignore)

**CB Measure Based on Random Walk Concept**:

- Flows of intermediate goods (one direction) + money flows back (reverse direction) = interdependencies
- **Random walk**: specifies origin and target sectors; flows leave system upon reaching target
- Probability distribution of walk depends on edge weights (out-degrees)

**Mathematical Framework**:

1. Out-strength: k_i = Σ_j a_ij
2. Transition matrix: **M** = K^(-1) **A** (normalized adjacency matrix)
3. For random walk source s → target t (t ≠ s): Probability of completion in r steps = ((M_{-t})^r)_{si}
4. Probability of path i → j completing (any steps): m_ij
5. Total walks passing edge: N^{st}_{ij} = m_ij ((I^{-1} - M_{-t})^{-1})_{si}
6. Since vertex i can be visited twice (as source and target): N^{st}_i = Σ_{j≠t} (N^{st}_{ij} + N^{st}_{ji})/2
7. **Counting Betweenness Centrality**: CB_i = [Σ_{s∈V} Σ_{t∈V-(s)} N^{st}(i)] / [n(n-1)]

**Interpretation**: Consider extra dollar used equally randomly in any sector. CB of sector i = number of times (or length of circulation) that extra dollar passes through sector i.

**Key Insight**: CB reflects significance of sectors in **amplifying or absorbing shocks** in economy.

## CHUNK 028 (Pages 271-280 / Book pages 246-255)

### Chapter 11 Section 4: Reconstruction of Input-Output Networks in a Marxian Framework

**Three Activity Categories**:

1. **Primary Activities**: Agriculture + Industry + Productive Services
2. **Secondary Activities**: Trade + Transportation
3. **Royalties and Government Sectors**: Finance + Real Estate + Public Administration

**Treatment**:

- **Secondary Activities**: Do NOT contribute to value production but conventional I-O treats as if they do. Products transferred at producers' prices; appear to sell "service" at trade margin value. Shaikh & Tonak (1994): Total value should reflect output of both Primary and Secondary. **Decision**: Keep intact (trade entirely unproductive; transportation partially unproductive; impossible to disentangle from I-O tables).

- **Royalties and Government Sectors**: Concern "Revenue Circuit"; totally non-production activities. **Decision**: **EXCLUDE** the following:
  - (i) Public Administration
  - (ii) Finance
  - (iii) Real Estate
  - (iv) Other Business Services
  - (v) Other community and personal services

**Resulting Network**: Symmetric intersectoral **value-form flows** constitute basis for Marxian network analysis.

**Coverage**: Marxian "Productive Capital Circuit"—inputs and outputs of production sectors. Intermediate input flows cover both:
- Value of fixed capital used up
- Circulating capital (raw materials, intermediate goods)
All in **value-form quantities**.

**Key Difference**: Marxian framework specifies sectors **productive** in creating value (vs. circulating or appropriating it). Elimination of unproductive sectors modifies rankings but does NOT affect sectoral interdependencies among productive sectors.

**Argument**: Money flows based on **"values"** (Marxian I-O) more important than flows based on **"market transactions"** (conventional I-O) for specifying sectors most vulnerable to global crisis.

**Visualization**: Graphical representations with vertex (sector) size adjusted to centrality measures; makes most vulnerable sectors + second-degree vulnerable sectors (directly linked) readily identifiable.

### Chapter 11 Section 6: Conclusion

**Key Findings**:

1. **Marxian distinction** (production vs. non-production activities) essential to understanding capitalist dynamics.

2. **Network analysis** of Marxian-reconstructed I-O tables reveals **different and important patterns** across countries.

3. **Classical Marxist view**: Surplus-value production = basis of unproductive activities (finance, trade). Inevitable shortage of appropriated surplus-value to support ever-growing trade/finance. Structural contradiction manifests via any triggering phenomenon.

4. **Structural Differences** in last decade before global crisis:

   - **United States**: Vulnerabilities from mostly unproductive top-ranking sectors → shortage of appropriated surplus-value likely.

   - **Germany**: Top-ranking sectors both productive and tradeable → **least affected** by crisis.

   - **Spain**: Construction dominance + high-ranking non-tradeable sectors → crisis effects **more dramatic**.

5. **Study Limitation**: Does NOT directly address relations between productive and non-productive sectors. Determines sectoral centrality for economy as whole by focusing on changes in productive sectors. Should be seen as **first study** drawing attention to crisis-triggering mechanisms in real sector via determining central sectors.

6. **Future Research**: Extend analysis to developing economies; trace structural transformations before and after global crisis.

**CHAPTER 12: Does the Digital Sector Produce Surplus-Value? The Case of Facebook**
*Author: E. Ahmet Tonak*

**Context**:
- Facebook founded 2004 (Menlo Park, California)
- Iconic social media brand (with Twitter)
- China has own platforms (WeChat, Sina Weibo—WeChat has 1 billion users, half of Facebook's)
- IPO February 2012: valued at $104 billion
- As of January 2024: ~$1.03 trillion
- Controversies: government surveillance collaboration, fake news platform, psychological attacks on self-esteem

**Central Conundrum**: How does Facebook make profit? It doesn't charge users fees.

**Revenue Sources**:
1. Advertisements run on site
2. Sale of bulk data about users to third-party vendors (targeted advertisements)

**Marxist Questions**:
- Where does surplus-value come from?
- Who are the workers?
- Who provides living productive labor extracting surplus from congealed labor and nature?
- Does Marxist analysis provide insight into Facebook/digital economy?

**Global Working Class Context** (ILO, 2020):
- Size: **3.3 billion employed** (out of 5.7 billion working-age population) in 2019
- **Largest working class in recorded history**
- No substantial evidence of dramatically shrinking workforce by automation
- Key digital economy sectors (mining, infrastructure construction, computer manufacturing) = minimal automation
- Example: Copper miners in Zambia use basic tools; printed circuit board makers in Malaysia use hand-held solder irons
- Without copper (wires) and printed circuit boards (computers), digital economy cannot function

**Digital Economy Workers**:
- Can be productive or unproductive
- Despite differences, all are working class members if they sell labor power for wages and are exploited

**Mystery**: Where in digital labor process does surplus-value get extracted?

**Clarification of "Surplus-Value"** (key Marx discovery):

Many on Left believe profit source = surplus-value. True, but Marx pointed out **TWO sources of profit**:

**1. Profit on Transfers** (trading profit / "profit on alienation" - Marx, 1969):
- Dominant in pre-capitalist times; appears in capitalism
- "Buy cheap and sell dear"
- Difference between buying and selling price = profit on transfers
- Can be appropriation of wealth (one trader's gain = another's loss)
- OR: Surplus-value produced by productive sectors **transferred** to unproductive sectors (finance)

**2. Profit from Surplus-Value**:
- **Dominant form** in capitalist system
- Extraction takes place in realm of **production** (NOT circulation/trade)
- Prerequisite: Free exchange between capitalist and worker
- Worker sells capitalist **"labor power"** (agreed hours of input)
- Purchased at market price (no cheating; socially acceptable wage)
- Amount covers cost of reproduction of labor power + dependents
- Capitalist workday designed: More labor hours than required for reproduction of labor power
- **Surplus labor time** = Total workday − Necessary labor time
- Surplus labor time = basis of **surplus-value**
- Surplus-value = essence of profit in capitalism

**Modern Capitalism**: Significant profit in **unproductive sectors** (trade, finance). No surplus-value produced there. Profit basis = surplus-value from productive sectors **transferred** via interest, rent, royalties.

**Rate of Exploitation**:
- Ratio of surplus labor time to necessary labor time
- Can be calculated for any employed wage labor (productive or unproductive)
- **Necessary labor time**: Time to produce goods equivalent to socially necessary monetary value for reproducing labor power (+ dependents)
- **Surplus labor time**: Excess working time over necessary labor time

**Productive vs. Unproductive Workers**:
- **Productive workers**: Rate of exploitation = rate of surplus-value (surplus labor time results in surplus-value extracted in production)
- **Unproductive workers**: Also exploited, but basis different. Do NOT produce surplus-value. Facilitate transfer of surplus-value from productive workers to unproductive enterprises.

**Digital Economy Surplus-Value Production**:

**Finance & Trade Companies** (Goldman Sachs, etc.):
- Do **NOT** produce surplus-value
- Appropriate other productive sectors' surplus-value via transfer mechanisms
- Example: Goldman Sachs broker fees = transfer of surplus-value, NOT production

**Environment/Service Companies** (Facebook, Google):
- Most laborers are **productive** and produce surplus-value
- Create environments (Facebook) and/or provide services (Google)
- Environments and services sold as **commodities** after modification (enriched by users' utilization)
- Sold to advertisers
- Act of selling = **realization of surplus-value**

**Determining Extent**: Surplus-value production vs. appropriation in digital economy can only be answered **empirically** by identifying production and non-production activities in companies.

### Section 1: The Political Economy of Facebook

**Platform Description**:
- Created by corporation with servers, programmers, designers, advertising executives
- **As of March 2023**: ~65,964 employees
- Produces: (1) Social interaction environment, (2) People in social interaction—marketable environment
- Both environment for social interaction AND environment for advertisers = **tangible commodities**

**Employee Labor**:
- Most Facebook employees' labor = **productive and exploited**
- Some supervisory/managerial = unproductive (as in all firms)
- Productive labouring activity produces **surplus-value**
- **Surplus-value realized** when end product (social interaction environment that is marketable) sold to advertisers

**User Characterization**:
- **As of October 2020**: 2.74 billion monthly active users
- Create accounts, post content (within Facebook's acceptable parameters)
- Users = **petty commodity producers**
- Product = profile and content
- **NOT exploited** (do not sell labor power to Facebook)
- Produce **value** but NO **surplus-value**
- **Petty commodity producer**: Production by individual (not capitalist firm + workers); individual owns means of production; produces commodities for sale; works for themselves

**Facebook Revenue**:
- **Bulk**: Digital advertising
- **Some**: Sale of user data

**Advertisers**:
- Capitalist companies
- Produce commercials to reach potential consumers
- Employees producing commercials = productive wage laborers producing surplus-value
- Advertising company **buys access** to targeted audience (people in social interaction in marketable environment) as **commodity** from Facebook
- **Realization of use-value**: User's attention → decision to purchase commodity marketed via commercial on Facebook

**Facebook's Commodities Purchased**:
- Hardware, software, infrastructure

**Non-Commodities Facebook Uses**:
- Free software
- Government-produced infrastructure
- User data (produced by petty commodity producers, not produced in capitalist fashion)

**Production Process** (Fig. 1 - Facebook and surplus-value production):

**Stage 1: Environment Production**

Facebook begins with **money capital**. Buys:
- **Means of production** (constant capital): Computers, scanners, cables, monitors, software, buildings, desks, chairs, servers, etc. (commodities)
- **Public goods**: Government-installed cable networks (non-commodities, publicly owned, but essential)
- **Labor power** (variable capital): 56,653 workers (assuming all productive; unproductive workers' wages come from surplus-value)

**Workers' Activity**:
- Combine labor effort with means of production
- Skills (server engineering, web design) produce pages for mutual interconnection
- End product = **material** (tangible life in electromagnetics; anyone with material interface—computers, phones, internet—can access)
- As material as making an automobile

**Is Environment a Commodity?**
- Appears NOT (no user monetary payment; accounts "free")
- BUT: User allowed access BECAUSE user produces content enriching Facebook's initial product toward end product
- **Exchange relation** exists between user and Facebook
- Money not exchanged, but commodity-exchange character present
- Facebook's commodity (social interaction environment) **"price"** paid **in kind** by user-supplied content
- User = petty commodity producer

**User-Supplied Content**:
- Is a **commodity**
- Contributes to production of modified Facebook product (social interaction environment with content more valuable to other users)
- More users → draws digital advertisers
- More sharply focused content → easier for Facebook algorithms to target advertisements

### Stage 2: Targetable Audience Creation

**Facebook Owns User Content**:
- Content = production of modified, newer product
- User-produced content (commodity) becomes part of Facebook's **means of production**
- Valuable input/raw material (like diamond on gold ring; diamond now defines ring)
- Facebook takes modified product (social interaction environment + users' content)
- Groups it with other content
- Packages as **targetable audience**

**Facebook's End Product**: **People in social interaction who produce a marketable environment**

- Targetable audiences sold to advertisers
- Specific access limitations: timing of availability, audience size

(iii) Distinction Marx makes (productive vs. unproductive labor) **can be made** for Facebook employees

   - Company profits
   - Wages of unproductive workers

**Core Issue**: Is end product of digital companies a **commodity** like those of other sectors?

**Answer for Facebook**: YES.
- **End product**: Targetable audience = people in social interaction producing marketable environment
- Produced through production process
- Marketed to advertisement companies
- Advertisers pay for this commodity to use for own ends

**"Immaterial Labor" Debate = Misunderstanding**:

Facebook workers develop end product (pages for mutual interconnection) serving as input to industry within overall capitalist economy. Pages have:
- **Tangible life and existence** in realm of electromagnetics
- Allow anyone with material interface (computers, phones, internet) to access platform
- **MORE material** than many service industry workers' products

**Comparison**: If singer produces end product sold as spectacle (concert) involving surplus-value production, then environment in which living concrete individuals interconnect/interact = end product = commodity with value containing surplus-value.

**Conclusion**: Facebook and, *mutatis mutandis*, all other digital companies = capitalist companies whose activity can be analyzed via **Marx's labor theory of value**.

### Section 1: The Real Subsumption of Labour Under Capital

**Epigraph** (Karl Marx):

> "A spider conducts operations which resemble those of the weaver, and a bee would put many a human architect to shame by the construction of its honeycomb cells. But what distinguishes the worst architect from the best of bees is that the architect builds the cell in his mind before he constructs it in wax. At the end of every labour process, a result emerges which had already been conceived by the worker at the beginning, hence already existed ideally." (Marx, 1976, 284)

**Production Process of Capital = Dialectical Unity**:

**Two Different Processes**:

1. **Valorisation (Self-Expansion) Process**: Geared towards expansion of capital via additional value appropriated through extracting surplus-value from workers.

2. **Labour Process**: Process in which subjective condition of production (human labor) brought together with objective conditions (means of production).

**Production Process of Capital qua Valorisation**:
- Comes about on basis of relations peculiar to capitalism
- For capital to expand: Objective conditions converted into capital; labor power converted into wage labor

**Labour Process**:
- Indispensable for existence/survival of all human societies
- Category common to human history in general
- Changes form/character depending on:
  - Level of development of productive forces
  - Relations between classes
- Under capitalism: Capital infuses labor process with **entirely different character**, changes it **revolutionarily**

**Capital's Takeover of Labour Process**:

**Dawn of Capitalism**: Capital takes over labor process in form it finds it. Cannot change productive forces and manner of producing overnight. Moulds labor process according to own needs **only over time**. Puts labor process in form adapted to increasing surplus-value and capital expansion at highest pace possible.

**Only when labor process reshaped**: Capital standing on own feet. Labor process branded with logic of valorisation process of capital.

**Two Stages of Subsumption**:

**Formal Subsumption of Labour Under Capital** (Marx, 1976, 646):
- Stage where capital uses labor process as it finds it
- Has not yet moulded that process to own needs
- Concept "form": Domination of capital limited to level of social relations peculiar to this society

**Real Subsumption of Labour Under Capital**:
- Capital succeeded to shape labor process according to own needs
- Concept "real": Capital gone beyond mere level of social relations; subordinated labor in its relation to nature
- Control by capital over labor process now **direct and from within**
- NOT one-sided, without contradiction/friction
- Environment = environment of **class struggle**
- Worker constantly tries (individually/collectively) to create breaches
- **But**: Supremacy passed to capital. Struggle continues, but over process **under capital's control**.

**Historical Development** (Western Europe, particularly Britain):

- **Late 18th century**: Industrial Revolution → growth of real subsumption begins
- **Throughout 19th century**: Advances step by step
- **Early 20th century**: Reaches maturity (imperialist stage)
- **Taylorism**: Arising at this stage, spreading rapidly → **systematisation of real subsumption**

**Acknowledgement**: Authors acknowledge debt to Harry Braverman, *Labor and Monopoly Capital. The Degradation of Work in the Twentieth Century* for fuller understanding of Taylorism's historic rise.

**History of Labour Process and Capital-Labour Relation** = History of construction of real subsumption. Capital has veritably appropriated production process = primary relationship of humanity to nature.

- Production process controlled by labor at capitalism's dawn; capital shapes from outside
- NOW: Controlled by capital directly, from within
- NOT control in vacuum; every moment filled with struggle/contradiction
- But **supremacy passed to capital**

**Establishment of Subsumption**: Does NOT come at single step but matures over various stages. Under law of combined/uneven development: Stages different in different countries. In late-coming countries, may not be possible to distinguish stages. But in Western Europe (where capitalism first arose), possible to separate stages within historical process.
