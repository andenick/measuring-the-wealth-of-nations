### SECTION 2: Explaining Crises - Mainstream Economics (Pages 344-351)

**Opening Critique** (Page 344):

> "As soon as we turn to mainstream bourgeois economics for its characterisation of crises, we immediately come face to face with the utterly ideological and apologetic character of this discipline."

**The Denialist School** (Pages 345-346):

**Say's Law** (Jean-Baptiste Say, 1767-1832):
- **Core idea**: "Law of outlets"
- **Logic**:
  - Production under capitalist division of labor → exchange
  - All production creates demand equal in magnitude to value produced
  - → All production creates its own outlet
  - → Total supply = total demand
  - → Economic cycle cannot be interrupted

**David Ricardo's Adoption**:
- Ricardo (1772-1823): Greatest classical economist (alongside Adam Smith)
- Adopted Say's Law (authors call it "vacuous tautology")
- Ricardo provided the law "pedigree" for half a century
- **Ricardo's pessimism**: Different focus
  - Land rent theory: Less fertile land → rising food prices → falling profit rate → stagnation
  - BUT: Does NOT address periodic crises

**Neoclassical Theory Continuation** (Page 345):

**Timeline**: 1870s (marginalist economics) → post-WWII (general equilibrium) → late 20th c. (rational expectations)

**Common theme**: Denial of economic crises = hallmark

**Modern version**:
- Dropped Say's Law terminology
- **New rationale**: "Smooth functioning of markets"
- **Mechanism**: Market clearing prices (demand/supply equilibrium)
- → No space for excess or insufficient demand
- **Efficient market hypothesis**: "Capitalist markets so rational and efficient they leave no space for gluts, deficits, or crises"

**Business Cycles Literature** (Page 346):

**Paradox**: How did profession reconcile crisis denial with real crises?

**Solution**: Specialized business cycles literature
- Studies "seesaw-like alternation of growth and contraction"
- Sophisticated, complicated technical tools
- **BUT**: "Never pierced through the thick ideological skin of the mainstream"
- Never questioned efficient market hypothesis

**Alternative Justifications for Real Crises** (Page 346):
1. Deny crisis exists (call it "correction" - stock exchange jargon)
2. Attribute to "external shock" (war, revolution, commodity price jumps, weather, etc.)
3. **Most common**: Blame mistakes in economic policy

**Marx's Critique** (Page 346):
> "The apologists content themselves with denying the catastrophe itself and insisting, in the face of their regular and periodic recurrence, that if production were carried on according to the textbooks, crises would never occur." (Marx, 1968, 500)

**Queen Elizabeth II Anecdote** (Page 346-347):

**Event**: Post-2008 visit to London School of Economics (LSE)

**Question to eminent economists**: "Why did no one see it coming?" (Pettifor, 2009)

**No satisfactory reply**

**Authors' observation**:
> "The duty of these eminent economists, up until the 15 September 2008 collapse of Lehman Brothers, had been to defend the impeccably rational functioning of markets in the face of criticism from their hapless less orthodox colleagues, choosing not to reply to Marxist criticism so as not to lend it legitimacy."

**Keynes and the Realist School** (Pages 347-349):

**Historical Skeptics of Say's Law**:
1. **Jean Charles Léonard de Sismondi** (1773-1842) - Swiss social critic
2. **Thomas Malthus** (1766-1834) - British conservative, Ricardo's friend
   - Correspondence with Ricardo: Could NOT convince him of Say's Law vacuity
3. **William Stanley Jevons** (1835-1882) - Marginalist school exception
4. **John Maynard Keynes** (1883-1946) - "Towering figure," major realist school representative

**Keynes' General Theory (1936)** (Page 347):

**Attack on Say's Law**: Head-on critique

**Core innovation**: Economy can reach equilibrium in MULTIPLE states:
1. Underemployment (crisis state)
2. Full employment (orthodox assumption)
3. Inflationary overheating

**"General theory" meaning**: Diversity of possible outcomes (not just one equilibrium)

**Crisis Definition** (Page 348):
> "A crisis, according to the scheme developed by Keynes, is a state in which aggregate effective demand (i.e. demand at the societal level that is backed by money) is insufficient to create full employment."

**= State of underemployment + underutilised productive capacity**

**UNDERCONSUMPTIONISM DEBATE** (Page 348):

**Misinterpretation**: Public thinks Keynes = lack of consumption power

**Underconsumptionist tradition**:
- Malthus: Structural lack of demand → need non-producing consuming class
- Broad school: Mainstream Keynesians + powerful Marxist tendency
- **Conclusion**: Overcome crisis by raising wages/benefits

**AUTHORS' CLARIFICATION**:
> "Keynes was NOT an underconsumptionist. His entire corpus of thinking was centred around the decision process of the capitalist class, focused not on consumption but on investment."

**Keynes as "false friend"**: Underconsumptionists cling to him (sometimes innocently, sometimes for policy leverage)

**Keynes' clarity**: "Investment, not consumption, was the prime mover in the functioning of the capitalist economy."

**Keynes' Distinctive Position** (Page 348):

**Admission**: Crises are part of overall economic functioning
- Drop in investment → underutilization of productive capacity + labor force

**Policy advocacy**:
1. **Monetary policy**: Interest rate manipulation (like present crisis)
2. **Fiscal policy**: Government expenditure to stimulate activity (unconventional for his time)

**Post-War Keynesianism (Mid-1940s to Mid-1970s)** (Page 349):

**"Fine-tuning" policies** + rise in state expenditure:
- Education, health, housing, transportation, social services
- **Social wage / Net social wage**: Services net of taxes paid by workers

**Illusion created**: Keynesianism = social democracy for working class benefit

**Reality**:
> "Keynes was a liberal bourgeois thinker who even advocated for a certain level of inflation in order to bring down real wages, which he alleged would make the recruitment of additional labour more attractive for capitalists, thereby reducing unemployment."

**Authors' position**: State expenditure CAN be progressive (education, health) BUT fight need not be under Keynesianism banner

**Keynes' Weakness - Investment Fluctuations** (Page 349):

**Problem**: Explanation for investment volume changes over time "leaves much to be desired"

**Keynes' approach**:
- Revolutionary monetary theory
- Evolution of money/finance = decisive factor
- Capitalists' calculations: Expected returns (marginal rate of efficiency of capital) vs. interest rate

**Ultimate determining factor**: **"Expectations"**
- Expected returns (future)
- Rate of interest (future)

**Critical question**: What determines expectations?

**Keynes' answer**: **"Animal spirits"** (Keynes, 1936, 161)

**Authors' Critique** (Page 349):

**Parallel to Marx on Ricardo**:
- Marx: Ricardo escaped to agronomy (land rent) - no economic law of motion
- Similarly: Keynes escapes to psychology ("animal spirits")
- **Reason**: Remains in circulation sphere, disregards production relations
- No theory of production → cannot determine profit rate → cannot explain accumulation pace independent of interest rate
- → Takes refuge in psychology to explain capitalism's laws of motion

**Schumpeter and Creative Destruction** (Pages 350-351):

**Joseph Alois Schumpeter** (1883-1950):

**Background**:
- Austrian by birth
- Nonconformist/maverick among 20th century economists
- Staunch capitalist defender BUT deeply influenced by Marx
- Well-rounded intellectual (unlike narrow specialists)
- **Fields**: International relations, political philosophy, sociology, economics
- "Perfect example of well-rounded intellectual of turn-of-the-century Vienna"

**Core Thesis on Business Cycles** (Page 350):

**Opening quote** from *Business Cycles* (1939):
> "Analysing business cycles means neither more nor less than analysing the economic process of the capitalist era. ... Cycles are not, like tonsils, separable things that might be treated by themselves, but are, like the beat of the heart, of the essence of the organism that displays them." (Schumpeter, 1939, v)

**Book title**: *Business Cycles: A Theoretical, Historical, and Statistical Analysis of the Capitalist Process*

**Implication**: Studying business cycle = studying capitalist process itself

**Distinction**: Sets Schumpeter apart from Keynes and all mainstream economists
- Keynes: Does NOT attribute regulator function to crisis
- Schumpeter: Business cycle/crisis = **kernel of capitalism's beauty**, propelling productive forces

**Innovation as Driving Force** (Page 351):

**Core concept**: Innovation (technical, socioeconomic, educational, etc.) = driving force of all human progress

**Capitalism's distinction**: Makes innovation **indispensable element** via crisis mechanism

**Creative Destruction Process**:
1. Crises periodically destroy previously accumulated productive forces
2. Destruction creates BOTH:
   - Need to fill evacuated space
   - Possibility of filling with NEW productive forces
3. New forces are superior quality/productivity (embody fruits of scientific research, technological application, innovation)
4. Result: Capitalism = endlessly advancing force, reaching new horizons

**Influence of Marx** (Page 351):

**Similarity**: Schumpeter called "bourgeois Marxist" (Catephores, 1994)

**Marx's prior formulations**:
- *Communist Manifesto* (1848, with Engels)
- *Capital* Vol. I (1867)
- **Thesis**: Capitalism cannot exist without "constantly revolutionising" means of production

**Influence on Schumpeter**: Obvious

**Unknown**: Whether Schumpeter knew Marx envisioned crisis as destruction → new productive means
- Marx's crisis remarks scattered across many writings
- Most not published by 1939 (when Schumpeter wrote *Business Cycles*)

**Ideological Divergence** (Page 351):

**For Marx**: Constant revolutionising → preparation for socialism

**For Schumpeter**: Everlasting benefit capitalism brings humanity

**Ideological operation (two aspects)**:

1. **Absolution**: Making crisis serve humanity's progress absolves capitalism's most undesirable aspect
2. **Justification**: Provides rationale for devastation caused by crises
