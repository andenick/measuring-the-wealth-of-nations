## Figure 4: Interest Rate Policy of Central Banks of Advanced Countries, Q1 2008 to Q3 2018

**Page**: 342 (362)

**Source**: Kuznetsova et al., 2019, 117–135

### Visual Description
- Line chart showing interest rate policies over approximately 10 years
- Four central banks tracked with different line styles:
  - **Fed** (Federal Reserve) - solid line
  - **ECB** (European Central Bank) - dotted line
  - **BoE** (Bank of England) - dash-dot line
  - **BoJ** (Bank of Japan) - dashed line
- Y-axis: Interest rates from -0.5% to 5.5%
- X-axis: Time period from Q1 2008 to Q3 2018 (quarterly intervals)

### Key Observations

1. **Initial Period (Q1 2008 - Q1 2009)**: Sharp decline in rates
   - All central banks started with rates between 3.5% and 5.5%
   - Dramatic coordinated reduction in response to 2008 financial crisis
   - Fed and ECB dropped most rapidly

2. **Zero Interest Rate Period (2009-2015)**:
   - Most rates stabilized near zero or just above
   - Fed maintained near-zero rates longest
   - ECB went below zero (negative rates) around 2014-2015
   - BoJ also moved to negative territory

3. **Divergence (2015-2018)**:
   - Fed began raising rates from late 2015 onwards
   - By Q3 2018, Fed reached approximately 2%
   - ECB and BoJ remained at or below zero
   - BoE showed modest increase to around 0.75%

### Theoretical Context
This figure illustrates the unprecedented monetary policy response to the 2008 global financial crisis. The text notes that this "policy of cheap money create liabilities in the long run, as expressed in the snowballing debt of the major imperialist economies (see Fig. 6)."

### Data Points Referenced
- 2008 Q1: All rates above 3%
- 2009: Sharp drop to near-zero
- 2014-2015: ECB and BoJ move to negative rates
- 2015-2018: Fed diverges upward while others remain low
- 2018 Q3: Fed ~2%, ECB ~0%, BoE ~0.75%, BoJ ~0%

## Figure 5: Total Government Expenditure in the United States and Eurozone Countries, Q1 2006 to Q3 2014

**Page**: 342 (362)

**Source**: Tooze, 2018, 355

### Visual Description
- Line chart comparing government spending in two regions
- Two lines:
  - **United States** (black line, upper)
  - **Eurozone** (gray line, lower)
- Y-axis: Scaled to 2008 Q1 = 100 (index)
- X-axis: Time period from Q1 2006 to Q3 2014 (quarterly)

### Key Observations

1. **Pre-Crisis Period (2006-2008)**:
   - Both started below 100 (index baseline)
   - United States: approximately 96-97
   - Eurozone: approximately 96-97
   - Gradual upward trend in both regions

2. **Crisis Response (2008-2010)**:
   - Sharp acceleration in government spending
   - United States rose more steeply to peak around 111-112
   - Eurozone also increased but to lower peak around 108
   - Peak reached around Q1 2010 for both

3. **Post-Crisis Divergence (2010-2014)**:
   - **United States**: Maintained high spending levels with slight volatility
     - Fluctuated between 107-111
     - Ended around 110 in Q3 2014
   - **Eurozone**: Declined significantly (austerity)
     - Dropped from 108 to approximately 103-104
     - Much more conservative fiscal stance

### Theoretical Context
This figure demonstrates the different fiscal policy responses to the 2008 crisis. The United States pursued expansionary fiscal policy (pump-priming), while the Eurozone, after initial stimulus, shifted to austerity measures. This relates to the chapter's discussion of Keynesian policies and government intervention during crises.

### Data Points Referenced
- 2008 Q1: Both at baseline (100)
- 2010 Q1: US peak ~111, Eurozone peak ~108
- 2014 Q3: US ~110, Eurozone ~103-104

## Figure 6: The G7's Debt-to-GDP Ratio, 1950–2012

**Page**: 343 (363)

**Source**: OECD

### Visual Description
- Area/silhouette chart showing debt accumulation over 62 years
- Filled area shows debt-to-GDP ratio for G7 countries aggregate
- Y-axis: Debt-to-GDP ratio (percentage), ranging from 0 to 120%
- X-axis: Years from 1950 to 2012

### Key Observations

1. **Post-WWII Decline (1950-1974)**:
   - Started very high (approximately 110-120% in 1950)
   - Steady decline over 24 years
   - Reached lowest point around 1974 at approximately 35-40%

2. **Golden Age Low Point (1974)**:
   - Minimum debt ratio of approximately 35-40%
   - Corresponds to end of post-war boom period

3. **Gradual Rise (1974-2007)**:
   - Slow, steady increase over 33 years
   - Rose from 35-40% to approximately 70-75%
   - Relatively stable growth pattern

4. **Crisis Spike (2007-2012)**:
   - Dramatic acceleration in just 5 years
   - Rose from ~70% to over 120%
   - Steepest increase in entire period
   - **Surpassed even post-WWII debt levels**

### Theoretical Context
The text emphasizes the historical significance: "Extraordinarily high levels of debt, which surpassed the 120% mark in 2012, signal the highest public indebtedness in the entire history of the capitalist mode of production, breaking the record of wartime indebtment."

The figure illustrates the long-term consequences of the monetary and fiscal policies shown in Figures 4 and 5.

### Data Points Referenced
- 1950: ~110-120% (post-WWII peak)
- 1974: ~35-40% (lowest point)
- 2007: ~70% (pre-crisis)
- 2012: >120% (exceeds wartime levels)

### Additional Context from Text
- By April 2023, US national debt reached $31 trillion (124% of GDP)
- Eurozone debt-to-GDP ratio: 97.7% (varies by country)
- Mediterranean countries (Greece, Italy) much higher
- Northern Europe countries lower

## Figure 7: US Private Debt-to-GDP (%), 1951–2014

**Page**: 344 (364)

**Source**: Carchedi & Roberts, 2018: 321

### Visual Description
- Line chart showing private corporate debt as percentage of GDP
- Single black line tracking debt ratio over 63 years
- Y-axis: Percentage of GDP, ranging from 30% to 100%
- X-axis: Years from 1951 to 2014 (marked in intervals)

### Key Observations

1. **Post-War Stability (1951-1965)**:
   - Started around 37-40%
   - Remained relatively flat with minor fluctuations
   - Slight upward drift

2. **Gradual Acceleration (1965-1985)**:
   - Slow increase from ~42% to ~52%
   - Steady upward trend over 20 years
   - One noticeable dip around mid-1970s

3. **Financial Expansion Era (1985-2001)**:
   - Accelerating growth
   - Rose from ~52% to ~78%
   - Steeper slope than previous periods
   - Brief plateau around 1991-1992

4. **Pre-Crisis Surge (2001-2008)**:
   - Dramatic acceleration
   - Rose from ~78% to peak around 92-93%
   - Steepest increase in entire series
   - Reflects housing/credit bubble

5. **Financial Crisis Adjustment (2008-2011)**:
   - Sharp correction
   - Dropped to approximately 82-84%
   - Deleveraging period

6. **Post-Crisis Recovery (2011-2014)**:
   - Resumed upward trend
   - Rose from ~84% to ~90-92%
   - Approaching pre-crisis levels

### Theoretical Context
The text notes: "It is important to note that public debt is not the only burden facing the economies of imperialist countries: private corporate debt also rose immensely both before and after the global financial crisis (see Fig. 7)."

This figure complements Figure 6 by showing that debt accumulation was not limited to government but also pervaded the private sector, creating a dual debt burden on the economy.

### Data Points Referenced
- 1951: ~37-40%
- 1985: ~52%
- 2001: ~78%
- 2008: ~92-93% (peak)
- 2011: ~82-84% (post-crisis low)
- 2014: ~90-92%

### Pattern Analysis
The figure shows three distinct phases:
1. **Stable capitalism** (1951-1965): Low, stable debt
2. **Financial deepening** (1965-2000): Gradual financialization
3. **Bubble era** (2000-2008): Explosive debt growth leading to crisis
4. **New normal** (2008-2014): High debt persists despite crisis

## Summary of Figures

All four figures work together to illustrate the chapter's argument about:
1. **Monetary intervention** (Fig. 4): Unprecedented zero/negative interest rate policies
2. **Fiscal intervention** (Fig. 5): Government spending expansion (US) vs. austerity (Eurozone)
3. **Public debt consequences** (Fig. 6): Historic debt levels exceeding wartime peaks
4. **Private debt burden** (Fig. 7): Corporate debt crisis paralleling public debt

Together, they document the "snowballing debt" and "liabilities in the long run" created by post-2008 crisis policies, supporting the text's argument that "a decade and a half after the 2008 Lehman Brothers moment, the world economy is still in a sorry state."
