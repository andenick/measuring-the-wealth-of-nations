## CHAPTER 11: RECASTING INPUT-OUTPUT NETWORKS IN A MARXIST FRAMEWORK

**Authors**: Alper Duman and E. Ahmet Tonak

### Introduction (Page 233)

The purpose of this study is to show the differences between structural changes in different economies by treating Input-Output (I-O) tables as weighted directed networks and applying the best suited centrality measure to those networks. This is important especially in the context of the ongoing global economic crisis, which continues to be a challenge for economists. The challenge manifests itself not only with respect to the identification of the causes of the crisis, but also in connection to the explanation of the divergence in the levels of susceptibility of different economies to a generalised downturn.

Our theoretical premise is that the Marxian distinction between production and non-production activities is essential to understanding the dynamics of capitalism. Therefore, our attempt to explore structural changes must include a sectoral reclassification based on that distinction. The latter choice distinguishes our analysis from other studies of structural change based on I-O tables. We argue that network analysis of I-O tables reconstructed in a Marxian framework reveals different and important patterns across countries.

Our focus is to explain whether some countries have, relative to others, potentially more vulnerable sectors that would aggravate the downturn triggered by the global crisis. In order to do that, we go beyond merely illustrating interdependencies among sectors and use complex network analysis to construct a measure of the vulnerabilities arising from such interdependencies.

### Methodological Framework (Page 234)

Conventional I-O analysis (Hirschmann, 1958; Leontief, 1986) provides analytical tools which can derive the effects of a change in one sector on the whole aggregate economy. These tools are mostly based on matrix operations which result in conventional measures such as backward and forward linkages of each sector. These measures reflect the extent of the downstream and upstream interdependencies of the sectors.

Complex network analysis improves upon these analytical tools. Random walk centrality and the counting betweenness centrality measures provide a sound framework for understanding the potential effects of an exogenous supply or demand shock (randomly and uniformly distributed over the whole economy) on each sector. Thus, these measures, especially the counting betweenness centrality measure, can be better in specifying the most vulnerable sectors given the global crisis. (Jackson, 2010; Newman, 2011).

### Paper Structure (Page 234)

The paper has three sections:

1. **Section 1**: We first briefly discuss various explanations of the global economic crisis and the literature of I-O networks and centrality.

2. **Section 2**: Focusing on methodological issues, we present a relatively new measurement of centrality, i.e. the counting betweenness centrality measure, and reconstructed I-O networks based on the distinction between productive and unproductive activities.

3. **Section 3**: We apply our framework to the economies of the United States, Germany and Spain and identify their central sectors in two different periods. The empirical illustration of the framework in a comparative and dynamic context enables us to speculate on the potentialities of our approach for further research in understanding the path to the crisis.

## SECTION 1: EXPLANATIONS OF THE GLOBAL CRISIS (Pages 235-237)

### Mainstream vs. Marxian Perspectives (Page 235)

As far as the causes of the economic crisis are concerned, the main difference between mainstream and Marxian economics is the primacy each perspective attaches to, respectively, policy mistakes and structural factors. Broadly speaking, mainstream economists identify the causes of the present global crisis in the realm of policy making, be it deregulation, debt accumulation, incorrect monetary policy, etc. Put differently, individuals (including political leaders, technocrats, advisers, etc.) make mistakes that in turn produce bottlenecks and imbalances, which themselves lead to national and global crises, depending upon the level of "incorrectness" and the sphere of influence of such policies.

Marxian economists, on the other hand, to identify the causes of crises have traditionally focused on structural aspects of the real economy, which are above and beyond any given individual's decisions. Obviously, at specific junctions those structural characteristics of any advanced economy may be affected by economic actors' behaviour. Nevertheless, such behaviour can also be seen because of competition among units of capital and conflictual relations between labour and capital, which are intrinsic to the very structure of the capitalist economy.

### The Authors' View on Crisis Causes (Pages 235-236)

In this chapter, without going into the detailed discussion of differences among Marxian economists, we simply state our view regarding the causes of crisis. In our opinion, the leading advanced countries had started to experience a secular decline in their average profitability due to intense competition among units of capital and to the conflict between capital and labour during most of the post-World War II period. Such a fierce competitive period forced individual units of capital to mechanise in order to decrease unit cost by way of increasing productivity so as to survive. However, the overall effect of "labor-shedding and productivity-increasing new technologies" was a generalised decline in average profitability, hence the crises of the 1970s throughout the advanced capitalist countries (Carchedi, 2011). For example, in the case of the US economy the average rate of profit declined approximately by 48% between 1948 and 1980 (Shaikh & Tonak, 1994).

An attempt was made, initially by Thatcher and Reagan and later by many other neoliberal governments, to reverse this trend of declining profitability by increasing the rate of surplus-value. Again, for example in the case of the US economy, average profitability increased by 8.3% from 1980 to 1989 mostly due to a 17.9% increase in the rate of surplus-value. Based on such restoration of profitability, neoliberal policies seemed successful. US profitability peaked in 1997 and subsequent peaks have not surpassed the level of 1997 (Roberts, 2011). However, this period also intensified the relative rise in the proportion of unproductive (e.g. finance and trade) to productive activities (manufacturing, mining, etc.).

From the Classical and Marxian point of view such unproductive activities can only sustain themselves by receiving transferred values that have been created by productive sectors. Hence, both causes of the current global crisis are structural in nature: first, especially for the pre-1970 period, declining profitability as a result of competition and class conflict forced productivity increases and, for the post-1980 period, as productive sectors became less profitable, there was the growing importance of unproductive activities, which rely for their good functioning on productive sectors in the long run.

### Underconsumptionist Explanations (Page 236)

As mentioned in the introduction above, highlighting income distribution inequalities in crisis analysis is common among radical economists. It is clear that, in the development of capitalism, wage increases sometimes lag behind the increase in labour productivity. In itself, this situation worsens income distribution, and its net effect may lead to a decrease in total consumption. According to some Marxists, this type of underconsumption is the main reason behind capitalist crises in general, including today's crisis. Interestingly, this view does not entirely deny the observed decline in average profitability in developed capitalist countries. These underconsumptionist Marxists propose that the decline in profitability occurs as an inevitable consequence of the underconsumption of goods and services and the consequent inability to sell them completely, or, to put it in Marxist terminology, the failure to realise surplus-value. This approach is consistent with the explanation given to the crisis by left Keynesians as well as by some radical economists (for example, Sweezy and Baran), who analyse the tempo and crisis of the capitalist economy by focusing on aggregate demand as a tradition.

**Footnote 1** (Page 236): It is a fact that, from time to time, wage increases stay behind rising labour productivity. This implies a worsening of income distribution, which in turn may produce a decline in overall consumption. For some Marxists, such a phenomenon of underconsumption is the main cause behind a generalised crisis, including the current one. Interestingly enough, this view does not completely deny an observable decline in average profitability throughout capitalist countries. It simply identifies such a decline as an inevitable result of unsold consumption goods and services, i.e. in Marxian terminology inability to realise surplus-value. The latter obviously suggests a decline in the average rate of profit. Traditionally, such an approach seems consistent with those explanations of crisis by certain radical economists, as well as Keynesians, who also tend to focus on aggregate demand problems in capitalist economies.

### Authors' Alternative Approach (Page 237)

Our approach is different from these explanations. We would rather link the structural tendency of the falling rate of profit in an ongoing process of expansion of unproductive activities to the vulnerability of sectors determined by the relevant measures of centrality applied to the I-O networks.

## SECTION 2: INPUT-OUTPUT NETWORKS AND CENTRALITIES (Pages 237-238)

### Rationale for Using I-O Networks (Page 237)

We think that analysis of I-O networks, which reflect the complex interrelations of the main sectors in the advanced countries, can reveal important clues to an understanding of the origins and the development of the global crisis. In this respect, a Marxian framework can facilitate attaining a relevant perspective in which real and financial causes can be looked into separately.

### I-O Tables vs. NIPA (Page 237)

It is worth mentioning the reasons behind our choice of I-O tables rather than the more commonly used National Income and Product Accounts (NIPA). As is commonly known, I-O accounts keep track of total product rather than net product, which is the case of NIPA. The fact that I-O tables include the portion of the product used as inputs by the various industries assures that they contain more insight about the structure of inter-industry relations. Moreover, especially when it is a question of comparing economies, total product is a more informative measure than net product. Two nations with the same net product per unit labour can have different input requirements. Focusing on net product alone would then be quite misleading when considering national productivity, employment, resource use, etc. Because our focus is on structural changes in the context of crises within more than one economy, I-O accounts are more suitable to the task for this reason as well.

### Sectoral Reclassification (Page 237)

Since we think that classifying economic activities (and sectors) as productive and unproductive is important in identifying the rhythm of structural changes in capitalist economies, regrouping sectors within I-O tables is needed. This was done based on the methodology proposed by Shaikh and Tonak (Shaikh & Tonak, 1994).

### Literature Review on I-O Networks (Page 238)

Recent literature has linked structural features of economic transactions networks (including I-O networks) to the aggregate economic performances Black (1987). Long and Plosser (1983) demonstrate that transmission of sectoral shocks through I-O networks can have important overall effects on aggregate output volatilities. However, their results in the Real Business Cycles (RBC) framework largely depend on the level of aggregation of sectors and sub-sectors. As the number of sectors increase the decay factor also increases (Acemoğlu et al., 2012).

C. I. Jones (2011) focuses on the critical role of intermediate inputs flowing across sectors in an I-O network. As generic intermediate input supplier sectors face idiosyncratic shocks, and these shocks are transmitted through the intermediate input links, almost all the sectors will be affected. The results can explain a great deal of the growth differentials across countries.
