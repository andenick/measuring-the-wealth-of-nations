# The Architecture of Capital - Four Grand Levels of Abstraction

**Source**: Savran & Tonak (2024), Chapter 3, Section 5 (pp. 46-51)

## Overview

Marx's *Capital* is structured on **four grand levels of abstraction**, moving from the abstract to the concrete through progressive relaxation of analytical constraints.

## The Four Grand Levels

### Level 1: Production in General
- **Location**: Outside *Capital* proper - foundational level
- **Content**: Shared attributes of production across ALL modes of production in human history
- **Theoretical basis**: Materialist conception of history (*The German Ideology*, "Preface" to *Contribution*)
- **Function**: Foundation/substratum - "like the foundation of a building"
- **Relationship to capitalism**: Necessarily present in capitalism but provides no clues to its specific nature
- **Analogy**: "Part of the building, but outside of the habitable space. By itself, it does not serve any immediate purposes, but without it, the whole edifice would collapse"

### Level 2: Exchange in General
- **Location**: *Capital* Volume I, Part I ("Commodities and Money")
- **Analysis**: The commodity in isolation
- **Key concepts**:
  - Use-value and value
  - Concrete labour and abstract labour
  - Social division of labour + private property
  - Value relation and money as universal equivalent
- **Abstraction**: Isolates exchange relations from capital-wage labour relationship
- **Theoretical achievements**:
  - Foundation for law of value (labour theory of value)
  - Foundation for theory of money
  - (Both receive further elaboration in subsequent volumes)
- **Why commodity first?**: Because labour power becomes a commodity under capitalism - must understand commodity before understanding capital-wage labour relation

### Level 3: Capital in General
- **Location**: Volume I (except Part I-II) and Volume II
- **Analysis**: Capital-wage labour relationship in isolation
- **Key abstraction**: Isolates from **competition between capitals**
- **What is frozen/held constant**:
  - Relations between different fractions of capital (industrial, commercial, financial, landed property)
  - Relations between different units of capital within same fraction
- **Result**: "Laboratory conditions" for studying the two main classes
- **Common misconception**: NOT that all capitals have identical organic composition
- **Actual method**: Abstracts from IMPACT of competition, not from different compositions
- **Focus**: "Relationship between the two main *dramatis personae* of the capitalist mode of production"

#### Volume I Coverage
- **Part III-**: Production process of capital
- **Focus**: How surplus-value is produced through exploitation of wage labour
- **Key categories**: Constant capital, variable capital, surplus-value, rate of surplus-value

#### Volume II Coverage
- **Focus**: Circulation process of capital
- **Key categories**: Turnover time, reproduction schemas, circuit of capital

### Level 4: Many Capitals
- **Location**: Volume III - "The Process of Capitalist Production as a Whole"
- **Analysis**: Competition between different fractions and units of capital introduced
- **Method**: Constraint on competition is relaxed
- **New phenomena analyzed**:
  - Struggle between different fractions of capital
  - Struggle between units within same fraction
  - Interaction of capital-wage labour struggle WITH inter-capital competition
- **Result**: "Concrete functioning of a capitalist society"
- **Why "as a whole"?**: Only now do we see interaction of class struggle + competition

## Key Methodological Points

### 1. Rising from Abstract to Concrete
- Abstract categories firmly grounded first
- Then progressive movement to more concrete categories
- Each level INCORPORATES previous levels, not replaces them

### 2. Transformation vs. Abandonment
**Critical insight**: Volume III does NOT abandon Volume I

- Phenomenal forms are **modified forms** of essential forms
- **Metamorphosis**, not replacement
- Volume I essential forms: value, surplus-value
- Volume III phenomenal forms: prices of production, profit (of enterprise), commercial profit, interest, ground rent

**The relationship**:
- Competition "necessarily modifies the qualitative as well as quantitative relations" of Volume I
- Values **converted into** prices of production
- Surplus-value **resolves itself into** various profit forms
- Phenomenal forms **conceal** essential forms from "unsuspecting eye"
- BUT: phenomenal forms are **generated by** metamorphosis of essential forms
- Transformation **subsumes and conserves** (*aufhebung*) essential forms

### 3. Not Two Systems, One Reality
**Against the "transformation problem" misunderstanding**:

- FALSE: "Value system" vs. "price system" as two different realities, "two different substances"
- TRUE: One reality at different levels of abstraction
- Phenomenal forms are "necessary outward forms of appearance" of essential forms

### 4. Historical Proof of Method's Coherence
- Marx wrote Volume III sections in 1864-1865
- Published Volume I in 1867
- Therefore: He KNEW about value/price divergence from the beginning
- This was not a "difficulty" to be patched up
- It was the **contradictory structure of capitalist reality itself**

### 5. Marx's Famous Quote
> "All science would be superfluous if the form of appearance of things directly coincided with their essence!" (Marx, 1991, p. 956)

## Method of Investigation vs. Method of Presentation

### Method of Investigation
- **Stage**: Before systematic presentation
- **Activities**: Collect, sort out, classify, categorize, put in order, relate empirical data
- **Goal**: Find the logic to order and relate different elements
- **Then**: Can proceed to abstraction

### Method of Presentation
- **Starts with**: Abstract categories firmly grounded
- **Process**: "Rises from the abstract to the concrete"
- **Appearance**: May seem deductive, "as from first principles"
- **Reality**: Relations discovered during investigation, now presented in logical order
- **Dialectical feature**: Contradictions solved by "only logically possible resolution" leading to higher concreteness

## Definition of Abstraction

**Abstraction** is:
> "The method used in the study of the forms or relationships that turn out, in the phase of investigation, to be determining for the totality **in isolation from all incidental, extraneous, arbitrary, secondary or derivative elements** so as to bring out the innermost structure of those central forms and relationships."

**Key insight**: Abstraction is not inherently good or bad
- Depends on **context**
- If abstraction comes at right moment + object chosen well = indispensable
- If wrong moment/object = problematic

## Practical Application: The Commodity Example

**Concrete aspect**:
- First sentence of *Capital*: "The wealth of those societies in which the capitalist mode of production prevails, presents itself as an immense accumulation of commodities"

**Abstract aspect**:
- Isolates commodity even from capital-wage labour relation at first stage
- Why? Because investigation revealed: labour power IS a commodity
- Therefore: Must understand commodity before understanding capital-wage labour

## Implications for Reading Capital

1. **Cannot understand parts in isolation**: Each volume depends on others
2. **Must track level of abstraction**: Different rules apply at different levels
3. **Cannot skip to Volume III**: Would miss essential forms underlying phenomenal forms
4. **Volume I not "outdated" by Volume III**: Essential forms remain operative
5. **Architecture provides "plan of the edifice"**: Makes navigation possible
6. **Like mathematical treatise**: Rigorous logical structure, not loose collection

## Summary Table

| Level | Location | What is Studied | What is Abstracted From | Key Achievement |
|-------|----------|----------------|------------------------|-----------------|
| 1. Production in General | Outside *Capital* | Universal production attributes | Specific modes of production | Materialist foundation |
| 2. Exchange in General | Vol I, Part I | Commodity, value, money | Capital-wage labour | Law of value, theory of money |
| 3. Capital in General | Vol I-II (mostly) | Capital-wage labour relation | Competition between capitals | Theory of surplus-value, exploitation |
| 4. Many Capitals | Vol III | Complete capitalist system | Nothing (most concrete) | Prices of production, profit forms |
