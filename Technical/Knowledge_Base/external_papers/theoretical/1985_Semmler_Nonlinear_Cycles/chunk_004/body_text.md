**1. INTRODUCTION**

The analysis of capitalist economic dynamics is not a simple matter. Capitalist economies produce growth, business cycles, and from time to time major crises which threaten their very existence. Any attempt to examine the logic of these dynamics has to be forgiven for making simplifying assumptions and conveniently neglecting part of the story. What is striking about much of contemporary dynamic theory, however, is its great difficulty in providing convincing explanations of the conjunction of growth and cycle. In the basic multiplier-accelerator model, as Pasinetti (1974, pp. 54-82) has clearly shown, growth and cycles are mutually exclusive alternatives. To have both, some exogenous factor must intervene. The same is true for "Keynesian" econometric models, the solutions to which produce cycles only if hit by random shocks. The currently fashionable "equilibrium" business cycle theory of the Lucas (1975) variety is also subject to this embarrassment, since it derives cycles from the accelerator mechanism, but has nothing to say about growth.

In this light the work of Richard Goodwin (1967) seems quite remarkable. There he constructed a model which generates growth and cycles without appealing to exogenuous shocks or trend growth in technique. He was able to do so while using some very simple assumptions: fixed coefficients of production, full employment of capital, investment determined by savings, and wages determined by a Marxian reserve army mechanism. The basic dynamic of the process is easy to describe. As accumulation of capital proceeds, the rate of employment increases. Once the rate of employment has crossed a threshold level, the real wage begins to rise. This lowers the rate of profit and therefore the

Goodwin was able to show that the system he was describing could be reduced to two differential equations in labor's share and the rate of employment. These equations reproduce the Lotka-Volterra system, the solution to which is well known. This system generates limit cycles around a fixed point and is therefore an inspired representation of an economy which generates growth and cycles from its internal functioning. There are, however, some limitations to its use. The most significant of these is that the equations are difficult to modify. Integrals for systems which are similar to, but not precisely like, the Lotka-Volterra system are not easy to obtain. Hence extension of the growth cycle framework to include analysis of phenomena such as effective demand requires some additional work.

A number of authors have developed various aspects of Goodwin's model. Desai (1973) and Vellupillai (1983) have looked at models which allow for variable capital-output ratios; and in Vellapulai's work a Kaldorian investment function was included. In a very insightful paper Medio (1981) has reformulated the growth cycle model to allow for variable capital-output ratios, but without an explicit investment function.

The purpose of this paper is to extend this type of analysis by combining the reserve army explanation of wage shares with a Keynesian model in which there is a technologically given maximum rate of growth. **2. A DYNAMIC AGGREGATE MODEL**

In the model to be studied we wish to allow output and employment to be determined by effective demand, and income distribution to be determined by a Marxian reserve army mechanism. Because the relevant mathematics extends only to two variables, it is necessary to confine the discussion to a single sector economy. Let Y = real GNP, K = real capital stock, and I = real gross investment. Capacity utilization is then determined by the Keynesian relation

Y/K = I/sK = g/s                                    (1)

where s is the average (and marginal) propensity to save and K is assumed to be non-depreciating.

In order for (1) to be useful, it is necessary to say something about what determines the rate of investment expenditure, g. We will assume that g is determined by the possibility of profit, taking account of two points. The first is the idea that even if the current rate of accumulation were low, it would begin to increase if labor's share fell far enough. The reasoning behind this is that a sufficiently large profit share will make new sets of projects profitable. This seems like an acceptable way to explain recovery from non-crisis downturns in accumulation. Second, it will be assumed that there is a maximum rate of growth for the economy which cannot be exceeded, even

---

### PAGE 255

if workers could live on air and all net income went to profits. A differential equation for the movement of g, consistent with these ideas, is given by

ġ/g = A + Bg - Cg² - F/(D + E ω)                    (2)

where all coefficients are greater than zero, A > DF, and where ω is labor's share in GNP.

Given the share of wages, a higher rate of accumulation means a higher rate of capacity utilization. For any potential addition to productive capital, this means higher profits. Given a rate of utlization, a lower wage share also means higher profits. Thus equation (2) ties accumulation decisions to currently realizable profits. And since future profits cannot be known with certainty, it includes the effects of expectations, at least to the extent that they are determined by current profits. The inclusion of the g² term is an easy way to represent the existence of a maximum growth rate. The requirement that A > DF guarantees that once g is positive, it will not collapse to zero if ω is small enough. It is useful to note here that Weisskopf's (1979) study of non-financial corporate profits in the post-war U.S. economy indicates that profitability was primarily determined by capacity utilization and labor's share, with relatively little change in the capacity output-capital ratio.

To determine the level of ω the model relies on a representation of the reserve army mechanism. In Volume I of Capital Marx suggested that the level of the real wage, which in the long run was determined by the socially defined costs of reproducing workers, varied directly with the rate of employment. As employment increased and the competition among workers for available job decreased, employers were forced to pay higher wages. Downturns reduced the strength of labor relative to employers by increasing the size of reserve army of the unemployed, and wages declined. In the work of Goodwin (1967), this mechanism is described by

In our representation of the reserve army mechanism, it will be assumed that the rate of employment is adequately described as an increasing function of the rate of growth, g. Since g determines both capacity utilization and the growth of capacity, this is not an implausible assumption. Thus in place of Goodwin's equation (3) we have

ω̇/ω = (ag - ε)(1 - ω)                              (4)

There is no avoiding the fact that this formulation does not specifically account for labor force growth. In the work of Goodwin, Desai and Vellupilai it is assumed to grow at a constant rate. This is one point on which the simulation model will allow us to be more precise.

**3. STABILITY PROPERTIES AND LIMIT CYCLES**

The next order of business is to discuss the stability properties of the fixed point H in Figure 1. To do so we make use of the linearization theorem for non-linear differential equation systems (Arrowsmith and Place, 1982, pp. 82-91). That theorem states that if

---

### PAGE 257

a non-linear system of ordinary differential equations has a simple fixed point, then in a neighborhood of that fixed point the phase portrait of the system is qualitatively equivalent to a linearized approximation, provided the linearized system is not a center. Hence, following the discussion in Arrowsmith and Place, we need only examine the matrix

M = [∂ġ/∂g    ∂ġ/∂ω]
    [∂ω̇/∂g   ∂ω̇/∂ω]                              (5)

(evaluated at the fixed point) to examine the stability of a fixed point. If |M| > 0 and M has non-zero eigenvalues, the fixed point will be simple. Calculation shows that

M = [2Bg-3Cg² - F/(D+Eω)    -E(Ag+Bg²-Cg³)/(D+Eω)²]
    [a(ω - ω²)              (1-2ω)(ag-ε)]           (6)

Since at the fixed point H the lower right element of M is equal to zero and the upper right element of M is some negative multiple of E, |M| will be greater than zero as long as ω <1. Since this is a necessary condition for the functioning of any capitalist system, we can assume that the values of the coefficients of equations (2) and (4) guarantee it. The eigenvalues of M at point H are given by

λ = (z ±√(z² + r))/2                                (7)

where

z = (A + 2Bg - 3Cg²)/(D+Eω) - F                    (8)

and

r = 4|M|                                            (9)

Hence the eigenvalues can never be zero. The fixed point is therefore simple.

**4. THE IMPORTANCE OF ANIMAL SPIRITS**

Although it may not be immediately apparent, the qualitative analysis of this model is heavily dependent on the assumed values of the coefficients A, D, and F. The ġ = 0 isocline is given by ω = (A+Bg-Cg²-DF)/EF. In order for it to have a positive intercept, it is necessary that A > DF, which was previously assumed. This constraint represents the existence of a desire to accumulate which is independent of the current utilization of capital stock. It guarantees that, even if current capacity utilization is very low, a sufficient decline in labor's share will cause the rate of accumulation to increase. If equation (2) violates this condition, it is no longer the case that the appropriate location of the ω̇ = 0 isocline guarantees a region where cycles bring neither explosion nor crash. To see this, redraw the phase diagram of the system (2) and (4), setting A < DF. The result is shown in Figure 3a. The change in qualitative behavior brought about by this change in the accumulation function suggests two economic interpretations. Taking the situation where A > DF to represent "normal" conditions, the reversal of the inequality could be taken to stand for a collapse of "animal spirits." As Joan Robinson (1980, pp. 168-177) argued, capital accumulation is ultimately sustained by the willingness of capitalists to make investments in an uncertain environment. When that will fails, so do capitalist systems. They switch from having normal business cycles to having crises of the Great Depression variety. Alternately, the reversal of the inequality could be taken to result from disarray in what Gordon et al. (1982) call the "social structure of accumulation." That is, when the social and legal conditions necessary for the conduct of business and government and the realization of profit are not functioning effectively, accumulation will take place at a reduced rate or may collapse. The course of normal accumulation will not resume until a new set of instructions, favorable to profit and consistent with existing social conditions, have been put in place. The model is consistent with either perspective.

**I. Introduction**

In his chapter V on the dynamic analysis of a Keynesian model Sargent (1979) analyzes the effects of a sudden change in the money supply (via open-market operations) for the case of adaptive expectations as well as for perfect foresight. However, the result he derives for the latter case (strict neutrality if the change in monetary policy is not foreseen and reaction which even precedes action when it is foreseen) look very odd when considered from the viewpoint of the standard dynamic analysis that he applies in the adaptive expectations case. Hence, solution methods must be different when the dynamic laws which govern prices and related expectations are changed from the adaptive to the perfect foresight case - despite the fact that only ordinary differential equations are applied in both cases. This change in methods and results is insufficiently explained and motivated by Sargent. Furthermore, and more importantly, his way of reasoning conceals significant ambiguities and inconsistencies in the analyzed dynamic situation.

We shall first provide a brief, alternative introduction into Sargent's dynamics in the following section. Section 3 then studies and clarifies the structure of Sargent's perfect foresight model. We shall see that additional and not well motivated assumptions are necessary to fix a unique time path for the model that produces the above stated results. Sargent's seemingly complete original model [see (1) - (10)] is thus simply left sufficiently incomplete and so allows for surprising new results in comparison to the case of adaptive expectations by an appropriate manipulation of its degrees of freedom.
