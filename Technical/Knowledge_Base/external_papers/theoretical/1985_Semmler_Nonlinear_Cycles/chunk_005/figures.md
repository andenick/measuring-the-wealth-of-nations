## Figure I: A Limit Cycle (Page 326)

**Type:** Phase diagram
**Title:** FIGURE I: A Limit Cycle
**Axes:** X_t (horizontal), Ẋ_t (vertical)
**Origin:** Center of diagram

**Description:**
This is a phase diagram illustrating the concept of a limit cycle in non-linear systems. The diagram shows concentric circular or elliptical trajectories in the X_t - Ẋ_t phase space. Multiple trajectories (shown with both solid and dashed lines) spiral toward a stable limit cycle in the center. Arrows indicate the direction of movement along the trajectories. The diagram demonstrates how all trajectories starting near the limit cycle converge toward it as time tends to infinity, regardless of initial conditions. This is a key characteristic of non-linear models that linear models cannot produce - the ability to generate endogenous, stationary cyclical behavior determined by system parameters rather than external perturbations.

## Figure II: Unemployment Rates 1959-1978 (Page 328)

**Type:** Multiple time series line graph
**Time Period:** 1959-1978 (ending December 1979)
**Y-Axis:** Not explicitly scaled in transcription
**X-Axis:** Years from 1959 to 1978

**Three Lines Plotted:**
1. **Unemployment rate, all civilian workers** (solid line, top) - Shows sharp peaks and asymmetric patterns around business cycle turning points
2. **Unemployment rate, both sexes, 25 years and over** (dotted line, middle) - Shows similar pattern but with lower magnitude
3. **Unemployment rate, job losers** (dotted line, bottom) - Shows similar cyclical pattern at lower levels

**Description:**
This figure demonstrates time-irreversible behavior in unemployment data. The graph shows clear asymmetry around business cycle turning points - sharp increases during recessions followed by more gradual decreases during recoveries. This asymmetric behavior is a key example of non-linear phenomena that cannot be captured by linear time series models, which would produce symmetric movements around turning points. The unemployment rate for all civilian workers shows the most pronounced asymmetric pattern.

## Figure IV: Power Spectrum - Gross National Product, Wharton Model (Page 331)

**Type:** Power spectrum plot
**Title:** Gross National Product, Wharton (β = 1/40 c²/q)
**X-Axis:** Frequency (0-20)
**Y-Axis:** Power (logarithmic scale from 0.005 to 5.000)

**Description:**
This power spectrum shows the periodogram implied by the Wharton econometric model. In stark contrast to Figure III (the observed data), this spectrum shows a smooth, monotonically decreasing curve without the oscillations characteristic of business cycles. The power starts high at low frequencies (around 5.0) and decreases smoothly to approximately 0.010 at frequency 20. The absence of peaks at business cycle frequencies demonstrates that the Wharton model fails to capture the 12-15 quarter oscillations present in the actual data. This comparison illustrates Howrey's (1972) finding that the model's dynamic structure does not endogenously generate business cycle variations.

## Figure V: Aggregate Hours - Actual vs. Predicted (Page 333)

**Type:** Time series comparison graph
**Title:** FIGURE V
**Time Period:** Approximately 1950-1980 (ending December 1979)
**Y-Axis:** Aggregate Hours (scale from approximately 900 to 1300.00)
**X-Axis:** TIME (years from approximately 0 to 100, representing the period)

**Two Lines Plotted:**
1. **Actual** (solid line) - Shows pronounced cyclical behavior with sharp peaks and troughs marked as "P" (peak) and "T" (trough). Displays clear asymmetry around turning points with sharp drops and gradual recoveries.
2. **Predicted** (dashed line) - Shows smoother trend with much less pronounced cyclical variation. Movements are more symmetric and gradual.

**Description:**
This figure compares actual man-hours in the US economy with hours predicted by the FMP (Federal Reserve) model. The graph dramatically illustrates the failure of the structural model to capture time-irreversible behavior. The predicted series is symmetric around business cycle turning points (time reversible), while the actual series shows clear asymmetry - sharp declines at peaks followed by gradual recoveries. This demonstrates that the FMP model cannot generate the non-linear, time-irreversible characteristics observed in actual economic data. The discrepancy is particularly evident at the marked peak (P) and trough (T) points throughout the series.
