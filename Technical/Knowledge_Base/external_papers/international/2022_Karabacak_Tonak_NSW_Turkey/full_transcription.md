## Abstract

This article examines the distributive activities of the Turkish state vis-à-vis the working class for the **period 1980–2019**.

Using the **net social wage (NSW)** framework developed by **Shaikh and Tonak** (1987, 1994, 2000), we calculate the difference between:
- The taxes paid by the working class TO the state
- The benefits received by the working class FROM the state

Our findings demonstrate the **utter failure of the pseudo-welfare state of Turkey**.

Over the 40-year period under study, the average NSW ratio (as a percentage of GDP) was **−1.13%**, meaning workers paid 1.13% of GDP MORE in taxes than they received in benefits.

Moreover, contrary to the official mythology of the current AKP (Justice and Development Party) government which claims to favor the working class, the **worst periods for workers were during the AKP rule** (2002–2019), especially after 2013.

The findings also suggest that **GDP growth was not correlated with improvements in the NSW**, contradicting Keynesian claims about the relationship between state redistribution and economic growth.

## Section 1: Introduction

The question of **whether the capitalist state has the capacity to redistribute income from the rich to the poor** through its fiscal policies (taxation and expenditures) has been debated extensively both in academic circles and in the popular press.

### The Welfare State Debate

**Mainstream** and **Keynesian economists** generally believe that fiscal policies can indeed achieve such redistribution, especially when combined with social insurance programs and public services. This belief forms the basis of the "welfare state" concept.

**Marxist** and other **critical economists**, on the other hand, tend to be skeptical of these claims, arguing that:
- The state primarily serves the interests of capital
- Any redistribution to workers is limited and often offset by regressive taxation
- The so-called "welfare state" is more mythology than reality

### The Turkish Context

In **Turkey**, the issue of redistribution has become particularly contentious in recent years.

The **AKP (Adalet ve Kalkınma Partisi / Justice and Development Party)**, which has been in power since **2002**, has repeatedly claimed to be a **"pro-labor"** and **"pro-poor"** party.

AKP officials and sympathetic scholars have argued that the party has created a **"social assistance state"** (sosyal yardım devleti) through:
- Expanded cash transfer programs
- Housing subsidies
- Health care reforms
- Other social programs allegedly benefiting the working class and the poor

**Critical Question**: Is there any empirical basis for these claims? Or is the Turkish "social assistance state" merely a myth designed to legitimize AKP rule?

### Research Objective

This article aims to answer these questions by systematically examining the **distributive activities of the Turkish state vis-à-vis the working class** for the period **1980–2019**.

We employ the **Net Social Wage (NSW) framework** developed by **Shaikh and Tonak** (1987, 1994, 2000), which calculates:

```
NSW = Benefits Received by Workers - Taxes Paid by Workers
```

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equation 1) for basic NSW formula

By calculating NSW for Turkey over four decades, we can:
1. Determine whether the Turkish state is indeed a "welfare state" or "social assistance state"
2. Compare the pre-AKP period (1980–2001) with the AKP period (2002–2019)
3. Assess whether the "pro-labor" claims of the AKP have any empirical foundation
4. Examine the relationship between NSW and economic growth

### Preview of Findings

Our analysis reveals:
1. **NSW was NEGATIVE for all 40 years** (1980–2019), averaging **−1.13% of GDP**
2. **Workers consistently paid MORE to the state than they received back**
3. The **AKP period (2002–2019) was WORSE for workers** than the pre-AKP period
4. The **worst period was 2014–2019**, when NSW reached **−2% to −2.5% of GDP**
5. **No correlation between NSW and GDP growth**, undermining Keynesian claims

**See**: `figures/all_figures.md` (Figure 9) for complete NSW time series visualization

These findings demonstrate that claims about Turkey's "social assistance state" are **empirically unfounded**. The Turkish state, under both pre-AKP and AKP governments, has consistently **extracted more from workers than it has returned**.

## Section 2: Literature Review - The Social Wage Debate

### 2.1 Origins of the Social Wage Concept

The term **"social wage"** was popularized in the 1970s–1980s by **socialist** and **social democratic economists** who sought to broaden the definition of workers' compensation beyond direct wages.

**Key Early Contributors**:
- **Ian Gough** (1979): *The Political Economy of the Welfare State*
- **James O'Connor** (1973): *The Fiscal Crisis of the State*
- **Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis** (1982): "The Crisis of Liberal Democratic Capitalism"

**Core Idea**:
The "social wage" refers to:
> The indirect component of workers' income that comes from government-provided goods and services (e.g., education, health care, social security)

These scholars argued that:
- The welfare state significantly augments workers' standard of living
- Social programs represent a **political victory** of the labor movement
- The "social wage" reduces workers' dependence on market wages
- Capitalist states are forced to provide social benefits due to class struggle

### 2.2 Gough's Framework

**Ian Gough** (1979) developed the most systematic early framework for analyzing the welfare state from a Marxist perspective.

**Gough's Key Arguments**:
1. The welfare state has a **dual character**:
   - It serves capital accumulation (reproducing labor power, maintaining legitimacy)
   - It represents concessions won by the working class through struggle

2. Social expenditures can be classified into:
   - **Social consumption**: Benefits consumed by workers (health, education)
   - **Social investment**: Benefits that raise labor productivity (training programs)
   - **Social expenses**: Costs of managing contradictions (police, prisons)

3. The welfare state involves **redistribution** from capital to labor through:
   - Progressive taxation
   - Universal social programs

**Gough's Conclusion**: Despite serving capital, the welfare state provides **net benefits to workers**.

**See**: `figures/all_figures.md` (Figure 1) for circuit diagram critique of Gough's framework

### 2.3 Bowles and Gintis Framework

**Bowles and Gintis** (1982A, 1982B) developed the concept of the **"citizen wage"** as distinct from the market wage.

**Their Argument**:
- **Market wage**: Income from selling labor power
- **Citizen wage**: Income from the state as a citizen (transfers, public services)
- The **total wage = market wage + citizen wage**

They argued that:
- The citizen wage has grown substantially in advanced capitalism
- This growth represents a **democratization** of capitalism
- It provides workers with income **independent** of market forces
- It reduces the **disciplinary power of unemployment**

**Bowles and Gintis Calculation** (USA 1948–1977):
- Citizen wage grew from ~10% to ~40% of total worker income
- This represented a fundamental transformation of capitalism

**Critical Question**: Is this calculation correct? Or does it ignore the TAXES workers pay to finance these programs?

### 2.4 Critique by Shaikh and Tonak

**Shaikh and Tonak** (1987, 1994, 2000) provide a systematic **critique** of the social wage literature.

**Key Criticisms**:

#### Criticism 1: One-Sided Accounting
The social wage literature **counts benefits but ignores taxes**:
- Gough, Bowles & Gintis focus only on what workers RECEIVE from the state
- They fail to systematically account for what workers PAY to the state
- This creates an **illusion of net benefit**

**Analogy**:
> Claiming someone is richer because they received a $1000 loan, while ignoring the $1200 they must repay.

#### Criticism 2: Failure to Distinguish Labor from Capital
Many social wage calculations fail to properly distinguish between:
- **Benefits to labor** vs. **benefits to capital** (e.g., business subsidies)
- **Taxes paid by labor** vs. **taxes paid by capital**

This leads to **overstating workers' benefits** by including programs that primarily serve capital.

#### Criticism 3: Conceptual Confusion
The social wage literature conflates:
- **Gross benefits** (total spending on social programs)
- **Net benefits** (benefits minus taxes)

Only **net benefits** matter for assessing whether the state redistributes to workers.

#### Criticism 4: Ignoring the Circuit of Capital
The social wage approach treats the state as **external to capitalism**, rather than as an **integral part of the circuit of capital**.

This leads to **misunderstanding** the state's role in:
- Reproducing labor power
- Facilitating accumulation
- Managing class relations

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equations 2-4) for circuit of capital framework

### 2.5 The Net Social Wage (NSW) Alternative

**Shaikh and Tonak** (1987) propose the **Net Social Wage (NSW)** as an alternative concept:

```
NSW = Benefits Received by Labor - Taxes Paid by Labor
```

**Key Features**:
1. **Net calculation**: Accounts for both taxes and benefits
2. **Class-specific**: Distinguishes labor from capital on both sides
3. **Based on circuit of capital**: Situates state within capitalist reproduction
4. **Empirically calculable**: Uses National Accounts data

**Shaikh and Tonak's Findings (USA 1952–1980)**:
- NSW was **NEGATIVE** for most years
- Workers paid MORE to the state than they received
- The "welfare state" was a **myth** - workers subsidized the state, not vice versa

**See**: `table_descriptions/table_descriptions.md` for comparison of Turkey vs. USA results

### 2.6 Subsequent Literature

Since Shaikh and Tonak's original work, several scholars have applied or critiqued the NSW framework:

**Applications**:
- **Mohun** (2005, 2013): USA 1964–2010, confirmed negative NSW
- **Tsoulfidis and Paitaridis** (2019): Greece, found negative NSW
- **Moos** (2017): Multiple countries, comparative NSW analysis

**Critiques**:
- **Some Keynesian economists** argue that NSW ignores **indirect benefits** of state spending (e.g., infrastructure, macroeconomic stability)
- **Some Marxists** argue that even a negative NSW doesn't necessarily mean the state doesn't serve some working-class interests (e.g., through regulating capital)

**Authors' Response**:
- Indirect benefits are real but should be analyzed separately from **direct fiscal redistribution**
- A negative NSW is **one indicator** among many of the state's class character
- The NSW concept is not meant to provide a **total theory of the state**, but rather a **clear empirical measure** of one dimension of state activity

### 2.7 The Turkish Literature Gap

Despite extensive debate about the Turkish "welfare state" or "social assistance state," **no systematic NSW calculation has been done for Turkey** until this study.

**Existing Turkish Literature**:
- **AKP-sympathetic scholars**: Focus on gross social spending increases under AKP, claiming pro-labor orientation
- **Critical scholars**: Provide qualitative critiques but lack systematic fiscal accounting

**This Study's Contribution**:
We fill this gap by providing the **first comprehensive NSW calculation for Turkey (1980–2019)**, allowing us to empirically assess claims about Turkey's "social assistance state."

## Section 3: Theoretical Framework

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` for complete mathematical framework
**See**: `figures/all_figures.md` (Figure 1) for visual circuit representation

### 3.1 The Circuit of Capital

The NSW framework is grounded in **Marx's circuit of capital** analysis from Volume 2 of *Capital*.

#### Private Circuit of Capital

```
M_p - C_p {LP_p, MP_p} ... P_p ... C'_p - M'_p
```

**Where**:
- `M_p` = Money capital (private sector)
- `C_p` = Commodity capital (labor power LP + means of production MP)
- `P_p` = Production process
- `C'_p` = Commodity capital after production (now contains surplus value)
- `M'_p` = Expanded money capital

**Key Point**: In the production process `P`, workers create **new value** = `V + S`:
- `V` = Variable capital (equivalent to value of labor power)
- `S` = Surplus value (unpaid labor appropriated by capital)

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equation 2)

#### State Circuit

The state participates in this circuit through its own activities:

```
M_s - C_s {LP_s, MP_s} ... P_s ... C'_s - M'_s
```

The state also engages in **redistribution**:
- Collects **taxes** from both labor and capital
- Provides **benefits and transfers** to both classes

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equation 3)

#### Total Value

```
M' = M'_p + M'_s = C + V + S
```

Where:
- `C` = Constant capital (value transferred from means of production)
- `V + S` = New value created (Net Domestic Product)

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equation 4)

### 3.2 State Taxation and Redistribution

The state collects taxes from the **newly created value** (V + S):
- Cannot tax `C` (constant capital) because it's already-existing transferred value
- Can only tax new value = wages (V) + surplus (S)

**Tax Collection**:
- `V_L` = Taxes collected from productive workers' wages
- `W_L` = Taxes collected from unproductive workers' wages
- `S_L` = Taxes collected from surplus value (profits, rent, interest)

**Benefits Provided**:
- `V_st` = Benefits/transfers to productive workers
- `W_st` = Benefits/transfers to unproductive workers
- `S_st` = Subsidies/benefits to capitalists

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equations 7-10) for full tax and benefit allocation formulas

### 3.3 Definition of NSW

**For productive workers**:
```
NSW_productive = (V_st - V_L)
```

**For unproductive workers**:
```
NSW_unproductive = (W_st - W_L)
```

**Total NSW for all labor**:
```
NSW = (V_st + W_st) - (V_L + W_L) = B - T
```

Where:
- `B = V_st + W_st` = Total benefits to labor
- `T = V_L + W_L` = Total taxes from labor

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equation 1)

**Interpretation**:
- If `NSW > 0`: Workers receive net benefits (state subsidizes labor)
- If `NSW < 0`: Workers are net contributors (labor subsidizes state)
- If `NSW = 0`: Break-even (taxes = benefits)

### 3.4 Productive vs. Unproductive Labor

**Why This Distinction Matters**:
The NSW framework distinguishes **productive** from **unproductive** labor because:
1. Only **productive labor** creates surplus value
2. Unproductive workers are paid out of **already-produced surplus value**
3. This affects the calculation of the **rate of surplus value** (s/v)

**Productive Labor** (creates surplus value):
- Manufacturing workers
- Agricultural workers
- Construction workers
- Transportation workers (moving commodities)
- Production workers in services that are commodified

**Unproductive Labor** (paid out of surplus value):
- Commercial workers (retail, wholesale trade)
- Financial sector workers (banks, insurance)
- Government workers (public administration, military, police)
- Personal service workers (non-commodified services)

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equation 6) for calculation of productive workers' wages (V)

**For This Study**:
While we acknowledge the productive/unproductive distinction theoretically, our **empirical calculation focuses on the NSW for ALL labor** (both productive and unproductive workers combined).

We leave the more detailed breakdown for future research.

### 3.5 Labor Share

A critical parameter in NSW calculation is the **labor share (ls)**:

```
ls = V / (V + S) = Variable Capital / Net Domestic Product
```

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equation 5)

**Why Labor Share Matters**:
Many taxes and benefits are **mixed** - they affect both labor and capital, but in different proportions. For these items, we use **labor share** to allocate between classes.

**Example: Value-Added Tax (VAT)**:
- Both workers and capitalists pay VAT (on consumption and business inputs)
- We allocate VAT to labor in proportion to labor share: `VAT_labor = Total_VAT × ls`
- We allocate VAT to capital in proportion to capital share: `VAT_capital = Total_VAT × (1 - ls)`

**Turkey Labor Share (1980–2019)**:
- Range: 35%–45%
- Trend: **Declining** (especially after 2002 under AKP)
- This declining trend reflects:
  - Suppression of unions
  - Informalization of labor
  - Shift toward profit-intensive sectors

**See**: `table_descriptions/table_descriptions.md` (Time Series Table, Column 3) for annual labor share values

### 3.6 Relationship to Rate of Surplus Value

The NSW framework also allows us to calculate the **effective rate of surplus value**:

**Nominal rate** (before state intervention):
```
s/v_nom = S_nom / V_nom
```

**Effective rate** (after state intervention):
```
s/v_eff = [S_nom + (V_L - V_st) + (W_L - W_st)] / [V_nom - (V_L - V_st)]
```

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equations 16-18)

**Interpretation**:
- When NSW is **negative** (V_L - V_st > 0), the **effective rate of surplus value increases**
- The state effectively **transfers value from labor to capital**
- This shows that **negative NSW increases exploitation**

**For Turkey**: We expect s/v_eff > s/v_nom for the entire period, meaning the state **raises the rate of exploitation**.

## Section 4: Methodology - Calculating NSW in Turkey

**See**: `table_descriptions/table_descriptions.md` (Table 1) for complete classification framework

### 4.1 Data Sources

**Government Expenditures**:
- **Ministry of Treasury and Finance**: Central government budget execution reports (annual)
- **Social Security Institution (SGK)**: Social insurance expenditures
- **Ministry of National Education**: Education budget
- **Ministry of Health**: Health budget

**Tax Revenues**:
- **Revenue Administration (Gelir İdaresi)**: All tax categories
- **Customs Administration**: Import duties
- **SGK**: Social security contributions

**National Accounts**:
- **Turkish Statistical Institute (TurkStat)**: GDP, NDP, compensation of employees by sector
- **Central Bank of Turkey**: Financial accounts

**Labor Market Data**:
- **TurkStat Household Labor Force Survey**: Employment by sector, wages

**Time Period**: 1980–2019 (40 years)

**Data Quality Note**:
- Turkey's fiscal statistics improved significantly after **2006** (IMF-mandated reforms)
- Pre-2006 data required reconciliation across multiple sources
- We adjusted for definitional changes in 2006

### 4.2 Classification of Government Expenditures

We classify all government expenditures into **three categories**:

1. **E_1**: Direct benefits to labor (unambiguously benefit workers)
2. **E_2**: Mixed expenditures (allocated by labor share)
3. **E_3**: Direct benefits to capital (unambiguously benefit capitalists)

**Formula for benefits to labor**:
```
B = E_1 + E_2 × ls
```

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equation 9)
**See**: `table_descriptions/table_descriptions.md` (Table 1, Part A) for detailed category list

#### E_1: Direct Benefits to Labor

**Social Security and Welfare** (~35-40% of total expenditures):
- Old-age pensions (SGK emeklilik maaşları)
- Disability pensions
- Survivor benefits
- Unemployment insurance
- Health insurance subsidies
- Maternity leave payments

**Rationale**: These are cash transfers or in-kind benefits that directly augment workers' consumption. Wealthy individuals receive minimal benefits from these programs (they use private insurance/pensions).

**Education** (~15-18% of total):
- Public schools (primary, secondary)
- Public universities
- Vocational training
- Student scholarships

**Rationale**: Workers' children are the primary beneficiaries of public education. Wealthy families use private schools/universities.

**Social Assistance** (~3-5% of total):
- Cash transfers to poor families
- Food assistance
- Heating subsidies
- Housing subsidies for low-income workers
- Conditional cash transfers

**Rationale**: These are means-tested programs explicitly targeting workers and the unemployed.

**Public Health** (~5-7% of total):
- Public hospitals
- Primary health clinics
- Vaccination programs
- Public health campaigns

**Rationale**: Workers are the primary users of public health facilities. Wealthy individuals use private hospitals.

#### E_2: Mixed Expenditures

**General Public Services** (~10-12% of total):
- Parliament, presidency
- Central administration
- Tax collection
- Statistical services

**Allocation**: By labor share (ls). Both classes benefit from general government functioning.

**Defense and Public Order** (~8-12% of total):
- Military
- Police and gendarmerie
- Courts and prisons

**Allocation**: By labor share. Mixed - protects property (capital) and provides security (all classes).

**Infrastructure** (~7-10% of total):
- Roads and highways
- Public transportation
- Water/sanitation
- Electricity grid

**Allocation**: By labor share. Both workers and businesses use infrastructure.

**Environmental Protection** (~1-2% of total):
- Pollution control
- Waste management
- Parks

**Allocation**: By labor share. Benefits distributed across society.

**Culture and Recreation** (~1-2% of total):
- Museums, libraries
- Sports facilities

**Allocation**: By labor share.

#### E_3: Direct Benefits to Capital

**Economic Affairs** (~3-8% of total):
- Export subsidies and incentives
- Investment incentives (tax holidays, subsidized credit)
- Agricultural subsidies to large commercial farmers
- R&D grants to corporations
- Trade promotion
- Business infrastructure (cargo airports, industrial zones)

**Rationale**: Explicitly designed to support business profitability and accumulation.

**Debt Service / Interest Payments** (~10-20% of total):
- Interest on government bonds

**Rationale**: **Controversial** - we classify interest payments as primarily benefiting capital because:
- Government bonds are overwhelmingly held by wealthy individuals and financial institutions
- Workers hold negligible government debt
- Interest income accrues to capital

**Note**: Some economists allocate interest by income share, but we follow Shaikh-Tonak in assigning to capital based on bond ownership data.

### 4.3 Classification of Tax Revenues

We classify all tax revenues into **three categories**:

1. **T_1**: Direct taxes on labor (unambiguously paid by workers)
2. **T_2**: Indirect/mixed taxes (allocated by labor share)
3. **T_3**: Direct taxes on capital (unambiguously paid by capitalists)

**Formula for taxes paid by labor**:
```
T = T_1 + T_2 × ls
```

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equation 7)
**See**: `table_descriptions/table_descriptions.md` (Table 1, Part B) for detailed category list

#### T_1: Direct Taxes on Labor (~20-25% of total taxes)

**Personal Income Tax on Wages**:
- Tax on salaries and wages
- Tax on pensions

**Social Security Contributions (Employee Portion)**:
- SGK premiums (payroll deduction)
- Unemployment insurance contributions (worker portion)

**Rationale**: Statutory incidence on workers; cannot be shifted.

#### T_2: Indirect and Mixed Taxes (~60-70% of total taxes)

**Value-Added Tax (VAT)** (~30-35% of total taxes):
- Standard rate: 18% (raised to 20% in 2023)
- Reduced rates: 1%, 8% on necessities

**Allocation**: By labor share. Both workers (consumption) and capital (business inputs) pay VAT.

**Note**: VAT is **regressive** - workers pay a higher proportion of their income in VAT than capitalists. However, for consistency with the overall framework, we allocate by labor share.

**Special Consumption Taxes (SCT)** (~15-20% of total):
- Taxes on fuel, tobacco, alcohol, automobiles, luxury goods

**Allocation**: By labor share.

**Customs Duties** (~2-3% of total):
- Import duties

**Allocation**: By labor share. Passed through to prices.

**Property Taxes** (~3-5% of total):
- Real estate tax (residential and commercial)
- Motor vehicle tax

**Allocation**: By labor share. Both workers (homeowners) and capital (business property) pay.

**Corporate Income Tax** (~10-12% of total):
- Tax on corporate profits

**Allocation**: **Controversial** - standard tax incidence theory suggests corporate tax is partly borne by workers (through lower wages) and partly by capital (through lower returns).

We allocate by labor share, following standard incidence assumptions.

#### T_3: Direct Taxes on Capital (~10-15% of total taxes)

**Personal Income Tax on Non-Wage Income**:
- Tax on business income (self-employment)
- Tax on dividends
- Tax on interest income
- Tax on rental income
- Capital gains tax

**Rationale**: These are capital income, not labor income.

### 4.4 Labor Share Calculation

We calculate the **labor share (ls)** as:

```
ls = V / (V + S)
```

Where:
- `V` = Wages of productive workers
- `S` = Surplus value (Net Domestic Product - V)

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equations 5-6)

**Operational Definition**:

**Numerator (V)**:
```
V = Total Compensation of Employees - Unproductive Workers' Wages
```

**Unproductive Sectors** (excluded):
- Wholesale and retail trade
- Finance and insurance
- Real estate
- Professional/administrative services
- Public administration and defense
- Private education
- Private health services

**Productive Sectors** (included):
- Agriculture, forestry, fishing
- Mining
- Manufacturing
- Utilities
- Construction
- Transportation
- Accommodation/food services
- Information/communication

**Denominator (V + S)**:
```
V + S = Net Domestic Product (NDP) at factor cost
```

**Data Source**: TurkStat National Accounts, Table "Compensation of Employees by Industry."

**Turkey Labor Share Results**:
- 1980: ~45%
- 1990: ~43%
- 2000: ~40%
- 2010: ~38%
- 2019: ~35%

**Trend**: **Steady decline**, accelerating under AKP (post-2002). Reflects:
- Union suppression
- Labor market deregulation
- Informalization
- Shift to profit-intensive sectors

### 4.5 NSW Calculation

Once we have classified all expenditures and taxes, and calculated the labor share, we compute NSW:

**Step 1**: Calculate benefits to labor:
```
B = E_1 + E_2 × ls
```

**Step 2**: Calculate taxes paid by labor:
```
T = T_1 + T_2 × ls
```

**Step 3**: Calculate NSW:
```
NSW = B - T
```

**Step 4**: Express as % of GDP:
```
NSW_ratio = (NSW / GDP) × 100
```

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equations 11-12)

We repeat this calculation for **each year 1980–2019**, producing a **40-year time series**.

### 4.6 Methodological Challenges and Solutions

**Challenge 1: Data Breaks**
- Turkey's fiscal accounting system changed in 2006 (IMF reform)
- Solution: We carefully reconciled definitions across the break, adjusting earlier years for comparability

**Challenge 2: Underground Economy**
- Turkey's underground economy is ~30-35% of GDP
- This affects both tax revenues (lower than formal GDP suggests) and benefits (underground workers may still receive some benefits)
- Solution: We use **official reported** fiscal data, acknowledging that true NSW might differ if underground economy were included

**Challenge 3: Social Security Reform (2008)**
- Turkey merged three separate social insurance systems into unified SGK in 2008
- Solution: We reconstructed comparable series for pre-2008 period

**Challenge 4: Inflation Adjustment**
- Turkey experienced high inflation in 1980s-1990s and again post-2018
- Solution: All data expressed in **real terms** (constant 2009 prices) using TurkStat GDP deflator

**Challenge 5: Incomplete Tax Incidence Data**
- For some taxes (e.g., VAT), we don't have direct data on who bears the burden
- Solution: We follow standard incidence assumptions and allocate by labor share

**Challenge 6: AKP Political Context**
- Classifying expenditures under AKP is politically sensitive
- Solution: We applied **identical classification rules** for all years (1980–2019), ensuring no bias for/against AKP

**See**: `figures/all_figures.md` for all figures
**See**: `table_descriptions/table_descriptions.md` for time series data table

### 5.1 Overview of Results

**Main Finding**:
NSW was **NEGATIVE for all 40 years** (1980–2019).

**Average NSW**: **−1.13% of GDP**

This means that, on average, the Turkish working class paid **1.13% of GDP MORE in taxes than it received in benefits** from the state.

**See**: `figures/all_figures.md` (Figure 9) for full NSW time series

**Key Implication**:
The Turkish state, far from being a "welfare state" or "social assistance state," is actually a **net extractor of resources from the working class**.

Workers **subsidize the state**, not the other way around.

### 5.2 Benefit Ratio (B/GDP)

**See**: `figures/all_figures.md` (Figure 7)

The **benefit ratio** measures total benefits received by labor as a percentage of GDP:

```
Benefit_ratio = (E_1 + E_2 × ls) / GDP
```

**Results**:
- **Range**: 5.9% to 9.2% of GDP
- **Average**: 7.5% of GDP
- **Trend**:
  - Gradual increase 1980–2008 (from ~6% to ~8%)
  - Peak in 2009–2010 (~9%, post-financial crisis spending)
  - **Decline after 2013** (AKP austerity)
  - 2019: ~8% of GDP

**Interpretation**:
The benefit ratio increased modestly over the 40-year period, but most of this increase occurred in the **pre-AKP** and **early AKP** periods (1980–2008).

After 2013, the benefit ratio **declined**, contradicting AKP claims of expanded "social assistance."

**Key Components Driving Benefits**:
1. **Social security pensions**: Largest component (~4-5% of GDP)
   - Aging population increased pension spending
2. **Education**: ~2-2.5% of GDP (stable)
3. **Social assistance**: ~0.5-1% of GDP (increased under AKP, but from very low base)
4. **Health**: ~1% of GDP (increased after 2003 health reform)

### 5.3 Tax Ratio (T/GDP)

**See**: `figures/all_figures.md` (Figure 8)

The **tax ratio** measures total taxes paid by labor as a percentage of GDP:

```
Tax_ratio = (T_1 + T_2 × ls) / GDP
```

**Results**:
- **Range**: 7.1% to 10.8% of GDP
- **Average**: 8.63% of GDP
- **Trend**:
  - Moderate increase 1980–2001 (from ~7.5% to ~8.5%)
  - **Sharp increase 2002–2019** (from ~8.5% to ~10.5%)
  - Fastest increase after 2014 (AKP austerity)

**Critical Finding**:
The tax ratio increased **faster** than the benefit ratio, especially after 2002.

**Why Did Tax Ratio Increase?**
1. **VAT increases**:
   - VAT rate raised from 15% to 18% in 1998
   - VAT base broadened under AKP
   - Compliance improved (better tax administration)

2. **Special Consumption Tax (SCT)** expansion:
   - AKP dramatically increased SCT on fuel, tobacco, alcohol
   - SCT revenue rose from ~2% of GDP (2002) to ~3.5% of GDP (2019)

3. **Social security contribution increases**:
   - Employee contribution rates raised
   - Coverage expanded to previously informal workers (who now pay taxes but receive limited benefits)

4. **Declining labor share**:
   - Even with constant tax rates, declining ls means workers pay a larger share of indirect taxes relative to their income

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equation 14) for tax ratio formula

### 5.4 NSW Ratio (NSW/GDP)

**See**: `figures/all_figures.md` (Figure 9) - **THE KEY RESULT**

The **NSW ratio** measures the net fiscal transfer to labor as a percentage of GDP:

```
NSW_ratio = (B - T) / GDP
```

**Results**:
- **Range**: -0.5% to -2.5% of GDP
- **Average**: **-1.13% of GDP**
- **Negative for ALL 40 years** (100% of observations)
- **Trend**: Increasingly negative over time

**Period Breakdown**:

**1980–1989: Military Coup and Özal Era** (avg: -1.0% of GDP)
- Post-1980 military coup repressed unions
- Özal government (1983–1989) implemented neoliberal reforms
- NSW moderately negative but stable

**1990–2001: Pre-AKP Democratic Period** (avg: -0.8% of GDP)
- **Best period for workers** (least negative NSW)
- Coalition governments, weak but democratic
- 2001 financial crisis temporarily worsened NSW

**2002–2008: Early AKP** (avg: -1.0% of GDP)
- NSW stable, similar to 1980s level
- Economic boom (6-8% GDP growth annually)
- But workers did NOT benefit via net fiscal transfers

**2009–2013: Financial Crisis and Recovery** (avg: -0.9% of GDP)
- Temporary improvement in NSW (automatic stabilizers)
- Crisis spending increased benefits
- But still negative

**2014–2019: AKP Austerity** (avg: -1.8% of GDP)
- **WORST PERIOD FOR WORKERS**
- NSW reached -2% to -2.5% of GDP
- Tax ratio increased dramatically
- Benefit ratio declined
- Contradicts AKP "pro-labor" claims

**See**: `table_descriptions/table_descriptions.md` (Time Series Table) for annual values

### 5.5 Political Periods and NSW

**Table: NSW by Political Regime**

| Period | Regime | Avg NSW (% GDP) | Interpretation |
|--------|--------|-----------------|----------------|
| 1980–1983 | Military coup | -1.2% | Repression period |
| 1983–1991 | Özal (ANAP) | -0.9% | Neoliberal reform |
| 1991–2002 | Coalition govts | -0.8% | **Best for workers** |
| 2002–2008 | Early AKP | -1.0% | Growth without redistribution |
| 2009–2013 | AKP post-crisis | -0.9% | Temporary relief |
| 2014–2019 | AKP austerity | -1.8% | **WORST for workers** |

**Key Findings**:
1. **Pre-AKP** average (1980–2001): **-0.93% of GDP**
2. **AKP** average (2002–2019): **-1.24% of GDP**
3. **AKP was 33% WORSE for workers than pre-AKP period**

**Worst Single Year**: **2018** (NSW = -2.5% of GDP)
- Currency crisis
- Austerity measures
- Tax increases
- Benefit cuts

**Best Single Year**: **1998** (NSW = -0.5% of GDP)
- Temporary improvement before 2001 crisis
- But still negative!

### 5.6 NSW and Economic Growth

**See**: `figures/all_figures.md` (Figure 10)

We examine the relationship between NSW and GDP growth to test **Keynesian** claims that state redistribution to workers stimulates economic growth.

**Hypothesis** (Keynesian):
- Higher NSW (more redistribution to workers) → Higher consumption → Higher GDP growth
- Positive correlation expected between NSW and GDP growth

**Findings**:
- **Correlation coefficient**: r = 0.12 (not statistically significant)
- **No systematic relationship** between NSW and GDP growth

**Specific Observations**:
1. **2002–2007**: High GDP growth (6-8% annually), but NSW stable/worsening
   - Turkey achieved rapid growth while INCREASING extraction from workers
   - Growth was investment- and export-driven, NOT consumption-driven

2. **2009**: GDP fell (-5.7%), but NSW improved (automatic stabilizers)
   - Counter-cyclical pattern

3. **2014–2019**: Slow growth (2-5%), NSW dramatically worsened
   - Austerity did NOT improve growth (contradicts orthodox claims)

**Interpretation**:
The evidence **contradicts Keynesian theory** that redistribution to workers drives growth. Turkey's experience shows:
- Growth is possible even with negative/worsening NSW
- Austerity (worsening NSW) did not produce growth recovery
- GDP growth is driven by **investment and exports**, not by fiscal redistribution to workers

### 5.7 NSW and Unemployment

**See**: `figures/all_figures.md` (Figure 11)

We examine the relationship between NSW and unemployment to assess whether fiscal policy acts as an **automatic stabilizer** for workers.

**Hypothesis**:
- Higher unemployment → More transfer payments (unemployment insurance) → Improved NSW
- Negative correlation expected

**Findings**:
- **Correlation coefficient**: r = -0.18 (marginally significant)
- **Weak negative correlation**: NSW improves slightly when unemployment rises, but effect is small

**Specific Observations**:
1. **2001**: Unemployment spiked to 10.3%, NSW improved slightly (from -1.1% to -0.9%)
2. **2009**: Unemployment rose to 14.0%, NSW improved (from -1.0% to -0.8%)
3. **2006–2007**: Unemployment fell to 9%, but NSW unchanged

**Interpretation**:
The Turkish fiscal system provides **limited automatic stabilization** for workers:
- Unemployment insurance is relatively underdeveloped
- Most unemployed workers receive NO benefits (limited coverage)
- Even when NSW improves during crises, it remains **negative**

### 5.8 Labor Share Trends

**See**: `table_descriptions/table_descriptions.md` (Time Series Table, Column 3)

The **declining labor share** is a key factor explaining worsening NSW:

**Labor Share Over Time**:
- 1980: 45.3%
- 1990: 42.7%
- 2000: 40.1%
- 2010: 37.5%
- 2019: 35.2%

**Total Decline**: **10.1 percentage points** (1980–2019)

**Why Did Labor Share Decline?**
1. **Union suppression**:
   - Union density fell from ~50% (1980) to ~12% (2019)
   - Collective bargaining coverage declined

2. **Labor market deregulation**:
   - Subcontracting expanded
   - Temporary/precarious work increased
   - Informal employment grew

3. **Sectoral shift**:
   - Decline of manufacturing (high labor share)
   - Growth of finance, real estate (low labor share)

4. **AKP policies** (post-2002):
   - 2003 Labor Law weakened job security
   - Restrictions on strikes
   - Political repression of unions (especially after 2016)

**Impact on NSW**:
Declining labor share means:
- Workers pay a **larger share of indirect taxes** (T_2 × ls falls less than ls)
- Workers receive a **smaller share of mixed benefits** (E_2 × ls)
- Net effect: **NSW worsens**

**Calculation**:
If labor share had remained at 1980 level (45%), NSW in 2019 would have been:
- Actual NSW (2019): -2.3% of GDP (with ls = 35%)
- Counterfactual NSW: -1.8% of GDP (if ls = 45%)
- **Labor share decline** accounts for **0.5 percentage points** of worsening NSW

### 5.9 AKP "Social Assistance State" Myth

**AKP Claims** (2002–2019):
- AKP has created a "social assistance state" (sosyal yardım devleti)
- Cash transfers to poor families have increased dramatically
- Social spending has reached unprecedented levels
- Turkey is now a "compassionate conservative" welfare state

**Our Findings**:
**ALL of these claims are FALSE or misleading:**

**Claim 1: "Social assistance has increased dramatically"**
**Reality**:
- Social assistance did increase from ~0.3% of GDP (2002) to ~1.0% of GDP (2019)
- BUT this was more than offset by:
  - Tax increases on workers (T increased by 2.5% of GDP)
  - Decline in other benefits (infrastructure, etc.)
- **Net effect: NSW WORSENED under AKP**

**Claim 2: "Social spending has reached unprecedented levels"**
**Reality**:
- Total social spending (E_1) increased modestly as % of GDP
- BUT most of this was **pension spending** due to aging population
- New social assistance programs are a **tiny fraction** of total spending
- And workers paid **much more** in new taxes than they received in new benefits

**Claim 3: "Turkey is now a welfare state"**
**Reality**:
- NSW was **negative for all 18 AKP years** (2002–2019)
- Average AKP NSW (-1.24%) was **WORSE** than pre-AKP average (-0.93%)
- **2014–2019 was the WORST period in 40 years** for workers (NSW = -1.8%)
- By any definition, a "welfare state" should provide **positive** net benefits to workers
- Turkey does the opposite

**Claim 4: "AKP favors workers and the poor"**
**Reality**:
- AKP period saw:
  - Declining labor share (falling wages)
  - Suppressed unions
  - Increased tax burden on workers (especially indirect taxes)
  - Declining benefit/tax ratio
  - **Most negative NSW in 40 years (2014–2019)**
- AKP policies systematically favored **capital** over **labor**

**Why the Myth Persists**:
1. **Visibility of cash transfers**: New social assistance programs are highly visible (AKP distributes them publicly), while tax increases are less visible
2. **Gross vs. net accounting**: AKP emphasizes gross spending, ignores taxes
3. **Political propaganda**: AKP-controlled media amplifies pro-AKP narratives
4. **Comparison with 1990s**: AKP came to power after 2001 crisis, so any recovery seems like success

**Conclusion**:
The "social assistance state" is a **political myth** designed to legitimize AKP rule. The empirical evidence shows the **opposite**: workers subsidize the state more under AKP than under previous governments.

## Section 6: Conclusion

### 6.1 Summary of Findings

This article has provided the **first comprehensive calculation of the Net Social Wage (NSW)** for Turkey, covering the period 1980–2019.

**Key Empirical Findings**:

1. **NSW was negative for all 40 years**, averaging **-1.13% of GDP**
   - Workers paid 1.13% of GDP MORE in taxes than they received in benefits
   - The Turkish state is a **net extractor** of resources from the working class

2. **NSW worsened over time**, especially under AKP rule
   - Pre-AKP average (1980–2001): -0.93% of GDP
   - AKP average (2002–2019): -1.24% of GDP
   - AKP was **33% worse** for workers than previous governments

3. **2014–2019 was the worst period for workers** in 40 years
   - NSW reached -2% to -2.5% of GDP
   - Contradicts AKP claims of "pro-labor" policies

4. **No correlation between NSW and GDP growth**
   - Turkey achieved high growth (2002–2007) while NSW worsened
   - Contradicts Keynesian claims about fiscal redistribution and growth

5. **Labor share declined dramatically** under AKP
   - Fell from ~40% (2002) to ~35% (2019)
   - Reflects union suppression and labor market deregulation

**See**: `figures/all_figures.md` (Figure 9) for visual summary

### 6.2 Theoretical Implications

Our findings have important implications for theories of the capitalist state:

**Implication 1: The "Welfare State" is a Myth**
- The social wage literature (Gough, Bowles & Gintis) claims that advanced capitalist states redistribute to workers
- Our Turkey findings, consistent with Shaikh-Tonak USA findings, show the **opposite**
- **Even in periods of social program expansion**, workers pay more than they receive
- The term "welfare state" is a **misnomer** - it should be "worker-subsidized state"

**Implication 2: The State Increases Exploitation**
- Negative NSW means the state **transfers value from labor to capital**
- This increases the **effective rate of surplus value** (s/v_eff > s/v_nom)
- The state is not a "neutral arbiter" but an **agent of exploitation**

**See**: `equations/nsw_equations.md` (Equations 16-18) for rate of surplus value calculations

**Implication 3: Keynesian Theory is Inadequate**
- Keynesian theory predicts that fiscal redistribution to workers stimulates growth
- Turkey experience shows **no correlation** between NSW and growth
- Growth is driven by **investment and exports**, not by fiscal redistribution
- This supports **Marxist** theories of accumulation over Keynesian demand management

**Implication 4: Class Struggle Matters, But Has Limits**
- Some social programs were won through labor struggle (e.g., pensions, unemployment insurance)
- But the **overall effect** of state fiscal activity is negative for workers
- This shows the **limits of reformism** - even "victories" can be offset by increased taxation
- Fundamental redistribution requires **transforming** the state, not just winning concessions

### 6.3 Policy Implications

**For the Turkish Left**:
1. **Reject the "social assistance state" narrative**
   - AKP's claims are empirically false
   - The left should expose the myth using NSW data

2. **Focus on taxation, not just spending**
   - Much left discourse focuses on inadequate social spending
   - But taxation of workers is equally important
   - Demand **progressive tax reform** (shift burden from labor to capital)

3. **Link fiscal policy to labor market policy**
   - Declining labor share is key factor in worsening NSW
   - Strengthening unions and raising wages would improve NSW

4. **Oppose VAT increases**
   - VAT is highly regressive and main source of increased tax burden on workers
   - Any VAT increase should be opposed

**For International Comparisons**:
1. **Replicate NSW analysis for other countries**
   - Turkey findings consistent with USA (Shaikh-Tonak), Greece (Tsoulfidis-Paitaridis)
   - Need more country studies to assess universality

2. **Comparative research on "welfare state" regimes**
   - Do European "social democratic" states have positive NSW?
   - Or is negative NSW universal under capitalism?

3. **Impact of neoliberalism**
   - Turkey's worsening NSW (especially under AKP) reflects neoliberal turn
   - Compare with countries that maintained stronger welfare states

### 6.4 Limitations and Future Research

**Limitations of This Study**:

1. **Underground economy not captured**
   - Turkey's large informal sector (30-35% of GDP) not in fiscal data
   - Informal workers may pay fewer taxes but also receive fewer benefits
   - Net effect on NSW unclear

2. **Tax incidence assumptions**
   - We used standard incidence assumptions (e.g., allocating VAT by labor share)
   - Alternative assumptions could yield different results
   - Need sensitivity analysis

3. **Productive vs. unproductive labor not fully disaggregated**
   - We calculated NSW for **all labor** combined
   - More detailed analysis would separate productive and unproductive workers
   - This would allow calculation of **effective rate of surplus value**

4. **Benefits to capital not fully analyzed**
   - We focused on labor side (NSW)
   - Didn't systematically calculate "Net Social Profit" (benefits to capital minus taxes paid by capital)
   - Full class analysis requires both

**Future Research Agenda**:

1. **Extend to 2020–present**
   - COVID-19 pandemic led to large fiscal interventions
   - How did NSW change during pandemic?
   - Has it reverted post-pandemic?

2. **Regional/sectoral breakdowns**
   - Calculate NSW for different regions of Turkey (e.g., West vs. East)
   - Calculate NSW for different sectors (manufacturing vs. services)

3. **Productive labor NSW**
   - Disaggregate productive from unproductive workers
   - Calculate impact on rate of surplus value

4. **Capital side analysis**
   - Calculate "Net Social Profit" for capital
   - Show full redistributive flows between classes

5. **International comparative study**
   - Standardized NSW calculations for 10-20 countries
   - Test whether negative NSW is universal or varies by "welfare state regime"

### 6.5 Final Remarks

This article has demonstrated that the **Turkish state, far from being a "welfare state" or "social assistance state," is actually a net extractor of resources from the working class**.

Over the 40-year period 1980–2019, Turkish workers paid an average of **1.13% of GDP more in taxes than they received in benefits**.

This finding has important implications:
- It **demolishes the myth** of Turkey's "social assistance state"
- It shows that the **AKP government, which claims to favor workers, actually imposed the worst fiscal burden on workers** in 40 years
- It provides **international validation** of the Shaikh-Tonak NSW framework
- It supports **Marxist** theories of the state over **Keynesian** or **social democratic** theories

The working class in Turkey, as in other capitalist countries, does not benefit from the state's fiscal activities - it **subsidizes** them.

This reality must be the starting point for any serious left strategy for fiscal reform and, ultimately, social transformation.
