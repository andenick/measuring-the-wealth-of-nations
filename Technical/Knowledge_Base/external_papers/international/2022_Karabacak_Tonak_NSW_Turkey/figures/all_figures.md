## Figure 1: Redistributive Activities of the State (page 1588)

**Location**: Section 3 (Theoretical Framework)
**Type**: Circuit of capital diagram

**Content Description**:
This is a **conceptual flow diagram** showing how the state participates in the circuit of capital through taxation and expenditure activities.

**Key Elements**:
1. **Private Circuit**: M_p - C_p{LP_p, MP_p}...P_p...C'_p - M'_p
2. **State Circuit**: M_s - C_s{LP_s, MP_s}...P_s...C'_s - M'_s
3. **Taxation Flows**: Arrows showing taxes collected from:
   - Variable capital (V_L from workers' wages)
   - Surplus value (S_L from capitalists' profits)
   - Wages of unproductive workers (W_L)
4. **Expenditure Flows**: Arrows showing state benefits/transfers:
   - V_st (benefits to productive workers)
   - S_st (subsidies to capitalists)
   - W_st (benefits to unproductive workers)
5. **Net Tax Calculation**: Visual representation of (Taxes - Benefits) for each class

**Theoretical Significance**:
- Shows state as **subcircuit of capital**, not external to it
- Demonstrates **redistributive role** between classes
- Visualizes the **Net Social Wage concept**: NSW = (V_st + W_st) - (V_L + W_L) for labor

**Connection to Formula**:
This diagram provides the visual foundation for the NSW formula:
```
NSW = B - T
where:
B = Benefits received by labor
T = Taxes paid by labor
```

## Figure 7: Benefit Ratio (1980-2019)

**Location**: Section 5 (Empirical Results)
**Type**: Time series line graph
**Y-axis**: Benefit ratio (% of GDP)
**X-axis**: Years 1980-2019 (40 years)

**Content Description**:
Shows the **ratio of total benefits received by labor to GDP** over the 40-year period.

**Key Features**:
1. **Starting point** (1980): ~6-7% of GDP
2. **Trend**: Generally increasing until late 2000s
3. **Peak**: Around 2009-2010 (post-financial crisis)
4. **Recent decline**: Falls after 2013 during AKP austerity period
5. **End point** (2019): ~8-9% of GDP

**Significant Periods**:
- **1980-2001**: Pre-AKP period, gradual increase
- **2002-2008**: Early AKP period, continued increase
- **2009-2013**: Financial crisis response, peak benefits
- **2014-2019**: AKP austerity, declining benefits

**Interpretation**:
While benefits increased in absolute terms, the paper shows this must be compared to **taxes paid by labor** (Figure 8) to determine net impact.

## Figure 8: Tax Ratio (1980-2019)

**Location**: Section 5 (Empirical Results)
**Type**: Time series line graph
**Y-axis**: Tax ratio (% of GDP)
**X-axis**: Years 1980-2019 (40 years)

**Content Description**:
Shows the **ratio of total taxes paid by labor to GDP** over the 40-year period.

**Key Features**:
1. **Starting point** (1980): ~7-8% of GDP
2. **Trend**: Steadily increasing throughout period
3. **Acceleration**: Sharp increase after 2002 (AKP period)
4. **End point** (2019): ~10-11% of GDP

**Critical Finding**:
The tax ratio **increased faster than the benefit ratio**, meaning:
- Workers paid an increasingly larger share of GDP in taxes
- This increase outpaced the growth in benefits received
- Result: **Net Social Wage became more negative over time**

**AKP Period (2002-2019)**:
- Tax ratio increased by ~3-4 percentage points of GDP
- Benefit ratio increased by ~2 percentage points
- **Net effect**: Workers subsidized state by additional 1-2% of GDP

## Figure 9: NSW Ratio (1980-2019)

**Location**: Section 5 (Empirical Results)
**Type**: Time series line graph
**Y-axis**: NSW ratio (% of GDP)
**X-axis**: Years 1980-2019 (40 years)

**Content Description**:
Shows the **Net Social Wage as a percentage of GDP** - the KEY RESULT of the paper.

**Formula Visualized**: NSW/GDP = (B - T)/GDP

**Key Features**:
1. **Entire period**: NSW is **NEGATIVE** for all 40 years
2. **Average**: -1.13% of GDP (workers subsidize state by 1.13% of GDP on average)
3. **Starting point** (1980): ~-1% of GDP
4. **Trend**: Becomes increasingly negative over time
5. **Worst period**: 2014-2019 (reaches -2% to -2.5% of GDP)
6. **Best period**: Late 1990s (~-0.5% of GDP)

**Critical Periods**:
- **1980-1989**: Military coup period, negative but moderate (-0.5% to -1.5%)
- **1990-2001**: Pre-AKP civilian governments, improved slightly
- **2002-2008**: Early AKP, stable but still negative
- **2009-2013**: Financial crisis, temporary improvement
- **2014-2019**: AKP austerity, **dramatically worse** (most negative in entire period)

**Interpretation**:
This figure **demolishes the myth of Turkey's welfare state**:
- Workers consistently pay MORE to state than they receive
- The so-called "pro-labor" AKP party presided over the **worst period for workers** (2014-2019)
- No evidence of "social assistance state" - opposite is true

## Figure 10: NSW and GDP Growth Rate (1980-2019)

**Location**: Section 5 (Empirical Results)
**Type**: Dual time series line graph (two y-axes)
**Y-axis (left)**: NSW ratio (% of GDP)
**Y-axis (right)**: GDP growth rate (%)
**X-axis**: Years 1980-2019

**Content Description**:
**Overlay comparison** of NSW ratio with real GDP growth rate to test relationship between state redistribution and economic growth.

**Key Findings**:
1. **No clear correlation**: NSW and GDP growth do not move together systematically
2. **Counter-cyclical pattern**: NSW sometimes improves during recessions (automatic stabilizers)
3. **Pro-cyclical tax increases**: During growth periods, tax burden on labor increases
4. **Growth without redistribution**: Turkey's growth spurts (2004-2007, 2010-2011) did NOT improve NSW

**Specific Observations**:
- **2001 crisis**: GDP fell sharply, NSW improved slightly (fewer taxes collected)
- **2002-2007 growth**: GDP grew 6-7% annually, NSW remained negative/worsened
- **2009 crisis**: GDP fell, NSW temporarily improved
- **2014-2019**: Slow growth + deteriorating NSW (worst combination)

**Theoretical Implication**:
Challenges the **Keynesian** claim that state redistribution drives growth:
- Turkey achieved high growth (2002-2007) while NSW was negative
- GDP growth did not translate to improved worker welfare via state redistribution

## Figure 11: NSW and Unemployment Rate (1980-2019)

**Location**: Section 5 (Empirical Results)
**Type**: Dual time series line graph (two y-axes)
**Y-axis (left)**: NSW ratio (% of GDP)
**Y-axis (right)**: Unemployment rate (%)
**X-axis**: Years 1980-2019

**Content Description**:
**Overlay comparison** of NSW ratio with unemployment rate to examine relationship between fiscal redistribution and labor market conditions.

**Key Findings**:
1. **Weak negative correlation**: When unemployment rises, NSW sometimes improves slightly (transfer payments)
2. **Limited automatic stabilizers**: Effect is small, doesn't offset overall negative NSW
3. **Structural problem**: Even with low unemployment (2006-2007), NSW remains negative
4. **AKP period paradox**: Unemployment stabilized (8-10%), but NSW got worse

**Specific Observations**:
- **Early 1980s**: High unemployment (~15%), NSW moderately negative
- **1990s**: Declining unemployment, NSW improved slightly
- **2002-2008**: Unemployment fell from 10% to 9%, NSW unchanged/worsened
- **2009**: Unemployment spiked to 14%, NSW improved temporarily
- **2014-2019**: Stable unemployment, but NSW dramatically worsened

**Interpretation**:
- Turkey's fiscal system **fails as automatic stabilizer** for workers
- Employment gains did not translate to improved net fiscal position of labor
- AKP's labor market "success" (stable employment) masked **fiscal exploitation** of workers

## Methodological Note on Figures

**Data Source**: All figures based on:
- Turkish Statistical Institute (TurkStat) National Accounts
- Ministry of Treasury and Finance budget data
- Calculations by authors using Shaikh-Tonak methodology

**Time Period**: 1980-2019 (40 years)
- Covers multiple political regimes
- Includes two major crises (2001, 2009)
- Captures full AKP period (2002-2019)

**Significance for NSW Project**:
These figures provide **international validation** of the NSW methodology:
- Same theoretical framework as 1984 Tonak dissertation
- Same result: Workers subsidize state (NSW negative)
- Different country context confirms universality of finding
- Longer time period (40 years vs. 28 years in US study)
