## Methodology Notes

### Shaikh-Tonak Framework Application
- **Data Source:** New Zealand System of National Accounts (NZSNA) + Inter-Industry Studies
- **Time Period:** 1972-1995 (24 years)
- **Industries:** 25 neoclassical production groups
- **Mapping Approach:** Output-side only (not use-side)
- **Key Adjustments:** Trade, finance, and owner-occupied dwellings categories

### Productive vs Unproductive Classification

**Productive Activities:**
- Agriculture, forestry, mining
- Manufacturing
- Energy and water
- Construction
- Hotels, repairs, entertainment
- Health and education
- Consumer-related transport

**Unproductive Activities (Trade):**
- Wholesale and retail trade
- Building and equipment rentals

**Outside Value Process:**
- Royalties (rent, interest, patents, taxes)
- Government administration (police, courts, defence)
- Rest of world transactions
- Financial transactions

### Data Construction
- Primary data: NZSNA annual series (intermediate production, total output by industry)
- Supplemental: Inter-Industry Studies (1972, 1977, 1982, 1986/87, 1991)
- Interpolation for non-Inter-Industry years
- Depreciation added to constant capital
- Self-employed imputed wages included in variable capital
- Non-capitalist production assumed negligible

## Key Findings Summary

### 1. Temporal Patterns (1972-1995)

**Period 1: 1972-1983 - Declining Accumulation**
- Accumulation rate (s/TV) declined from 34% to 31%
- Value composition of capital (c/v) rose 20% (from 293% to 355%)
- Rate of surplus-value (s/v) relatively stable around 200-210%
- Classic falling rate of profit scenario

**Period 2: 1984-1989 - Recovery**
- Accumulation rate rose from 32% to 38%
- Rate of surplus-value increased 37% (from 228% to 280%)
- Value composition of capital fell 12% (407% to 356%) due to recession write-offs
- Driven by neo-liberal labor market reforms

**Period 3: 1990-1995 - Stagnation**
- Accumulation rate stable around 38%
- Rate of surplus-value continued rising to 307%
- Value composition of capital increased again (356% to 397%)
- Rising s/v could not offset rising c/v

### 2. Comparison with USA (Shaikh & Tonak 1994)
- **Rate of surplus-value:** NZ shows higher volatility and higher levels (200-307% vs USA 200-250%)
- **Value composition of capital:** NZ significantly higher than USA (290-410% vs USA 240-300%)
- **Overall pattern:** Similar trends but more extreme fluctuations in NZ

### 3. Methodological Findings

**Problems with Conventional Measures:**
- Operating surplus/Employee compensation ratio is poor proxy for rate of surplus-value (50-75% vs 165-285%)
- Gross output overstates productive activity due to inclusion of unproductive sectors
- GDP growth can mask stagnation in productive value creation

**Problems with Pearce (1986) Estimates:**
- Defined productive labor as wage earners only (excluded supervisors/managers)
- Included unproductive sales/transaction workers
- Included fixed capital stocks in constant capital (should be only depreciation)
- Result: Rate of surplus-value understated by 66%, value composition overstated by 600-800%

### 4. Unproductive Activity Growth
- **Key Evidence:** Ratio of gross output to total value (GO/TV) increased from 120% to 132% (1972-1992)
- **Labor Evidence:** Ratio of total wages to variable capital (w'/v) increased from 167% to 203%
- **Sectoral Driver:** Financial sector expansion during neo-liberal reforms
- **Productivity Paradox:** Low productivity growth in unproductive sectors explains aggregate stagnation

### 5. Neo-Liberal Reform Assessment

**Conventional View Claims:**
- Lower inflation achieved
- Lower budget deficits achieved
- Higher economic growth achieved
- "Economic fundamentals were right"

**Classical View Findings:**
- Short-term rise in rate of surplus-value (labor exploitation)
- Growth in unproductive activity gave appearance of sustainable growth
- Productive activity remained weak
- Explains persistence of high unemployment, high real interest rates, volatile business confidence
