## ABSTRACT/SUMMARY

This article examines the relation between postwar fiscal policy and the standard of living of workers in the United States from 1952-1985. The authors critique the radical literature on the "social wage" or "citizen wage," demonstrating that such studies either ignore taxes paid by recipients of social welfare expenditures (Therborn 1984) or seriously underestimate them (Bowles and Gintis 1982a). The authors propose an alternative operational concept of **net transfer** - benefits and income received from the state minus taxes paid by workers. The empirical analysis reveals that:

1. The so-called "social wage" is actually a **social subsidy of the state** by workers (net transfer is negative for most of the period)
2. Workers generally transfer a net portion of their wages to the state
3. State intervention has served to **increase the rate of exploitation** of workers
4. By 1985, the social benefit rate fell below 1966 levels while the tax rate reached an all-time high (41% higher than 1966)
5. The net transfer rate fell dramatically from +5.4% in 1975 to -11.0% in 1985

The study challenges claims that rising "social wage burdens" caused the economic crisis, arguing instead for a classical Marxian understanding of falling profit rates driven by rising organic composition of capital.

## INTRODUCTION (pp. 184-185)

### Purpose and Scope

This article deals with the relation between postwar fiscal policy and the standard of living of workers. The aim is to analyze the growth of the so-called welfare state and its impact on the working class in the United States.

### Critique of Conventional Methodology

**Two Major Problems with Conventional Studies:**

1. **Income Classification Problem**: Conventional studies classify people according to the amount of income they receive, grouping those whose income derives from labor together with those who derive income from ownership of property. This obscures the distinction between workers and non-workers.

2. **Treatment of Government Spending**: All government spending is treated as a pure benefit. Within such a framework, the very notion of social benefit loses meaning because a great expansion of military spending (as during the Vietnam War) is treated as essentially equivalent to an expansion in social welfare spending. The methodology obscures the social costs and benefits of state intervention.

### Evolution of Radical Literature

**First Position (Therborn 1984)**:
- Focus: Social welfare expenditures only, without regard to taxes paid by workers
- Conclusion: In advanced capitalist countries, between 1/5 and 1/3 of all household income derives from public revenue
- Problem: This conclusion is only valid if the taxes paid out of household income were equal to social welfare expenditures. If taxes equal benefits, the state would have merely intervened to siphon off income in money-form (taxes) and inject it back as commodities and payments (social welfare expenditures). Rising social expenditures would not in itself imply a rising burden for the system.

**Second Position (Shaikh 1978; Tonak 1984, 1987; Bowles and Gintis 1982a)**:
- Focus: Both social welfare expenditures and taxes
- Debate: Direction and size of net transfer between workers and the state

### Earlier Findings (Shaikh 1978; Tonak 1984, 1987)

Over the postwar period in the United States:
- There has been a **net transfer from workers to the state**
- The so-called social wage is actually a **social subsidy of the state**

### Bowles and Gintis Position (1982a)

Claim: Net transfer goes the other way - from state to workers
- Over the postwar period, the state induced a "substantial redistribution from capital to labor"
- The resulting "citizen wage" has grown so rapidly that by the 1970s it plays "a critical role in producing and prolonging" an economic crisis

### Methodological Deficiencies in Bowles and Gintis

**Problem 1: Household Size Overestimate**

Bowles and Gintis use official series on gross and spendable income of an "average" production worker with three dependents, treating this as representative of the average working class family. However:
- The published series refers to a **hypothetical family** with four people, only one of whom is assumed to work
- A **stereotypical family** with one male worker, a non-working wife, and two children
- By contrast, the actual average United States household in 1977 contained roughly three people, and 1.2 working members (includes significant proportion of working women)

**Result**: Bowles and Gintis **overestimate the number of people per household by one-third**, correspondingly **overestimate social welfare expenditures** received by average household.

**Problem 2: Tax Underestimate**

Bowles and Gintis **underestimate the average household tax payment by two-thirds**. This occurs for two reasons:

1. They underestimate the number of income earners per household (by implicitly leaving out working women), thereby underestimate federal income taxes paid
2. The published series leaves out all state, local, and other taxes paid

**Problem 3: Mutual Reinforcement**

With the former (benefits) overestimated and the latter (taxes) underestimated, the measure of the social wage becomes greatly inflated. This set of mutually reinforcing errors produces the apparently dramatic rise in their measure of the citizen wage.

### Consequences of Correction

When one corrects for these errors:
- Conclusions are totally reversed
- Instead of a social subsidy of workers by the state, one finds a social subsidy of the state
- Their strained and artificial construction linking social welfare expenditures to the current crisis simply falls to the ground
- A much simpler and more sensible explanation of the course of social welfare expenditures emerges

## THE RADICAL LITERATURE ON THE SOCIAL WAGE (pp. 185-186)

### Two Main Positions

**Position 1: Expenditures Only (e.g., Therborn)**
- Treat social expenditures as a kind of social wage
- Without regard to taxes paid by workers

**Position 2: Expenditures and Taxes (e.g., Bowles and Gintis)**
- Deal with both expenditures and taxes
- Estimate net transfers involved

### Therborn's Position (1984)

**Argument**:
- Rise of welfare state capitalism attended by rapid growth of "politically determined and regulated income flows"
- Ends up concluding that "in advanced capitalist countries today, between one-fifth and one-third of all household income derives from public revenue and not from property or labor" (Therborn 1984:25-26)

**Problem**:
Conclusion that large proportion of household income derives from state expenditures is only valid if these expenditures represent a transfer which is the net of taxes paid. Obviously, if taxes paid out of household income were equal to the sum of social welfare expenditures (including wages of workers employed in administering these expenditures), then no matter how large or rapidly growing the state expenditures, households would experience no net gain at all.

**Analogy**: The state would have merely intervened to siphon off income in money-form (taxes) and then inject it back as a mixture of commodities and payments (social welfare expenditures). Whereas this might alter the distribution of income among households, it would leave the total income unchanged. Thus, a rising level of social expenditures would not in itself imply a rising burden for the system, since it could merely represent a rising amount of income re-circulated via the state.

**Conclusion**: Line of argument represented by Therborn is therefore not adequate for an analysis of socialized consumption and its impact on accumulation and crises, because it improperly identifies the level of social welfare expenditures with that of the social wage.

## EMPIRICAL METHODOLOGY (pp. 186-187)

### Overview

In order to measure the net impact of state expenditures and taxes on the standard of living of the working class, we focus on the **net transfer** in relation to workers' income.

### Definition of Net Transfer

```
Net Transfer (NT) = Benefits and income received by workers from the state
                    - Taxes paid by workers to the state

NT = B - T_w

Where:
B   = Benefits received by workers
T_w = Taxes paid by workers
```

**Key Point**: Because the net transfer is negative over most of this period (1952-1985), it actually represents a **net tax on workers**.

### Adjustment for Cyclical Factors

The estimated net transfer is adjusted for:
- Inflation
- Changes in employment
- Other factors

This is done to capture certain significant trends within the context of cyclical capital accumulation.

### Classification of Government Expenditures

State expenditures are classified into **three major groups**:

#### GROUP I: Direct Benefits to Workers
Items received entirely by workers, either in money or commodity form:

**Included:**
- Labor Training and Services
- Housing and Community Services
- Income Support
- Social Security and Welfare (except Military Disability and Military Retirement)

**Note**: Military Disability and Military Retirement are treated as a cost of war and excluded.

#### GROUP II: Social Consumption
Conventional categories treated as social consumption in general, with workers' share estimated by multiplying group total by share of total labor income in personal income:

**Included:**
- Education
- Health and Hospitals
- Recreational and Cultural Activities
- Energy
- Natural Resources
- Transportation
- Postal Services

**Estimation Method**: Workers' share = (Total labor income / Personal income) × Group II total

**Note**: Within Marxian terminology, this is the same as (nominal) variable capital if we ignore the distinction between productive and unproductive labor. When we do treat this distinction, variable capital becomes the total employee compensation of productive labor alone (Tonak 1984:Chap. IV).

#### GROUP III: Reproduction Costs
Two kinds of expenditures:

**Type 1: Expenses of reproducing and maintaining the system itself** (faux frais of Marx 1974:446 of capitalist society):
- Central Executive, Legislative and Judicial Activities
- International Affairs
- Space
- National Defense
- Civilian Safety
- Veteran Benefits
- Agriculture

**Type 2: Expenditures directed mainly toward small businesses and related administrative activities** (interest payments to highest income brackets):
- Economic Development
- Regulation and Services
- Net Interest
- Others and Unallocables

**Treatment**: Both sets are excluded from labor income and consumption.

### Classification of Taxes

Taxes are analyzed by identifying two main groups which flow out of employee compensation:

#### GROUP I: Social Security Contributions
```
Total Employee Compensation = Wages and Benefits + Employer Contributions for Social Insurance
```

**Key Points:**
- Employee Compensation is the total cost incurred by capitalists for the purchase of labor power
- Comprises both wages and benefits, including Employer Contributions for Social Insurance and Other Labor Income
- Two main groups of taxes flow out of this total
- Employee Compensation is the cost to the capitalists for hiring workers
- But the income received by workers is less than this because a certain portion labeled Employee and Employer "Contributions" is deducted for social security

**First Group of Taxes:**
- Portion of employee compensation which goes towards social security taxes
- This is government-administered contributions for Social Insurance and Other Labor Income

#### GROUP II: Personal Taxes
Taxes levied directly on workers' compensation:

**Included:**
- Personal Income Taxes (Federal & State)
- Motor Vehicle Licences
- Property Taxes (primarily on homes)
- Other Taxes and Non-Taxes (very small category including passport fees, fines, etc.)

**Estimation Method**: Since these are levied on both earned and unearned incomes, the portion emanating from labor is estimated by using the share of total labor income in personal income.

### Summary of Net Transfer Calculation

```
Social Welfare Expenditures (to workers):
= Group I (direct benefits)
  + Labor's share of Group II (social consumption)

Where labor's share = (Total labor income / Personal income)

Taxes (from workers):
= Group I (Social Security contributions from employee compensation)
  + Labor's share of Group II (personal taxes)

Net Transfer = Social Welfare Expenditures - Taxes
```

- Ever larger numbers of people slipping into poverty
- The benign welfare state, so long a favorite of social democratic theorists, has long since begun to show its teeth

## EMPIRICAL RESULTS AND THEIR IMPLICATIONS FOR THE CURRENT CRISIS (pp. 189-190)

### Summary of Key Findings

#### Finding 1: Workers are Net Taxpayers

Figure 1 shows that the true wage of workers is almost always below their apparent wage. This means that it is the workers who generally end up transferring a net portion of their wages to the state.

**Implication**: Arguments such as those of Bowles and Gintis, which claim that "there has been a substantial redistribution from capital to labor" over the postwar period, and which trace the current economic crisis back to a supposed increased social wage, appear to be quite ill-founded.

#### Finding 2: State Intervention Increases Exploitation Rate

A second implication of our results is that the intervention of the state has actually served to increase the rate of exploitation of workers (relative to its trend), since their true wage is below their apparent wage (Figure 1).

**Important Qualification**: But this should not be taken to imply that the increased rate of surplus value has thereby raised the rate of profit (relative to its trend).

**Reason**: It does not follow that the net tax paid by workers was transferred over to capitalist enterprises. In all probability, this amount was absorbed by the state itself, in support of various activities ranging from general administration to the military.

**Further Analysis Required**: To analyze this further, one would have to extend our basic approach to encompass transfer to/from capitalist enterprises and the capitalist class itself. This is beyond the scope of the present paper.

#### Finding 3: "Social Wage Burden" Argument is Invalid

The fact that a rising "social wage burden" cannot be blamed for the current crisis leads us back to the question of the causes of the crisis.

**Authors' Position**: Here we would argue in favor of the classical Marxian notion of a **falling rate of profit** in which a rising organic composition of capital lowers the rate of profit despite a generally rising rate of surplus value.

**Reference**: The logical and empirical bases for this argument are spelled out in the paper by Shaikh (1987; in this volume).

**Key Point on State Intervention**: Within such a conception, the basic capitalist dynamic originates in the antagonistic process involving the production of capital itself, and is then modified by more concrete class struggles and by the intervention of the state.

**Limits on State Power**: It is important to realize that the state is not only subject to the general limits of the capitalist mode of production in its various stages, but also to the specific conjunctural limits arising from particular phases of accumulation:

- **When accumulation is healthy and economy is booming**: The intervention of the state is least needed. Yet, paradoxically, this is precisely when the state has the greatest latitude.

- **When system enters a crisis phase**: The capitalist state is at its most constrained even as the need for intervention is the greatest.

### Three Historical Phases

The preceding point of view is nicely borne out by the history of the net transfer rate. This rate will be recalled, is the difference between benefits received and taxes paid expressed as a proportion of the apparent wage.

#### Phase 1 (1952-1969): Normal Accumulation

**Characteristics:**
- Looking at benefit and tax rates in Figure 2, and net transfer rate in Figure 3
- Real taxes are higher than real benefits (Figure 2)
- Net transfer rate negative throughout (-2% to -7%)

**Dynamics:**
- Security afforded by the boom, combined with the strength of labor
- These serve to increase real benefits at a somewhat faster rate than real taxes
- As a result, the net transfer rate becomes less negative
- Finally stabilizing around -3 percent (Figure 3)

**Mark of Crisis Onset**: Widely acknowledged as marking onset of the economic crisis
- Attended by soaring unemployment as unemployment rate in Figure 2 jumps from 3.5 to 8.5

#### Phase 2 (1969-1975): Crisis and Unemployment

**Characteristics:**
- The second phase, from 1969-1975
- Widely acknowledged as marking the onset of the economic crisis
- Attended by soaring unemployment (unemployment rate jumps from 3.5 to 8.5)

**Dynamics:**
- Because unemployment insurance and welfare payments accelerate with this jump in crisis-induced unemployment

**Result:**
- Sharp rise in the net transfer (from -4.2 percent to +5.4 percent, in Figure 3)
- This is therefore a **consequence of the crisis and its attendant unemployment, and not a cause**

#### Phase 3 (1975-1985): State Attack on Labor

**Overall Characterization:**
The last phase from 1975-1985 is the one in which the state responds to the crisis by joining in the attack on labor:
- First through "benign neglect"
- Then through Reagan's direct attack

**Dynamics:**
- During this whole period, tax rates rise more or less steadily
- Movements of the benefit rate dominate the trend of the net transfer rate

**Three Sub-periods:**

**Sub-period 1: Ford-Carter Years (1975-1980)**
- Unemployment recedes somewhat from previous high in 1975
- Benefit rates fall (Figure 2)
- Then reflected in the net transfer rate (Figure 3)

**Sub-period 2: First Reagan Term (1980-1983)**
- Unemployment shoots up to an all time high

**Sub-period 3: Reagan Cutbacks Accumulate (1983-1985)**
- As Reagan cutbacks accumulate, benefit rates drop sharply from 1983-1985
- In the face of only moderate decline in unemployment rates

**Critical Finding for 1985:**
- It is striking that by 1985 the social benefit rate is at a level **below that of 1966**
- While the tax rate continued to climb to an all time high (41 percent higher than in 1966, in Figure 3)
- The net transfer rate therefore falls dramatically from +5.4 percent in 1975 to **-11.0 percent in 1985** (an all time low)

All of this takes place in the context of:
- True real wages per worker falling back to the levels of 1970
- Unemployment rates hovering at historically high levels
- Ever larger numbers of people slipping into poverty
- **The benign welfare state, so long a favorite of social democratic theorists, has long since has begun to show its teeth**

## NOTES (pp. 193-194)

### Note 1: Scope and Terminology

Our aim here is to determine the net impact of state redistributive activities on workers' living standards. We therefore focus on wages and net transfers. But neither the wage nor the adjusted wage ("social wage") should be taken to represent the overall standard of living of workers, since neither includes the critical component of production within the household. In a similar vein, we use the term "welfare state" as a convenient and widely used label for the redistributive activities of the state. This does not mean that we accept the notion that the capitalist state can be basically class neutral or structured around the welfare of the working class.

### Note 2: Bowles and Gintis Household Size Error

Bowles and Gintis even fail to notice that in order for the average worker to have three dependents the population would have to be 60-70 percent larger than it actually is! For instance, in 1977 there were 90.5 million employed workers, which implies a population of 362 million if each worker is assumed to have three dependents. But the actual population was only 217 million. Their implicit population estimate was therefore **67 percent larger** than the actual figure.

### Note 3: Social Welfare Expenditure Overestimate

Thus, in calculating the average family share of social welfare expenditures, they multiply the per capita social expenditure level by 4 (the hypothetical family size) as opposed to 3 (the actual household size): an **overestimate of 33 percent** in social expenditure per household.

### Note 4: Tax Underestimate by Bowles and Gintis

For 1977, Bowles and Gintis estimate a direct tax per worker of $10 per week, in 1967 dollars. Since they assume one working member per household, this is also their estimate of the direct tax per household (Bowles and Gintis 1982a:73, Table 3, Col.2).

On the other hand, Tonak (1984) shows that a more systematic accounting of direct taxes paid yields an estimate of **$22.96 per worker** (Tonak 1984:128, Chap. V, Table 5, Col.5), times 1.22 workers per household (Tonak 1984:126, Chap. V, Table 5, Col.5), for an overall estimate of **$30.42** in direct taxes paid per household, per week.

The Bowles and Gintis estimate is therefore **two-thirds lower** than Tonak's.

### Note 5: BLS Spendable Earnings Data

The BLS spendable earnings data are calculated by deducting only Federal income and social security taxes from gross earnings; all other taxes at the level of State and Local governments are left out.

### Note 6: Labor Share Method

For rationale and procedures of "labor share method," see Tonak (1984: Chap. IV; 1987).

### Note 7: Variable Capital in Marxian Terminology

Within Marxian terminology this is the same as (nominal) variable capital if we ignore the distinction between productive and unproductive labor. When we do treat this distinction, variable capital becomes the total employee compensation of productive labor alone (Tonak 1984:Chap. IV).

### Note 8: Taxes from Employee Compensation

We are interested here in tracing the taxes which flow directly out of employee compensation. This is quite distinct from the time honored question of tax shifting in which one attempts to estimate what workers' income *might have been* if some taxes (such as sales taxes) had been different.

Our framework is similar to that of Bowles and Gintis (1982a). On the other hand, it is quite different from that of Miller (1986). First of all, his allocation of taxes between capital and labor is rooted in the tax shifting approach. Secondly, his treatment of expenditures is rooted in the debate around productive and unproductive expenditure rather than the present around the social wage.

### Note 9: Government Lotteries and Parimutuels

An additional very small category was added here. It consists of net government receipts from lotteries, etc., which we treat as a kind of direct tax. It is actually listed as a net expenditure in government accounts, but since it is consistently negative we treat it as a positive net tax (Tonak 1987).

### Note 10: Further Details on Methodology

For further details, see Tonak (1984:Chap. IV; 1987).

### Note 11: Taxes Excluded from Analysis

We leave out the group of taxes comprising:
- Corporate Profit Taxes
- Indirect Business Taxes
- Estate and Gift Taxes (which only apply to the highest income levels)

Because they are not levied on workers.

### Note 12: Public Assistance Treatment

The preceeding concept of net transfer includes public assistance (i.e. welfare payments) on the benefit side. This is appropriate if we are interested in the issue of the overall effect of state taxes and social expenditures on the standard of living of workers, since public assistance is a benefit to the working class as a whole.

But if we are interested in the rate of exploitation of labor, we are concerned with employed workers alone. Public assistance is then not an appropriate item on the benefit side, since (unlike social security or unemployment insurance) it is not based upon the present or past employment of the individuals who receive it.

For this reason, we leave out public assistance payments when adjusting the apparent rate of exploitation for the effects of state taxes and expenditures (Tonak 1984:Chap. IV).

### Note 13: Data Sources

A more detailed description of sources and methods is available in Tonak (1984:Chap. IV, Appendix I, II; 1987).

### Note 14: True Wage of Employed Workers Even Lower

In calculating the effect on the rate of exploitation, we must leave out public assistance from the benefit side (see note 12). This means that the true wage of *employed* workers is even lower than that shown in Figure 2.

## KEY FORMULAS AND CONCEPTS

### Net Transfer

```
NT = B - T_w

Where:
NT  = Net transfer
B   = Benefits received by workers from the state
T_w = Taxes paid by workers to the state
```

**Interpretation:**
- NT > 0: Net transfer from state to workers
- NT < 0: Net transfer from workers to state (net tax on workers)

### Net Transfer Rate

```
Net Transfer Rate = NT / AW

Where:
AW = Apparent wage (employee compensation)
```

**Time Series:**
- 1952-1969: Ranges from -2% to -7%, stabilizes around -3%
- 1971: +1% (ONLY positive year in 1952-1971 period)
- 1975: +5.4% (peak due to crisis-induced unemployment benefits)
- 1985: -11.0% (all-time low)

### True Wage

```
True Wage = Apparent Wage + Net Transfer
True Wage = AW + NT

Where:
AW = Employee compensation (apparent wage)
NT = Net transfer
```

**Key Finding**: True wage is below apparent wage for most of the postwar period, meaning workers are net taxpayers to the state.

### Benefit Rate

```
Benefit Rate = B / AW

Where:
B  = Benefits received by workers
AW = Apparent wage (employee compensation)
```

**Time Series:**
- 1952: ~11%
- 1966: Base level
- 1975: ~33% (peak)
- 1985: Below 1966 level (~20%)

### Tax Rate

```
Tax Rate = T_w / AW

Where:
T_w = Taxes paid by workers
AW  = Apparent wage (employee compensation)
```

**Time Series:**
- 1952: ~17%
- 1966: Base level (~22%)
- 1984: ~31% (41% higher than 1966)

### Labor Share

```
Labor Share = Total labor income / Personal income

Used to allocate Group II expenditures and taxes
Example (1964): 0.73
```

### Adjusted Exploitation Rate

**Reference**: Tonak 1984:Chap. IV

**Method**: Adjust apparent rate of exploitation for effects of state taxes and expenditures

**Important Note**: When calculating effect on rate of exploitation, public assistance must be excluded from benefit side (unlike when calculating overall standard of living effect).

## CRITIQUE OF BOWLES AND GINTIS METHODOLOGY

### Three Major Errors

#### Error 1: Household Size Overestimate (33%)

**Bowles and Gintis:**
- Use official series for "average production worker with three dependents"
- Hypothetical family: 4 people (1 worker + non-working wife + 2 children)
- Stereotypical family with one male worker

**Reality (1977):**
- Actual average U.S. household: 3 people
- 1.2 working members (includes working women)

**Mathematical Error:**
- Implied population: 362 million (90.5 million workers × 4)
- Actual population: 217 million
- Their implicit population estimate was **67% larger** than actual

**Impact on Social Expenditure Estimate:**
- They multiply per capita expenditure by 4 instead of 3
- **Overestimate: 33%**

#### Error 2: Tax Underestimate (Two-Thirds)

**Two Reasons:**

1. **Underestimate income earners per household:**
   - Implicitly leave out working women
   - Underestimate federal income taxes paid

2. **Omit state and local taxes:**
   - Published series (BLS spendable earnings) only deducts Federal income and social security taxes
   - Leaves out all state, local, and other taxes paid

**Comparison (1977, per household per week, in 1967 dollars):**
- Bowles and Gintis estimate: $10
- Tonak (1984) estimate: $30.42 ($22.96 per worker × 1.22 workers/household)
- **Underestimate: Two-thirds (66%)**

#### Error 3: Mutual Reinforcement

**Result**: With benefits overestimated (by 33%) and taxes underestimated (by 66%), the measure of the social wage becomes **greatly inflated**.

**Consequences:**
- Produces apparently dramatic rise in "citizen wage"
- Leads to incorrect conclusion of "substantial redistribution from capital to labor"
- Incorrectly links rising social wage to current crisis

### When Errors are Corrected

**Result**: Conclusions are **totally reversed**
- Instead of social subsidy of workers by state
- Find social subsidy of the state by workers
- Strained construction linking social welfare expenditures to current crisis falls to the ground
- Emerges possibility of much simpler and more sensible explanation

## THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

### Marxian Analysis of Capitalist Reproduction

**Key Principle**: The framework is rooted in a Marxian analysis of capitalist reproduction.

### Relation to Crisis Theory

**Alternative to "Wage-Induced Profit Squeeze":**

Bowles and Gintis (1982a:53) argue for a "wage-induced profit squeeze" where:
- Large social wage burden borne by capital
- Reduction in income available for profits
- This causes the crisis

**Authors' Counter-Argument:**

The hypothesis that there exists a large social wage whose burden is borne by capital through a reduction in income available for profits is:
- A version of "wage-induced profit squeeze" argument
- Our analysis falls within purview of Marxist literature on crises
- But demonstrates that the empirical basis for this version is flawed

### Classical Marxian Alternative: Falling Rate of Profit

**Preferred Framework**: Classical Marxian notion of falling rate of profit

**Key Features:**

1. **Rising organic composition of capital** lowers the rate of profit
2. Despite **generally rising rate of surplus value**
3. Basic capitalist dynamic originates in the antagonistic process involving the **production of capital itself**
4. Then modified by:
   - More concrete class struggles
   - Intervention of the state

**Reference**: Logical and empirical bases spelled out in Shaikh (1987; in this volume)

### State and Accumulation

**State Not Autonomous:**

The state is subject to:
1. **General limits** of capitalist mode of production in its various stages
2. **Specific conjunctural limits** arising from particular phases of accumulation

**Paradox of State Intervention:**

**When accumulation is healthy and economy is booming:**
- Intervention of state is **least needed**
- Paradoxically, this is when state has **greatest latitude**

**When system enters a crisis phase:**
- Capitalist state is at its **most constrained**
- Even as the need for intervention is the **greatest**

**Evidence**: This paradoxical relationship is "nicely borne out by the history of the net transfer rate"

### Faux Frais of Capitalist Society

**Concept**: Expenses of reproducing and maintaining the system itself

**Reference**: Marx (1974:446) on "faux frais" of capitalist society

**Examples in this study**:
- Central Executive, Legislative and Judicial Activities
- International Affairs
- Space
- National Defense
- Civilian Safety
- Veteran Benefits
- Agriculture

**Treatment**: Excluded from labor income and consumption because they are costs of maintaining the capitalist system itself, not benefits to workers.

## SUMMARY OF MAIN ARGUMENTS

### Central Thesis

The concept of "social wage" or "citizen wage" is a **myth**. Empirical analysis of U.S. data from 1952-1985 reveals that:

1. Workers generally make a **net transfer TO the state**, not from it
2. The so-called "social wage" is actually a **"social subsidy of the state"** by workers
3. State fiscal intervention has **increased the rate of exploitation** of workers (by reducing their true wage below their apparent wage)
4. Rising "social wage burdens" **cannot be blamed** for the economic crisis

### Three Major Contributions

#### 1. Methodological Innovation

**Net Transfer Concept**: Operational measure that properly accounts for both:
- Benefits and income received by workers from the state
- Taxes paid by workers to the state

**Proper Classification**: Distinguishes:
- Workers from non-workers (class-based analysis)
- Different types of government expenditures (benefits vs. system maintenance vs. business support)
- Different types of taxes (those levied on workers vs. others)

#### 2. Empirical Correction

**Critique of Bowles and Gintis**: Demonstrates that their methodology contains:
- 33% overestimate of household size and thus social expenditures
- 66% underestimate of taxes paid by workers
- These mutually reinforcing errors produce artificially inflated "citizen wage"

**Corrected Results**: When properly calculated:
- Net transfer is negative for 32 of 34 years (1952-1985)
- Only 1971 shows meaningful positive net transfer (+1%)
- 1975 positive transfer (+5.4%) is a **consequence of crisis-induced unemployment**, not a cause
- By 1985, net transfer hits all-time low of -11.0%

#### 3. Theoretical Reorientation

**Away from "Wage-Induced Profit Squeeze"**: Rising social wage burdens did not cause the crisis

**Toward Classical Marxian Crisis Theory**:
- Falling rate of profit driven by rising organic composition of capital
- Despite generally rising rate of surplus value
- Basic capitalist dynamic originates in production of capital itself
- Modified by class struggles and state intervention
- State subject to conjunctural limits of accumulation phases

### Policy Implications

#### Myth of the Benign Welfare State

**Social Democratic Position Challenged**: The notion that the welfare state represents a fundamental shift in favor of workers is empirically unfounded.

**Reality**: "The benign welfare state, so long a favorite of social democratic theorists, has long since has begun to show its teeth."

#### Three Historical Phases

**Phase 1 (1952-1969): Normal Accumulation**
- State has greatest latitude but intervention least needed
- Net transfer moderately negative (-3%)
- Benefits rise somewhat faster than taxes due to boom conditions and labor strength

**Phase 2 (1969-1975): Crisis Onset**
- Unemployment soars from 3.5% to 8.5%
- Benefits rise sharply (unemployment insurance, welfare)
- Net transfer becomes positive (+5.4% in 1975)
- This is a **consequence** of crisis, not a cause

**Phase 3 (1975-1985): State Attack on Labor**
- State increasingly constrained as crisis deepens
- Responds by attacking labor ("benign neglect" then Reagan's direct attack)
- Benefit rate falls below 1966 levels
- Tax rate reaches all-time high (41% above 1966)
- Net transfer plummets to -11.0% (all-time low)
- True real wages fall back to 1970 levels
- Poverty increases dramatically

#### Paradox of State Power

**During Boom**: State has greatest capacity to help workers but least pressure to do so

**During Crisis**: Greatest need for state intervention coincides with state being most constrained and most aligned with capital against labor

### Strengths of the Study

1. **Class-Based Framework**: Properly distinguishes workers from non-workers
2. **Comprehensive Tax Accounting**: Includes federal, state, local, and other taxes
3. **Careful Expenditure Classification**: Distinguishes benefits to workers from system maintenance costs

### Limitations and Scope

1. **Focus on Workers as a Whole**: Does not distinguish between employed and unemployed workers for standard of living analysis (though distinction made for exploitation rate calculation)

2. **Production vs. Household**: Does not include production within household (acknowledged in Note 1)

3. **Limited to Direct Transfers**: Does not extend to transfers between workers and capitalist enterprises or capitalist class (acknowledged as beyond scope)

4. **Tax Incidence vs. Tax Shifting**: Focuses on taxes that flow directly out of employee compensation, not on what workers' income "might have been" under different tax structures

5. **Excludes Corporate and Indirect Taxes**: Does not allocate corporate profit taxes, indirect business taxes, or estate/gift taxes to workers (appropriate given framework)

## TECHNICAL DETAILS

### Labor Share Calculation

```
Labor Share = Total labor income / Personal income

Used to allocate:
- Group II expenditures to workers
- Group II taxes from workers

Example (1964): 0.73
```

### Group Classifications

**Expenditures:**
- Group I: 100% to workers (direct benefits)
- Group II: Labor share × total (social consumption)
- Group III: 0% to workers (system reproduction, business support)

**Taxes:**
- Group I: 100% from workers (social security from employee compensation)
- Group II: Labor share × total (personal taxes)
- Excluded: Corporate taxes, indirect business taxes, estate/gift taxes

### Adjustments and Refinements

**Transportation (Group II Expenditure)**:
- Also adjusted by "Gas Share of Passenger Cars"
- Reference: Tonak 1984:Chap. IV; Appendix II

**Property Taxes**:
- Consider only part paid by home-owners
- Further adjusted by labor share
- Reference: Tonak 1984:Chap. IV; Appendix I

**Government Lotteries and Parimutuels**:
- Listed as expenditure in government accounts
- Consistently negative (net government receipts)
- Treated as positive net tax (direct tax)
- Very small category

### Employee Compensation Structure

```
Total Employee Compensation =
    Wages and Benefits
    + Employer Contributions for Social Insurance
    + Other Labor Income
```

**Key Insight**: Total Employee Compensation is the cost to capitalists for hiring workers. But income received by workers is less than this because "Employee and Employer Contributions" are deducted for social security.

### Real Wage Calculation

**Apparent Real Wage per Worker**:
```
Apparent Real Wage = (Nominal Employee Compensation / Employment) / Price Index
```

**True Real Wage per Worker**:
```
True Real Wage = [(Nominal Employee Compensation + Net Transfer) / Employment] / Price Index
```

### Rates as Proportions

**Benefit Rate**:
```
Benefit Rate = Benefits to Workers / Total Employee Compensation
```

**Tax Rate**:
```
Tax Rate = Taxes from Workers / Total Employee Compensation
```

**Net Transfer Rate**:
```
Net Transfer Rate = (Benefits - Taxes) / Total Employee Compensation
                  = Net Transfer / Total Employee Compensation
```

## FINAL ASSESSMENT

### Contribution to Literature

This article makes three major contributions:

1. **Methodological**: Provides clear, replicable operational concept of net transfer that properly accounts for both expenditures and taxes at class level

2. **Empirical**: Corrects significant errors in Bowles and Gintis methodology and provides comprehensive 34-year time series showing workers are net taxpayers to state

3. **Theoretical**: Refutes "wage-induced profit squeeze" arguments based on rising social wage, supporting classical Marxian crisis theory based on rising organic composition of capital

### Implications for Theory

**Crisis Theory**: Economic crises cannot be blamed on rising social wage burdens. Instead, classical Marxian framework of falling profit rates driven by rising organic composition of capital provides better explanation.

**State Theory**: State intervention is fundamentally constrained by phases of capital accumulation:
- Paradoxically has most latitude when boom makes intervention least needed
- Most constrained when crisis makes intervention most needed
- Responds to crisis by attacking labor to restore conditions for accumulation

**Class Analysis**: Proper class-based accounting reveals that fiscal policy actually **increases** exploitation of workers rather than redistributing from capital to labor.

### Implications for Politics

**Social Democracy**: The social democratic belief in progressive redistribution through welfare state is empirically unfounded. Workers actually subsidize the state, not vice versa.

**Working Class Strategy**: Cannot rely on state as mechanism for improving living standards. True wage of workers has been systematically reduced through fiscal policy.

**Crisis Response**: Historical pattern shows that state responds to crisis by attacking workers (reducing benefits, increasing taxes), not by protecting them. This accelerated dramatically under Reagan.

### Relevance Beyond 1987

While this study ends in 1985, its framework and findings remain relevant for:
- Analyzing subsequent periods (Clinton welfare reform, Bush tax cuts, Obama era, Trump/Biden)
- Understanding relationship between austerity and accumulation crises
- Evaluating claims about "social spending" and "tax burdens"
- Developing class-based fiscal analysis

The methodological framework can be updated and applied to contemporary data to track evolution of net transfers in subsequent decades.
