\begin{abstract}
We replicate and extend every empirical claim in Shaikh and Tonak's \textit{Measuring the Wealth of Nations} (1994) using a fully automated pipeline. The replication covers 33 book series across Chapters 2, 4--9, extended from 1948--1989 to 1948--2024 using BEA, BLS, and FRED public data. We additionally replicate 8 related studies spanning the US, Turkey, and New Zealand. Key findings: the rate of exploitation rises from 1.70 (1948) to 2.44 (1989) to approximately 7.3 (2024). The net social wage, negative for the entire book period, turns positive after 2005 due to post-GFC fiscal expansion. The Marxian profit rate shows a secular decline through the mid-1990s followed by a partial recovery. Labor values and prices of production remain highly correlated ($R^2 = 0.85$--$0.99$ at NAICS benchmarks). All 59 series pass 15 automated validators with 0 failures.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Shaikh and Tonak (1994) reconstruct US national accounts from a Marxian perspective. Their central contribution is distinguishing \textit{productive} labor---which creates surplus value through the production of use values---from \textit{unproductive} labor, which is socially necessary but does not produce surplus. This distinction yields measures of the economy (the rate of exploitation, the Marxian profit rate, the net social wage) that differ fundamentally from orthodox national accounting.

The book's main empirical claims for the postwar period (1948--1989) are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The rate of exploitation ($e = S^*/V^*$) rose from 1.70 to 2.44, a 43\% increase
\item The productive labor share ($L_p/L$) fell from 57\% to 36\%
\item The net social wage was negative in almost every year---workers subsidize the state
\item The Marxian profit rate ($r^* = S^*/K^*$) showed a secular decline
\item Labor values predict market prices with $R^2 > 0.90$ at most IO benchmarks
\end{enumerate}

This project replicates every table and figure, extends all extendable series through 2024, and asks: do these patterns continue?

\section{From Theory to Data: Industry Classification}

\subsection{The Theoretical Foundation}

The core of the Shaikh-Tonak framework is the distinction between \textit{productive} and \textit{unproductive} labor. In Marxian theory, productive labor creates surplus value through the production or transformation of use values---material objects of social use. As the book states: ``Production involves the creation or transformation of the useful properties of material objects of social use (use values).''

Three categories emerge:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Productive labor}: Creates surplus value by producing commodities. A factory worker, a farmer, a nurse, a construction worker---their labor produces tangible use values.
\item \textbf{Trading labor}: Circulates commodities without creating new value. Wholesale and retail workers transfer goods from seller to buyer. Their wages are paid from surplus value, not from value they create.
\item \textbf{Unproductive labor}: Neither produces nor circulates commodities. Bank employees, lawyers, advertising executives, government bureaucrats---all socially necessary, but their wages are a deduction from surplus value.
\end{itemize}

The key insight: a bank employee, a lawyer, and a factory worker all earn wages, but only the factory worker's labor creates surplus value. The exploitation rate $e = S^*/V^*$ measures surplus relative to \textit{productive} wages only. If we counted all wages in the denominator, we would get the conventional profit share---not the Marxian exploitation rate.

\subsection{The Marxian Accounting Framework}

\begin{align}
TP^* &= \text{Gross output of productive + trading sectors} \\
C^*_m &= \text{Intermediate inputs of productive sectors} \\
GFP^* &= TP^* - C^*_m \quad \text{(Marxian value added)} \\
V^* &= \text{Compensation of productive workers} \\
S^* &= GFP^* - D_p - V^* \quad \text{(surplus value)} \\
e &= S^*/V^* \quad \text{(rate of exploitation)} \\
r^* &= S^*/K^* \quad \text{(Marxian profit rate)}
\end{align}

\subsection{Borderline Cases}

Three reclassifications account for most of the disagreement between Shaikh-Tonak and Mohun:

\textbf{Eating and drinking places} (NAICS 722, 8.4\% of employment): Shaikh and Tonak classify restaurants as part of retail trade---the circulation of commodities. Mohun argues that restaurants \textit{transform} raw food into prepared meals, which constitutes material production. This single industry accounts for roughly 60\% of the ST/Mohun exploitation rate divergence, because its 8.4\% employment share moves between the productive and unproductive categories.

\textbf{Information sector} (NAICS 51, 2.1\% of employment): This sector did not exist in the SIC classification. We follow the book's logic by splitting it: motion pictures (512) and broadcasting/telecom (517) produce material information commodities (productive), while publishing/software (511) and data processing (514) are intermediary services (unproductive). Mohun classifies the entire Information sector as productive. The judgment depends on whether software is a ``material object of social use'' or an intermediary tool.

\textbf{Professional services} (NAICS 54, 5.4\% of employment): Legal, accounting, consulting, architectural, and engineering services. All unproductive in both classifications---these are services \textit{to} capital, not production of use values. But this sector has nearly doubled its employment share since 1989 (from 2.8\%), which is a major driver of the rising exploitation rate: more workers in unproductive sectors means a smaller $V^*$ denominator.

\subsection{Manufacturing: The Core of Productive Employment}

Manufacturing is the largest and most unambiguous productive sector---every manufacturing industry produces material commodities. Its decline is the single biggest driver of the falling productive labor share:

\begin{itemize}
\item 1948: Manufacturing = 33\% of total employment (17.3M of 52.3M)
\item 1989: 18.6\% (21.4M of 115.0M)
\item 2024: 9.2\% ($\sim$12.5M of $\sim$135.5M)
\end{itemize}

This 24-percentage-point decline was partially offset by growth in productive services---especially health care (3.5\% $\rightarrow$ 12.8\%), education (1.8\% $\rightarrow$ 2.4\%), and accommodation/food (1.3\% $\rightarrow$ 1.5\%). But the net effect is still a large shift toward unproductive employment: finance (3.1\% $\rightarrow$ 3.8\%), professional services (2.8\% $\rightarrow$ 5.4\%), and business services (5.2\% $\rightarrow$ 7.8\%).

\subsection{The SIC--NAICS Transition}

In 1997, the US replaced the Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) with the North American Industry Classification System (NAICS). This created a classification break:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SIC era (1947--1997)}: 75 productive + 7 unproductive sectors in the 85-sector IO tables
\item \textbf{NAICS era (1997--present)}: 48 productive + 12 unproductive in our 71-sector IO mapping
\end{itemize}

New sectors emerged (Information, NAICS 51), existing sectors were reorganized (eating/drinking moved from retail to accommodation), and the level of detail changed. Our bridge uses log-linear interpolation for 1990--1997 between the book's 1989 endpoint and the first NAICS year (1997/1998). This is documented as decision DEC-007 and assumption ASM-M-002.

\section{Extension Methodology}

\subsection{General Approach}

Series are extended from the book's endpoint (1989) through 2024 using growth-rate splicing:
\[
T_{\text{ext}}(t) = T_{\text{book}}(1989) \times \frac{\text{Source}_{\text{ext}}(t)}{\text{Source}_{\text{ext}}(\text{first year})}
\]
This preserves the book's published values while applying the dynamics of modern data.

For formula-based series (ratios, rates), we extend the \textit{components} separately and recompute the formula, rather than splicing the ratio directly. This follows the Anu Data Framework's ``no lazy splices'' rule: if the book computed $e = S^*/V^*$, the extension computes $S^*$ and $V^*$ separately and divides, rather than splicing $e$ directly.

\subsection{Key Series: How Each Is Extended}

\textbf{T511 ($L_p/L$, productive labor share)}: The book computes this from the IO sector classification of 85 SIC industries. For the extension, we use NIPA Table 6.5D (FTE by industry), classifying each of the $\sim$20 aggregate industry lines as productive, trading, or unproductive using the mapping in Table 5. Because the NIPA aggregate lines give a higher $L_p/L$ than the book's detailed IO classification (0.52 vs 0.36 at 1989), we use a growth-rate splice to preserve the book's level while applying the NIPA trend. Result: $L_p/L$ stabilizes at $\sim$0.35--0.36 post-1989.

\textbf{T512 ($V^*/W$, productive wage share)}: The book computes this from sector-level compensation data. For the extension, we use NIPA Table 6.2D (compensation by industry), summing compensation across productive industries to get $V^*$ and dividing by total compensation $W$ from NIPA Table 2.1. Again, a growth-rate splice preserves the book's 1989 level (0.36) while using the NIPA trend. Result: $V^*/W$ declines gently to $\sim$0.33 by 2024.

\textbf{T506 ($e = S^*/V^*$, exploitation rate)}: We compute $S^*$ and $V^*$ from their components. $V^*$ is derived from $W \times (V^*/W)$ (using the T512 extension). $S^* = GFP^* - D_p - V^*$, where $GFP^*$ is extended using IO-classified value added from BEA GDP-by-Industry data. The exploitation rate then follows: $e = S^*/V^*$. The steep rise (2.44 $\rightarrow$ 7.3) reflects $S^*$ growing with nominal GDP while $V^*$ is constrained by the shrinking productive sector.

\textbf{T513 ($r^* = S^*/K^*$, profit rate)}: The book uses pre-computed values from Table 5.11. The extension computes $S^*/K^*$ directly, where $K^*$ = productive sector capital stock from BEA Fixed Assets by industry (Table FAAt401), classified using the same productive/unproductive mapping. A growth-rate splice at 1989 preserves the book's level ($\sim$1.86) while applying the extension trend. Result: $r^*$ declines through the early 1990s, then recovers to $\sim$2.8 by 2024.

\textbf{T607 (NSW, net social wage)}: $NSW = B_w + G_w - T_w$, where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $T_w$ = taxes allocated to workers using the income-proportional method: $T_w = T_{\text{total}} \times W/PI$ (worker wages as a share of personal income)
\item $B_w$ = government benefits to workers, following Appendix N's three-group classification:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Group 1 (100\% to workers): Income security transfers (NIPA Table 3.16, line 36)
  \item Group 2 (allocated by labor share): Education, health, transportation expenditures
  \item Group 3 (excluded): National defense, public order (``faux frais of capitalist society'')
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
The post-2005 reversal (NSW turns positive) is driven by benefits growing 8.5$\times$ (\$521B $\rightarrow$ \$4,456B) while taxes grew only 3.9$\times$ (\$1,117B $\rightarrow$ \$4,338B). Key fiscal events: ARRA 2009, ACA Medicaid expansion 2014, COVID stimulus 2020--2021.

\section{Results and Analysis}

\subsection{Rate of Exploitation ($e = S^*/V^*$)}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../Figures/Fig_5_5_exploitation_rate.png}
\caption{Rate of exploitation $e = S^*/V^*$, 1948--2024. Solid: book period, dotted: extension. (Book Figure 5.3)}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Shaikh \& Tonak's argument}: The exploitation rate measures how much surplus labor is extracted relative to productive workers' compensation. Its secular rise reflects the ongoing structural transformation of the US economy: as productive employment declines relative to total employment, a smaller wage bill to productive workers supports an ever-larger total output.

\textbf{What the extension shows}: The exploitation rate continues to rise sharply, from 2.44 (1989) to approximately 7.3 (2024). The acceleration is steeper than the book period---the rate roughly triples in 35 years, compared to a 43\% increase over the prior 41 years.

\textbf{Why it looks this way}: The acceleration is driven by two forces. First, $V^*$ (productive worker compensation) grows slowly because the productive share of total employment ($L_p/L$) continues to decline as manufacturing shrinks and finance, tech, and professional services expand. Second, $S^* = GFP^* - V^*$ grows rapidly because total value added grows with nominal GDP while $V^*$ is constrained by the shrinking productive sector. The widening gap between GFP* growth and V* growth produces the steep rise in $e$.

\textbf{Limitations}: The extension uses the book's $VA^*/W \approx 1.238$ constant for deriving $V^*$ from total compensation. If this ratio has shifted post-1989 (plausible given sectoral transformation), the exploitation rate could be overstated. The WP-1 annual IO classification framework provides annually-varying compensation data that may moderate this acceleration in future iterations.

\subsection{Productive Labor and Wage Shares}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../Figures/Fig_5_6_labor_wage_shares.png}
\caption{Productive labor share and wage share, 1948--2024. Solid: book period, dotted: extension. (Book Figure 5.2)}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Shaikh \& Tonak's argument}: The productive labor share measures the fraction of total employment engaged in surplus-value-producing activities. Its decline reflects the structural transformation from a manufacturing-based to a services-based economy.

\textbf{What the extension shows}: Both shares stabilize post-1989. $L_p/L$ fluctuates around 0.35--0.36, and $V^*/W$ around 0.33--0.35. The dramatic decline of the book period (0.57 to 0.36 for $L_p/L$) gives way to a plateau.

\textbf{Why it looks this way}: Manufacturing employment, the largest productive sector, reached a floor around 1990 at roughly 17 million workers. While it continued to decline in absolute terms, this was partially offset by growth in productive services (healthcare, education, transportation). The net effect is a roughly stable productive share.

\subsection{Net Social Wage}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../Figures/Fig_6_1_net_social_wage.png}
\caption{Net social wage, 1952--2025. Solid: book period, dotted: extension. (Book Figure 6.1)}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Shaikh \& Tonak's argument}: The net social wage measures whether workers receive more in government benefits ($B_w$) and services ($G_w$) than they pay in taxes ($T_w$). Their finding that NSW is negative for almost every year demolishes the notion that the welfare state redistributes income from capital to labor. Instead, the state is a mechanism for transferring value from workers to capital.

\textbf{What the extension shows}: The NSW remains negative through the late 1990s (reaching -\$261B in 2000), then turns positive after 2005, reaching extraordinary levels during the 2008--2009 financial crisis (\$958B in 2010) and the COVID pandemic (\$1,812B in 2020).

\textbf{Why it looks this way}: Three fiscal regime changes drive this reversal:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Post-GFC expansion (2008--2012)}: The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (ARRA), extended unemployment benefits, and Medicaid expansion transferred hundreds of billions to workers.
\item \textbf{ACA implementation (2014+)}: Medicaid expansion and premium subsidies under the Affordable Care Act permanently raised benefits to low-income workers.
\item \textbf{COVID fiscal response (2020--2021)}: Direct stimulus payments, enhanced unemployment, and expanded child tax credits produced the largest fiscal transfers in US history.
\end{enumerate}

This represents a genuine departure from the book's finding. Whether it represents a structural change in the state's redistributive function or a temporary crisis response remains an open question. The sharp decline from \$1,812B (2020) to \$1,235B (2024) as COVID programs expire suggests partial reversion.

\subsection{Marxian Profit Rate}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../Figures/Fig_5_7_profit_rates.png}
\caption{Marxian profit rates, 1948--2024. Each series: solid = book, dotted = extension. (Book Figure 5.5)}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Shaikh \& Tonak's argument}: Marx's law of the tendential fall in the rate of profit predicts that $r^*$ declines secularly as the organic composition of capital ($C^*/V^*$) rises---production becomes more capital-intensive. The book confirms this empirically for 1948--1989.

\textbf{What the extension shows}: The profit rate ($r^* = S^*/K^*$) declines through the early 1990s, then recovers through the late 1990s dot-com boom. After a dip during the 2001 and 2008 recessions, $r^*$ rises through the 2010s, reaching 2.8 by 2024---above the 1989 level.

\textbf{Why it looks this way}: The extension uses a growth-rate splice at 1989 to preserve the book's published values while applying the dynamics of the modern $S^*/K^*$ ratio. The post-1990 rise in $S^*/K^*$ reflects surplus value growing faster than the productive capital stock, driven by the same exploitation dynamics that push $e$ upward. Whether this constitutes a ``recovery'' of the profit rate or a measurement artifact of the extension methodology is an important question for future research.

\textbf{Note on $r^*$ vs $r^*_{\text{adj}}$}: The capacity-adjusted rate ($r^*_{\text{adj}} = r^*/TCU$) adjusts for capacity utilization---when factories run below capacity, the effective profit rate is lower than the measured rate. In the book period, $r^*_{\text{adj}} > r^*$ because $TCU < 1$. In the extension period, the $r^*_{\text{adj}}$ series uses pre-computed values from a different source, which may explain the different dynamics.

The systematic divergence between our exploitation rate (T506) and Mohun's (N1401) is not a computational error---it reflects different productive/unproductive classification boundaries:

Mohun (2005) uses a broader productive sector: he classifies the entire Information sector and retail eating/drinking establishments as productive, while Shaikh and Tonak classify them as unproductive (circulation activities). A broader productive sector means more workers counted as productive ($V^*$ larger, $S^*$ smaller), yielding a lower exploitation rate. The ratio rises from 1.49 to 1.80 over the period, suggesting the classification boundary matters more as the disputed sectors (Information, professional services) grow relative to the economy.

\subsection{International: Turkey and New Zealand}

\textbf{Turkey}: Karabacak \& Tonak (2022) find that the net social wage is negative for every single year from 1980 to 2019---workers in Turkey are net subsidizers of the state in all observed years. This contrasts sharply with our US extension, where NSW turns positive after 2005. The difference reflects the much larger US welfare state (especially post-ACA/post-COVID) compared to Turkey's more limited fiscal transfers.

Turkish labor share (compensation/GDP) ranges from 0.19 to 0.29, substantially lower than the US ($\approx$ 0.55--0.60 in NIPA terms), reflecting Turkey's more capital-intensive industrial structure and lower formal-sector wage share.

\textbf{New Zealand}: Cronin (2001) finds that the productive capital share ($K^*/K$) in New Zealand ranges from 0.31 to 0.37, similar to the US productive capital ratio ($\approx$ 0.57 in our framework, though definitions differ). The post-reform (post-1984 Rogernomics) period shows a shift in the productive/unproductive boundary as New Zealand deregulated and privatized state-owned enterprises.

\section{Reproducibility}

The entire dataset is reproducible from source inputs:
\begin{verbatim}
git clone https://github.com/andenick/measuring-the-wealth-of-nations.git
cd measuring-the-wealth-of-nations/Technical/NickyData
pip install -r requirements.txt
python run.py --validate-only   # 2 seconds, verifies cached data
python run.py --test-all        # 15 seconds, re-fetches and recomputes
\end{verbatim}

The pipeline produces 59 series, 53 structured CSVs, 53 Excel workbooks, and 11 publication figures. Data format follows the \href{https://github.com/andenick/anu-data-framework}{Anu Data Framework} standard. Full per-series documentation is available in the repository's \texttt{docs/series/} directory.
