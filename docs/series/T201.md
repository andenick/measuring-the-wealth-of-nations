## From the Book

> GFP (Gross Final Product) as used by Shaikh and Tonak (ST) is an alternative to orthodox GDP/GNP that rests on a classical production/nonproduction distinction. Under the ST framework, activities in trading, military, police, and administration are classified as nonproduction (social consumption) and are therefore excluded from the measure of net product. This contrasts with orthodox GDP/GNP, where all potentially marketable activities — including wholesale/retail, financial services, legal services, advertising, and government administration — count as final output. Figure 1.1 (book page 16) presents seven comparative GFP estimates for the mid-1960s: NT (Nordhaus-Tobin), Z (Zolotas), JF (Jorgenson-Fraumeni), K (Kendrick), R (Ruggles-Ruggles), E (Eisner), and ST (Shaikh-Tonak), all expressed relative to the official BEA measure of GNP. All seven estimates exceed official GNP: they range from 112% (Ruggles-Ruggles) to 468% (Jorgenson-Fraumeni). The ST estimate is approximately 121% of GNP on a pure market-production basis, rising to roughly 180% of GNP when Eisner's imputation for housework is added to make coverage more comparable with other extended accounts. The large spread across estimates reflects fundamentally different definitions of what counts as production: the JF result is inflated primarily by leisure-time valuation and human-capital investment imputations.

> The ST production boundary distinguishes outcomes from outputs. Production activity uses up wealth to create new wealth (a production outcome). Nonproduction activities — including distribution (trading, wholesale/retail, advertising), social maintenance (military, police, administration), and personal consumption — use up wealth in pursuit of nonproduction outcomes. The distinction is not between goods and services: most service activities (transportation, lodging, entertainment, repairs) are classified as production; many services (wholesale/retail, financial services, legal services, advertising, civil service) are nonproduction. Under capitalism, 'productive labor' has a second condition: it must be capitalistically employed (wage labor exchanged against capital). Both conditions are required — capitalistically employed distribution or maintenance labor remains unproductive of capital.

> Alternative GFP measures from the six orthodox extended accounts all share certain critical characteristics: (1) they are extensions, not alternatives, to conventional accounts; (2) their theoretical foundation is neoclassical economics; (3) they expand production by adding imputed 'services' of consumer and government durable goods; (4) most add value-of-leisure estimates (ranging from 48.7% to 115.5% of official GNP); and (5) several shift police, fire, defense, and guard activities from final product to intermediate input (NT, Z, E), rather than excluding them from production entirely as ST does. The ST approach differs: it excludes nonproduction activities from both total and net product, not just from net product.
